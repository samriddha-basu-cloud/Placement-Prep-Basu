## 1. Anchor Sheet and the Five-Part Answer Template
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ structure, assumptions, calculation, sanity check, follow-up; the anchors every consumer estimate reuses

### Definition
A consumer guesstimate is graded on structure and judgement, not on hitting a "true" number. Use the same five parts every time:

1. **Structure**: draw the tree in one line (e.g. households × share buying × units per year × price).
2. **Assumptions**: say each input aloud with a reason; round to numbers that multiply easily.
3. **Calculation**: keep units visible (per day, per year, ₹, crore, lakh crore).
4. **Sanity check**: a second route (supply side, per-capita spend, company data) or a plausibility test (share of GDP, share of household budget).
5. **Follow-up**: one decision the number supports (pricing, store count, capacity).

Reusable anchors from [[103 Key India Data Points to Memorize]] (rounded; population 1.4-1.46 billion, about 300 million households of 4.5 persons, 35% urban):

| Anchor | Value used in this note |
|---|---|
| Households | 300M (urban 105M, rural 195M) |
| Population | 1.46B (UNFPA 2025); 24% aged 0-14, 68% aged 15-64 |
| One age cohort | about 22M people |
| Internet / smartphone users | about 886M / about 700M |
| Cross-check unit | 1 lakh crore = $10^{12}$ rupees |

For method background see [[102 Guesstimate Framework]], [[104 Classic Guesstimate Types & Templates]] and the error list in [[107 Common Mistakes & How to Avoid]]. Counting problems follow $N=\dfrac{\text{demand}}{\text{capacity per unit}}$; market problems follow $\text{Market}=\text{buyers}\times\text{units}\times\text{price}$.

### Example
**G1. How many kirana stores are there in India?**
- **Structure**: total spend through neighbourhood stores ÷ average sales per store.
- **Assumptions**: spend at kirana-type outlets (groceries, daily needs): urban ₹5,000 a household a month, rural ₹3,000 (assumption). Average store sales ₹5,000 a day (a blend of ₹2,000-a-day village shops and ₹20,000-a-day city shops).
- **Calculation**: urban 105M × ₹5,000 = ₹5.25 × 10¹¹ (₹52,500 crore) a month; rural 195M × ₹3,000 = ₹5.85 × 10¹¹; total ₹1.11 × 10¹² a month = **₹13.3 lakh crore a year**. Store sales ₹5,000 × 365 = ₹18.25 lakh a year. Stores = 13.3 × 10¹² ÷ 18.25 × 10⁵ ≈ **7.3 million**.
- **Sanity check**: AICPDF quotes about 13 million (news box), so the estimate is 0.56× of the published figure. The gap is explainable: the 13 million counts every small shop including paan, tea and general stalls with sales well below ₹5,000 a day. Per-store sales of ₹3,000 a day would give about 12 million.
- **Follow-up**: how many stores does one distributor serve? 13M ÷ 450,000 distributors ≈ **29 stores per distributor** (the news-box numbers).

### In the news
See news box. AICPDF's 13 million and NIQ's 6% urban e-commerce share show that kiranas still carry the bulk of FMCG volume, which is why this estimate is the base for route-to-market cases in [[130 FMCG & Retail Distribution - India Route-to-Market]].

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Estimate the number of kirana stores in India" or "How would you size the general-trade channel for a snack brand?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Demand-side tree with urban/rural split, then a second route (households per store)
> - Honest comparison with a published count and a reason for any gap
> - Clean units: ₹ crore and lakh crore without slips
> - A follow-up that links the number to distribution cost or coverage

## 2. Neighbourhood Retail and Personal Services
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ footfall × ticket × margin; haircut as a frequency × price market

### Definition
Local businesses are sized from **bills a day × average bill**, then converted to profit with a margin and a fixed-cost stack:

$$\text{Daily revenue}=\text{bills per day}\times\text{average bill}$$

For services bought on a cycle (haircuts, repairs) use **people × (days in year ÷ cycle length) × price**, and then adjust for share served by informal or self-service.

### Example
**G2. Daily revenue and profit of a Mumbai kirana.**
- **Structure**: catchment households × share loyal × visits a day × average bill.
- **Assumptions**: 500 households within a 3-minute walk; 50% buy here regularly; each loyal household visits on 0.6 of days; average bill ₹150; gross margin 12%; rent ₹25,000 a month, one helper at ₹500 a day, other costs ₹150 a day.
- **Calculation**: bills = 500 × 0.5 × 0.6 = 150 a day; revenue = 150 × ₹150 = **₹22,500 a day** (₹82 lakh a year). Gross profit = 12% × 22,500 = ₹2,700; less rent ₹833, helper ₹500 and other ₹150 = **₹1,217 a day, about ₹4.4 lakh a year** for the owner.
- **Sanity check**: ₹22,500 is 4.5× the national ₹5,000 average in G1, plausible for a dense Mumbai location; ₹4.4 lakh a year is roughly a junior office salary, which fits the typical owner-operator.
- **Follow-up**: what happens if a quick-commerce dark store takes 20% of the bills? Revenue falls to ₹18,000 and gross profit to ₹2,160, so profit drops to ₹677 a day (-44%), because fixed costs do not shrink.

**G3. Size of the men's haircut market.**
- **Structure**: men and boys × cuts a year × share paid for × price.
- **Assumptions**: 700M males; one cut every 35 days; 80% pay a barber (the rest cut at home); blended price ₹90 (rural ₹40-60, city ₹150-400).
- **Calculation**: cuts = 700M × 0.8 × 365/35 = **5.84 billion a year = 16 million a day**; market = 5.84B × ₹90 = **₹52,560 crore (about ₹0.5 lakh crore)**. Barbers needed at 15 cuts a day each: 16M ÷ 15 ≈ **1.07 million**; at two chairs a shop, about 0.53 million shops.
- **Sanity check**: ₹52,560 crore ÷ 1.46B people = ₹360 per capita a year, about ₹30 a month, plausible for a service people use every five weeks.
- **Follow-up**: premium salons at ₹400 serve 5% of cuts: 0.05 × 5.84B × ₹400 = ₹11,680 crore, which is 22% of revenue from 5% of volume; that is the pricing-tier story for a chain.

### In the news
See news box. NIQ's channel data suggests the neighbourhood store is under pressure mainly for baskets that quick commerce can serve in minutes (an analytical reading); haircuts cannot be delivered, so service spend stays local.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How much does a typical kirana earn?" or "Estimate the haircut market in India."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Bills × ticket for stores; frequency × price for services
> - A fixed-cost stack to move from revenue to profit
> - Sensitivity: show how a 20% revenue loss hits profit more than 20%
> - Tier mix (premium vs mass) when asked about a chain

## 3. Street Food and Eating Out
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ demand-side people × frequency and supply-side outlets × throughput must agree

### Definition
Food-service questions are the cleanest place to run **two routes**: (a) demand: city population × share eating the item on a day × units, and (b) supply: outlets × units per outlet. The two should land within about 30% of each other. Adjust for **who can buy** (income, diet) and for **peak vs average** day. Consumption-per-capita questions (tea, milk) can also use **kilograms ÷ grams per serving**.

### Example
**G4. Dosas sold in Bengaluru in a day.**
- **Structure**: people × share eating dosa out on a day × dosas each; check with outlets × throughput.
- **Assumptions**: 1.4 crore people; 15% eat a dosa outside the home on a given day; 1.5 dosas each; price ₹60 average (₹30 at a cart, ₹100+ at a restaurant); 20,000 outlets serving dosa (assumption).
- **Calculation**: 14M × 0.15 = 2.1M eaters; × 1.5 = **3.15 million dosas a day**; value 3.15M × ₹60 = **₹18.9 crore a day (₹6,900 crore a year)**.
- **Sanity check**: supply route: 20,000 outlets × 150 dosas = 3.0M, within 5%.
- **Follow-up**: unit economics of one stall: 150 × ₹60 = ₹9,000 a day; ingredients 30% = ₹2,700; staff ₹1,800; rent and gas ₹1,500; **profit ₹3,000 a day**. Breakeven covers: fixed ₹3,300 ÷ ₹42 contribution per dosa (70% of ₹60) = **79 dosas a day**.

**G5. Cups of chai a day in India.**
- **Structure**: tea leaf consumed ÷ leaf per cup; cross-check with drinkers × cups.
- **Assumptions**: per-capita tea consumption about 840 g a year (figure reported in the Drishti IAS summary of India's tea industry; the Indian Tea Association page says about 750 g, so say "0.75-0.85 kg"); 3 g of leaf a cup (strong milk tea).
- **Calculation**: 1.4B × 0.84 kg = **1.18 billion kg a year**; cups = 1.18 × 10⁹ ÷ 0.003 = 3.9 × 10¹¹ a year = **1.07 billion cups a day**.
- **Sanity check** (demand route): adults 15+ ≈ 68% × 1.4B = 952M; 65% drink tea; 2.2 cups a day: 952M × 0.65 × 2.2 = **1.36 billion**. The two routes differ by 27%; quote "1.1-1.4 billion cups a day".
- **Follow-up**: if a quarter of cups are bought out at ₹10, revenue = 1.07B × 0.25 × ₹10 = ₹267 crore a day, about **₹1 lakh crore a year** (₹97,600 crore); the chai-stall count at 150 cups a day is 1.07B × 0.25 ÷ 150 ≈ **1.8 million stalls**.

**G6. Vada pav sold in Mumbai in a day.**
- **Structure**: demand route 2 crore people × share × units; supply route vendors × sales.
- **Assumptions**: 20% eat vada pav on a given day, 1.5 each; 20,000 vendors selling 200 a day (assumption).
- **Calculation**: 20M × 0.2 × 1.5 = **6.0 million**; vendors: 20,000 × 200 = **4.0 million**; at ₹20, revenue ₹8-12 crore a day.
- **Sanity check**: the two routes differ by 1.5×; commuter-heavy stations (not in the demand base) push the true number up, so say "4-6 million".
- **Follow-up**: what is the vendor pool worth? 20,000 vendors × ₹4,000 a day × 365 = **₹2,920 crore a year**, which is why a QR-code and working-capital offer to these vendors is a plausible fintech wedge.

### In the news
See news box. NIQ shows e-commerce at only 6% of urban FMCG, and street food is bought at the counter; the UPI average ticket of about ₹1,300 in [[103 Key India Data Points to Memorize]] is far above a ₹10 chai or a ₹20 vada pav, so value-based UPI data under-represents these small tickets (analytical reading).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How many dosas are sold in Bengaluru each day?" or "How many cups of chai does India drink?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Two independent routes that agree within a band
> - Price tiers (cart, darshini, restaurant) before taking an average
> - Cart-level unit economics and breakeven as the follow-up
> - Peak vs average day and city commuter inflows

## 4. Dairy and Eggs
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ milk per capita; Amul butter via branded-buyer funnel; milk-equivalent check

### Definition
Dairy sizing uses **per-capita consumption** and a **milk-equivalent** check: a litre of milk is the common unit, and processed products are converted back (about **20 litres of milk for 1 kg of butter** is a standard rough ratio; fat content varies). A brand's volume must fit inside its **procurement capacity**; Amul procures about 300 lakh litres a day (news box). Eggs follow a simple per-capita rule: share of people who eat eggs × eggs a week ÷ 7.

### Example
**G7. Milk consumed per day in India.**
- **Structure**: population × litres per person per day (all milk, including milk used for tea, curd and sweets).
- **Assumptions**: 0.3 L a person a day (about 300 g, a modest figure given 1.4B people include infants and non-consumers).
- **Calculation**: 1.4B × 0.3 L = **420 million litres a day**; households route: 300M households × 1.0 L = 300M L a day (a lower bound for fluid milk only).
- **Sanity check**: Amul's 30M litres a day is 7% of 420M, a plausible leading brand share when most milk is still sold loose. Government statistics put annual milk production at roughly 24 crore tonnes (recalled; check the latest DAHD figure), which is about 650 million litres a day, so 420M is the share that is consumed as drinking milk and tea milk while the rest becomes ghee, butter, curd and so on.
- **Follow-up**: value at ₹55 a litre: 420M × ₹55 = ₹2,310 crore a day, about **₹8.4 lakh crore a year**.

**G8. Amul butter sold in a day.**
- **Structure**: urban households × share buying branded butter × kg a month → tonnes a year → Amul's share → packs.
- **Assumptions**: 105M urban households; 20% buy branded butter regularly; 0.4 kg a month; Amul holds 60% of that segment (assumption); shelf price ₹60 per 100 g (assumption, check the shelf).
- **Calculation**: buyers = 105M × 0.2 = 21M households; consumption = 21M × 0.4 kg × 12 = 100,800 tonnes a year (276 tonnes a day); Amul = 60% = **60,480 tonnes a year = 166 tonnes a day**; in 100 g packs that is **1.66 million packs a day**; revenue = 60,480 t × ₹600 per kg = **₹3,629 crore a year**.
- **Sanity check**: milk needed = 166 t × 20 L per kg = 3.3M litres a day, **11% of Amul's 30M litres** procured, plausible alongside milk, curd, ghee and powder. Butter revenue = 5.5% of GCMMF's ₹65,911 crore, also plausible.
- **Follow-up**: if the price rises 10% and volume falls 4%, revenue changes by 1.10 × 0.96 − 1 = **+5.6%**; the elasticity here is −0.4.

**G9. Eggs eaten per day.**
- **Structure**: people eating eggs × eggs a week ÷ 7.
- **Assumptions**: 60% of 1.46B eat eggs; 3 eggs a week.
- **Calculation**: 876M × 3 ÷ 7 = **375 million eggs a day = 137 billion a year**, or 94 eggs per person per year.
- **Sanity check**: national production is reported in the region of 138-142 billion eggs a year (recalled from DAHD; re-check), so the estimate lands within 3%.
- **Follow-up**: at ₹6 an egg, 375M × ₹6 = **₹225 crore a day, ₹82,125 crore a year**; a 10% price rise needs only ₹0.6 an egg, so ask what share of eggs is bought loose from small retailers.

### In the news
See news box. Amul's ₹65,911 crore turnover, 300 lakh litres a day and 36 lakh farmers give the supply-side cap for any dairy estimate; the January 2025 cut in prices of larger packs, as reported by [Dairy News Today](https://dairynews.today/news/revenue_of_amul_s_parent_firm_gcmmf_rises_by_11_to_rs_65_911_crore_in_fy25_7432391.html), shows Amul treats household volume as price-sensitive.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Estimate how much Amul butter is sold every day" or "How many eggs does India eat?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - A funnel (urban households → branded buyers → brand share) rather than a flat per-capita number
> - A supply-side cap using milk-equivalents and procurement
> - Packaging mix (100 g vs 500 g) as the converter to packs
> - Elasticity or channel-mix follow-up

## 5. Packaged Food, Ice Cream and Water
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ households × packs × price; ice cream value vs volume; bottled water per capita

### Definition
For impulse and snack categories use **households × share buying × packs a week × price**. For beverages and frozen desserts, split **volume** (litres) from **value** (₹) and check the implied **price per litre**: market-research reports mix definitions (retail vs manufacturer value, litres vs units), so an implied price outside the shelf range exposes the problem. Seasonality matters for ice cream and cold drinks; use annual numbers unless told otherwise.

### Example
**G10. Biscuit packets sold per day (with a failed first pass).**
- **Structure**: households × share buying × packs a week × price.
- **Assumptions (first pass)**: 70% of 300M households buy; 1.5 packs a week; average ₹12.
- **Calculation**: 300M × 0.7 × 1.5 ÷ 7 = **45 million packs a day**; revenue 45M × ₹12 = ₹54 crore a day = **₹19,700 crore a year**.
- **Sanity check**: published industry estimates run roughly double this (recalled; re-check against Britannia and Parle disclosures), so the first pass is low. The likely errors: too few packs (two a week is more realistic) and price (₹15 with larger packs).
- **Revised**: 80% × 2 packs a week at ₹15: 300M × 0.8 × 2 ÷ 7 = **68.6 million packs a day**; revenue **₹103 crore a day, ₹37,500 crore a year**. Say aloud why you revised: this is the habit interviewers reward.

**G11. Bottled water bottles per day.**
- **Structure**: per-capita packaged water × population ÷ bottle size.
- **Assumptions**: 16.5 L per person per year (the IMARC report cites 15-18 L); average pack 0.8 L (mix of 1 L bottles, 500 ml, jars counted by litre).
- **Calculation**: 1.46B × 16.5 L = **23.1 billion litres a year = 63 million litres a day = 79 million bottles a day**.
- **Sanity check** (demand route): 500M urban × 15% buying one bottle a day = 75M. Value check: IMARC puts the market at US$10.71 billion (2025), which at an assumed ₹85/$ is ₹91,000 crore, implying ₹39 per litre across 23.1B litres. That is well above a ₹10-20 shelf price per litre, so one of the two published figures is likely defined differently; treat vendor market sizes with care.
- **Follow-up**: at an ex-factory ₹6 per litre, volume of 23.1B L is ₹13,860 crore; the retail-vs-factory gap is the distributor and retailer margin story.

**G12. Ice cream market in India.**
- **Structure**: consumers × servings a year × realised price per serving.
- **Assumptions**: 40% of 1.46B eat ice cream in a year; 18 servings each; ₹30 realised per serving (mix of ₹10 sticks and ₹100+ tubs).
- **Calculation**: 584M × 18 = 10.5 billion servings; value = 10.5B × ₹30 = **₹31,500 crore**.
- **Sanity check**: IMARC estimates the market at ₹243.50 billion (₹24,350 crore) in 2025, so the estimate is 1.3× the published number. The same report cites per-capita consumption of about 1.6 litres (2023): 1.6 L × 1.46B = 2.3B litres, which at ₹24,350 crore implies about ₹104 per litre, too low for packaged ice cream, so the report's volume and value do not reconcile either.
- **Follow-up**: cold-chain intensity: a freezer cabinet sells 40 servings a day in season × 120 days; 10.5B servings ÷ (40 × 120) = about 2.2 million freezers' worth of seasonal capacity, which is why distribution of cabinets, not demand, limits growth.

### In the news
See news box. NIQ notes about 60% of the FMCG portfolio had GST rate revisions after 22 September 2025 (the GST structure moved to 5%, 18% and 40% slabs, per the Wikipedia summary of the reform), a reminder to state whether price assumptions are before or after the tax change; see also [[227 GST & Indirect Tax for Supply Chains]].

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How many packets of biscuits does India buy a day?" or "Estimate the ice cream market."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - A first pass, a sanity check that exposes an error, and a visible revision
> - Volume, value and implied price per unit reconciled
> - Retail vs ex-factory value stated
> - Seasonality and cold-chain constraints for frozen goods (see [[133 Food, Agri & Perishables Supply Chain - India]])

## 6. Personal Care and Hygiene
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ use-up rate per household; sachet economics; target-group funnels

### Definition
Personal-care goods are sized with a **use-up rate**: grams or units a person consumes a day, then convert to packs. **Sachet economics**: low-income buyers purchase small ₹1-5 units per use, so unit volumes are high while value is modest. For gendered products, start with the relevant age band (e.g. women aged 15-49 ≈ 49% × 52% × 1.46B).

### Example
**G13. Toothpaste tubes sold per day.**
- **Structure**: households using paste × days a tube lasts.
- **Assumptions**: 70% of 300M households use toothpaste; 2.25 users per household (half of 4.5 persons use paste); 1 g per person per day; 100 g tube lasting 100 ÷ 2.25 = 44 days; ₹60 per 100 g.
- **Calculation**: users: 210M households; tubes a year = 210M × 365 ÷ 44.4 = **1.72 billion = 4.7 million a day**; revenue = 1.72B × ₹60 = **₹10,350 crore a year (₹28 crore a day)**.
- **Sanity check**: per household ₹60 × 8.2 tubes = ₹493 a year (₹41 a month), plausible; the figure is of the same order as the combined revenues of leading oral-care firms (check annual reports; not re-verified).
- **Follow-up**: a 5% share gain equals 0.05 × ₹10,350 crore = ₹518 crore, which sets how much an acquisition or ad budget could be worth.

**G14. Shampoo sachets sold per day.**
- **Structure**: shampoo users × share using sachets × sachets a month ÷ 30.
- **Assumptions**: 40% of 1.46B use shampoo (584M); half buy sachets; 8 sachets a month; ₹2 each.
- **Calculation**: 584M × 0.5 × 8 ÷ 30 = **77.9 million a day**; revenue = **₹15.6 crore a day, ₹5,684 crore a year**.
- **Sanity check**: per user 8 sachets × 12 months × ₹2 = ₹192 a year, so 292M users × ₹192 = ₹5,606 crore, within 1.5% of the ₹5,684 crore above (the small gap is 365 days vs 360).
- **Follow-up**: moving 10% of sachet users to a ₹110 bottle that lasts 30 days adds 29.2M × ₹110 × 12 = ₹3,854 crore, or about ₹3,290 crore net of the ₹561 crore of sachet spend they stop, showing why premiumisation matters more than unit growth.

**G15. Sanitary pads used per day.**
- **Structure**: women aged 15-49 × share using pads × pads a month.
- **Assumptions**: women 15-49 = 1.46B × 49% × 52% = 372M; 40% use commercial pads; 8 pads a cycle.
- **Calculation**: 148.8M users × 8 ÷ 30 = **39.7 million pads a day**.
- **Sanity check**: 39.7M ÷ 148.8M users = 0.27 a day, consistent with one pad every 3-4 days averaged over the month.
- **Follow-up**: raising usage from 40% to 60% adds 0.2 × 372M = 74.4M users, or 74.4M × 8 ÷ 30 = **19.8 million more pads a day**, the volume case for a rural distribution push.

### In the news
See news box. NIQ's finding that rural volumes grew 2.9% against 2.3% urban in Oct-Dec 2025 supports a rural-first view of these low-price, high-frequency categories.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How many toothpaste tubes are sold in India daily?" or "Size the market for sanitary napkins."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Use-up rate converted to packs
> - Target-group funnel (age, gender, usage share) before applying price
> - Sachet vs bottle value mix and premiumisation
> - Rural reach and price points as the growth lever

## 7. Telecom and Devices
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ recharge cycles; ARPU cross-check; smartphone replacement cycle

### Definition
Telecom revenue is $\text{Subscribers}\times\text{ARPU}\times 12$; recharge counts come from **recharge cycle length**: a 28-day plan means 13 recharges a year, an 84-day plan about 4.3, an annual plan 1. Devices follow a **stock ÷ replacement life** rule, adjusted for first-time buyers (growth) and the feature-phone-to-smartphone shift.

### Example
**G16. Mobile recharges a day.**
- **Structure**: active prepaid users × recharges a year per user ÷ 365.
- **Assumptions**: active subscribers 1,207.59 million (TRAI, August 2026: 93.36% of wireless base); 90% prepaid (assumption); mix of plans: 40% on 28-day (13 a year), 30% on 84-day (4.3 a year), 30% on long plans (1 a year).
- **Calculation**: recharges per user = 0.4 × 13 + 0.3 × 4.3 + 0.3 × 1 = **6.79 a year**; prepaid active = 1,207.59M × 0.9 = 1,087M; recharges = 1,087M × 6.79 = **7.38 billion a year = 20.2 million a day**; average recharge = ARPU ₹215.6 × 12 ÷ 6.79 = **₹381**; market = 7.38B × ₹381 = **₹2.8 lakh crore**.
- **Sanity check**: Jio's reported ARPU of ₹215.6 (Q1 FY27) × 1,207.6M × 12 = ₹3.1 lakh crore for all active users including postpaid, within 10% of the recharge route. Compare with UPI: 20.2M recharges is about 3% of the 66 crore daily UPI transactions in [[103 Key India Data Points to Memorize]].
- **Follow-up**: a 10% tariff rise with no churn adds 10% × ₹2.8 lakh crore = ₹28,000 crore of industry revenue; with 1.6% monthly churn (Jio's figure), the break-even question is how many users must stay.

**G17. Smartphones sold per year.**
- **Structure**: smartphone users ÷ average life, plus first-time buyers.
- **Assumptions**: 700M users (vault anchor); replacement every 4.5 years (Indian users keep phones long); ASP ₹21,000 (assumption).
- **Calculation**: 700M ÷ 4.5 = **155.6 million a year**; value = 155.6M × ₹21,000 = **₹3.27 lakh crore**.
- **Sanity check**: market-tracker shipment estimates are generally reported around 140-150 million a year (recalled, re-check IDC or Counterpoint); a replacement-only model is slightly high because some of the 700M are shared handsets, so use 5 years if needed (140M).
- **Follow-up**: GST on phones at 18% means tax of ₹3.27 lakh crore × 18/118 = ₹49,800 crore if all sales are at the stated prices including GST; always ask whether prices include tax.

### In the news
See news box. TRAI's 1,312.25 million wireless subscribers (Aug 2026) and Jio's ARPU of ₹215.6 (Q1 FY27, per [TelecomTalk](https://telecomtalk.info/jio-adds-million-subscribers-q1fy27-arpu-rs215/1009790/)) are the two anchors above; the same page shows Jio's churn at 1.6% a month and its 5G base of 285 million.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How many mobile recharges happen in India each day?" or "Estimate smartphone sales per year."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Plan-length mix to get recharges per user, not a single guess
> - Cross-check with ARPU × subscribers × 12
> - Stock ÷ life for devices, with a growth adjustment
> - Tax and price definitions stated

## 8. Mobility Accessories and Media
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ helmets from two-wheeler stock; newspaper copies from households

### Definition
Accessories follow the installed base: **units in circulation ÷ life** gives annual replacement demand, plus new-vehicle bundling. Media copies are sized from **households × share subscribing**, then compared with an audited count; audited circulation (ABC) covers only member publications, so it is a floor, not the market.

### Example
**G18. Helmets sold per year.**
- **Structure**: two-wheeler stock × helmets per bike ÷ helmet life.
- **Assumptions**: 250M two-wheelers (vault anchor); 0.5 helmets in use per bike on average (many riders and pillions do not own one); life 4 years; price ₹700.
- **Calculation**: helmets in use = 125M; sales = 125M ÷ 4 = **31.3 million a year**; value = 31.3M × ₹700 = **₹2,190 crore**.
- **Sanity check**: SIAM reports 1,96,07,332 two-wheelers sold in FY2024-25, so if each new bike's rider buys one helmet, new-bike demand is 19.6M, or 63% of the estimate; the remaining 11.7M are replacements and pillion helmets, which looks reasonable.
- **Follow-up**: a rule that every new two-wheeler must ship with a helmet (check the current MoRTH position) would move 19.6M units a year from the dealer-accessory channel to the manufacturer; model the dealer margin loss.

**G19. Newspaper copies sold per day.**
- **Structure**: urban and rural households × share buying a paper (one paper a household).
- **Assumptions**: 25% of 105M urban households; 5% of 195M rural households.
- **Calculation**: 26.25M + 9.75M = **36 million copies a day**; at ₹5 cover price, copy sales = 36M × ₹5 × 365 = ₹6,570 crore a year, while advertising is the main revenue source.
- **Sanity check**: ABC certified 2.91 crore (29.1M) copies for H2 2025, so the estimate is 1.24× the audited count, consistent with the audited figure excluding non-members.
- **Follow-up**: a new daily needs 100,000 copies to be viable in a city of 10 lakh households. That is **10% of households**, or 40% of the 25% who buy a paper today (10 ÷ 25), so it must win four of every ten existing buyers or grow the base; that sets the entry choice.

### In the news
See news box. SIAM's FY25 domestic two-wheeler sales of 1,96,07,332 units (9.1% growth) and ABC's 2.91 crore copies are the audited anchors; use them as floors for the two estimates above.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How many helmets are sold in India each year?" or "Estimate newspaper circulation in India."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Installed base ÷ life plus new-vehicle bundling
> - An audited number used as a floor with the reason it is a floor
> - Regulation and enforcement as demand drivers (helmets are compulsory for riders; BIS certification applies)
> - Substitution (digital news) as the long-run trend

## 9. Gold and Weddings
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ weddings from age cohorts; gold in tonnes then ₹; share of GDP check

### Definition
Celebration markets are driven by **events a year × spend per event**. Weddings: marriages a year ≈ (marriage-age cohort × share marrying) ÷ 2, because one wedding involves two people. Gold: size in **tonnes**, then convert with price per 10 g: $\text{Value}=\text{tonnes}\times 10^{5}\times\text{price per 10 g}$ (since 1 tonne = $10^{6}$ g = $10^{5}$ lots of 10 g). Check demand against World Gold Council data and against income plausibility.

### Example
**G20. Weddings in India and total spend.**
- **Structure**: one cohort × share marrying ÷ 2 × average spend.
- **Assumptions**: one age cohort 22M; 90% marry at some point; average spend ₹7 lakh a wedding (skewed: the median is far lower than the mean, assumption).
- **Calculation**: 22M × 0.9 ÷ 2 = **9.9 million, about 1 crore weddings**; spend = 10M × ₹7 lakh = **₹7 lakh crore a year**.
- **Sanity check**: ₹7 lakh crore is about 2.1% of an assumed ₹330 lakh crore GDP (recalled FY25 level; check), and a few percent of household consumption, which is plausible for India's biggest discretionary event.
- **Follow-up**: concentration in auspicious dates (muhurat) sets peak demand for halls, caterers and jewellers; if 40% of weddings fall in 60 days, the daily load is 4M ÷ 60 = 66,667 weddings a day at peak.

**G21. Gold jewellery bought in a year.**
- **Structure**: wedding gold + non-wedding gold, in tonnes; then value.
- **Assumptions**: 10M weddings × 30 g of gold bought for the wedding; 150 tonnes for festivals (Akshaya Tritiya, Dhanteras) and routine purchases.
- **Calculation**: wedding gold = 10M × 30 g = **300 tonnes**; total = **450 tonnes**. Value at ₹1 lakh per 10 g (the 2025 average: Q2 2025 was about ₹95,011 per 10 g per the Deccan Herald WGC report): 450 × 10⁵ × ₹1 lakh = ₹4.5 lakh crore.
- **Sanity check**: WGC reports 430.5 tonnes of jewellery in 2025, so the estimate is within 5%; WGC's spend figure of US$49 billion at about ₹85-88 per dollar is ₹4.2-4.3 lakh crore, within 7% of ₹4.5 lakh crore. In 2024 jewellery demand was 563.4 tonnes (WGC via DT Next), so volume is volatile: price rose 74% in 2025.
- **Follow-up**: a 20% price rise with elasticity of -0.5 cuts tonnes by 10%: 430 × 0.9 = 387 tonnes, but spend rises 1.2 × 0.9 − 1 = **+8%**, the exact pattern WGC reported (volume down, value up).

### In the news
See news box. WGC's 2025 data (430.5 tonnes jewellery, US$49 billion, 74% price rise) is the sourced anchor; 2024 total demand was 802.8 tonnes with jewellery at 563.4 tonnes ([DT Next](https://www.dtnext.in/news/business/gold-demand-in-india-sees-5-pc-rise-at-8028-tonnes-in-2024-2025-projection-at-700-800-tonnes-wgc-821867)).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Estimate gold jewellery demand in India" or "How many weddings happen each year, and how much is spent?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Tonnes first, ₹ second, with the 10⁵ conversion done correctly
> - A split between wedding and non-wedding demand
> - Price elasticity: volume falls while value rises when prices spike
> - Seasonality (muhurat, festivals) and rural vs urban buying

## 10. Quick Commerce and Online Grocery
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ households ordering × frequency ÷ orders per store; channel share from NIQ

### Definition
Dark-store networks are a **capacity** problem: $\text{Stores}=\dfrac{\text{orders per day}}{\text{orders per store per day}}$. Orders per day come from ordering households × orders a month ÷ 30. Channel questions compare the online share (NIQ: 6% of urban FMCG, 18% in the top 8 metros) with kirana share. Express stores per catchment too: a dark store usually serves a radius of 2-3 km. See [[129 E-commerce & Quick-Commerce Fulfilment]] for the operating model.

### Example
**G22. Dark stores needed for Bengaluru.**
- **Structure**: ordering households × orders a month ÷ 30 ÷ orders per store.
- **Assumptions**: 1.4 crore people ÷ 4.2 per household = 3.33M households; 25% order quick commerce; 12 orders a month; a store handles 1,200 orders a day; average order value ₹500.
- **Calculation**: ordering households = 0.83M; orders = 0.83M × 12 ÷ 30 = **333,000 a day**; stores = 333,000 ÷ 1,200 = **about 278**; GMV = 333,000 × ₹500 = ₹16.7 crore a day, **₹6,083 crore a year**.
- **Sanity check**: with about 700 km² of urban area (assumption), 278 stores means one store per 2.5 km², a radius of about 0.9 km, tighter than the 2-3 km norm. If a busy store handles 2,000 orders a day, the need falls to 333,000 ÷ 2,000 = **167 stores** (radius about 1.2 km). State the range **170-280 stores**.
- **Follow-up**: a store with 1,200 orders at ₹500 earns ₹6 lakh a day of GMV; at a 20% take (margin plus fees) of ₹1.2 lakh, less rent, staff and last-mile costs, profitability depends on orders per store, the same lever as in [[160 Case Interview - Cost Reduction, Turnaround & Pricing]].

### In the news
See news box. NIQ: quick commerce is over 75% of e-commerce FMCG sales, and e-commerce is 18% of FMCG in the top 8 metros, so the dark-store count above is a metro-heavy phenomenon.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How many dark stores does a quick-commerce firm need in Bengaluru?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Orders ÷ orders per store with a sensitivity to throughput
> - Cross-check with catchment radius and area
> - Basket value and contribution per order as follow-up
> - Cannibalisation of kirana and modern-trade baskets

## 11. ⭐ Advanced: Triangulation and Reconciling Gaps
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
A strong candidate does not stop at one number. Reconcile three sources: the **bottom-up estimate**, a **top-down check** (market or per-capita), and a **published anchor**. Define the ratio $r=\dfrac{\text{estimate}}{\text{published}}$ and use these rules: $0.7\le r\le 1.4$ is acceptable for guesstimates; between 0.5 and 2 needs a stated reason; outside needs a rebuild. Typical reasons for gaps: definition (retail vs ex-factory), scope (all shops vs grocery shops), unit (litres vs servings), timing (peak vs average), double counting and stale data.

### Example
Summary of this note's reconciliations (all computed above):

| Guesstimate | Estimate | Published or alternative route | Ratio | Reason for gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kirana stores (G1) | 7.3M | 13M (AICPDF) | 0.56 | Definition: all small shops |
| Chai cups a day (G5) | 1.07B | 1.36B (demand route) | 0.79 | Leaf per cup and drinker share |
| Newspaper copies (G19) | 36M | 29.1M (ABC) | 1.24 | ABC covers members only |
| Gold jewellery (G21) | 450 t | 430.5 t (WGC 2025) | 1.05 | None needed |
| Ice cream (G12) | ₹31,500 cr | ₹24,350 cr (IMARC) | 1.29 | Retail vs ex-factory, servings |
| Amul butter milk-equivalent (G8) | 3.3M L | 30M L procured | 0.11 share | Plausible share of procurement |
| Biscuits, first pass (G10) | ₹19,700 cr | about 2× higher (recalled) | about 0.5 | Frequency and price too low |

When two routes disagree by more than a factor of 2, say which one you trust and why, and then report the range. Error compounds multiplicatively: if each of four inputs is off by ±30%, the product's likely band is about $\pm\sqrt{4}\times 30\%=\pm 60\%$ (random errors), and the worst case $1.3^4=2.9\times$ high or $0.7^4=0.24\times$ low.

### In the news
See news box. NIQ's 7.8% value growth against 2.3-2.9% volume growth means a one-year-old market number is already stale in value terms by 5-8%; scale published numbers by growth before comparing.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How confident are you in this number, and how would you check it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Two routes and a published anchor, with the ratio stated
> - Named reasons for gaps (definition, scope, unit, timing)
> - Which assumption moves the answer most (sensitivity)
> - A range, not false precision; see [[107 Common Mistakes & How to Avoid]]

## 12. ⭐ Advanced: From Guesstimate to Business Decision
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
Most consumer guesstimates are openers for a case. Prepare the **bridge**: (1) restate the number with its unit, (2) name what it enables (stores, capacity, revenue pool), (3) convert to profit with price, cost and margin, (4) pick the lever (volume, price, mix, channel). Common bridges: market size → **market entry** ([[027 Case Interview — Market Entry]]); revenue → **profitability** ([[025 Case Interview — Profitability]]); price changes → **pricing** ([[160 Case Interview - Cost Reduction, Turnaround & Pricing]]).

### Example
**Dosa stall (from G4) to a decision: should the owner add a second tawa?**
- Current: 150 dosas a day at ₹60, profit ₹3,000 a day. Queue data (assumption): 25% of walk-ups leave when the wait exceeds 10 minutes; demand without the queue is 200 a day.
- A second tawa adds 50 dosas: contribution = 50 × ₹42 = ₹2,100 a day; cost ₹300 a day for the extra cook's share and gas; net gain **₹1,800 a day = ₹6.6 lakh a year** (365 days: 1,800 × 365 = ₹6,57,000).
- Payback on a ₹40,000 tawa-and-burner set: 40,000 ÷ 1,800 = **22 days**.

**Kirana to dark-store threat (from G2).** Profit is ₹1,217 a day at 150 bills. A 20% bill loss costs ₹677 a day of profit (−44%); the owner's counters are credit (a kirana lends to loyal households), product breadth and delivery by a helper at ₹500 a day: delivery pays only if it adds more than ₹500 a day of gross profit, i.e. above ₹4,167 a day of extra sales at 12% margin (500 ÷ 0.12 = 4,167).

**Amul butter price move (from G8).** At ₹600 per kg and 60,480 tonnes, revenue is ₹3,629 crore; a 10% price rise with elasticity −0.4 lifts revenue 5.6% to ₹3,832 crore (+₹203 crore), but the margin gain depends on cost: if milk-fat cost rises 12%, the net could be zero, so ask for the cost of milk fat.

### In the news
See news box. NIQ's report that small manufacturers outgrew larger ones in volume during the GST transition is a reminder that price changes ripple through the whole channel, not only the brand you are modelling.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Now that you have the market size, what would you recommend to the client?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - A one-line restatement and the decision the number supports
> - A unit-economics step (price, variable cost, fixed cost, breakeven)
> - One lever with a quantified effect and one risk
> - A next-data request (what you would verify first); see [[028 Guesstimates & Market Sizing]] and [[105 Operations & SCM Guesstimates]] for companion practice
