## 1. Why Consulting?
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Problem-solving breadth, exposure to multiple industries, impact at scale

### Definition
"Why consulting?" tests **motivation, understanding of the job and fit**. A credible answer has three parts: **what you like about the work** (structured problem solving, hypothesis-driven analysis, communication), **why now / why you** (evidence from your experiences) and **why consulting rather than a corporate role**.

Typical true drivers (use only those that are genuinely yours): breadth (multiple industries and problem types early in a career), steep learning curve, exposure to senior decision makers, impact at scale (recommendations affecting many people), and working with smart teams.

Show you understand the reality: client-facing work, long hours, project-based travel, up-or-out culture at many firms, and the difference between strategy, operations and technology consulting. For an Ops and SCM student, **operations/supply-chain consulting** is a natural bridge: diagnosing end-to-end flows, sizing savings, designing networks.

Structure: **Pull (what attracts you) + Proof (what you have already done) + Plan (what you will contribute and learn)**.

### Example
Skeleton: "I enjoy breaking an ambiguous problem into parts and testing hypotheses with data. In **[internship/project]** I **[diagnosed X and recommended Y with result]**, which showed me I like the problem-solving cycle more than **[alternative]**. Consulting lets me do that across industries at scale. In operations consulting I can combine my supply-chain knowledge with my data skills."

### In the news
See news box. Consulting's strong share of placements means interviewers hear "variety and learning" from everyone; proof of your own problem-solving is what differentiates.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why consulting?" / "Why not join industry directly?" / "What do you think consultants do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - 2 to 3 genuine reasons, not buzzwords
> - Evidence from your own projects
> - Realistic view of the job's demands
> - Why consulting vs industry
> - A link to the firm's practice area

## 2. Why Product Management?
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Tech background + MBA; user empathy; building things that scale

### Definition
A **product manager** owns the "what and why" of a product: discovering user problems, prioritising, defining requirements, aligning engineering, design and business, and measuring outcomes. Interviewers test whether you understand PM as **ownership without authority** and not as "CEO of the product" or a project coordinator.

Your genuine angle (verify it is yours): a **CSE background** (you can speak to engineers, understand feasibility) plus an **MBA in operations** (you understand cost, process, supply-side constraints), plus **user empathy** from building tools for real users (e.g. dashboards or an IoT product from your tracker).

PM skill checklist: user research, prioritisation (RICE, MoSCoW), metrics (north-star, funnel, retention), trade-offs, communication, execution. Operations-heavy products (logistics, supply chain, B2B, marketplaces, quick commerce) are where an Ops-plus-tech profile is strongest.

Structure: **user problem you enjoy solving + proof you have built something + why PM over engineering or consulting**.

### Example
Skeleton: "On **[project]** I started with the user's pain, **[problem]**, built **[solution]** and measured **[outcome]**. I enjoyed owning the 'why' and trade-offs more than writing code. My operations training lets me build products for supply-side problems such as **[domain]**."

### In the news
See news box. Strong consulting demand also means PM roles are sought after; show product thinking with evidence, not only interest.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why PM and not engineering or consulting?" / "What does a PM do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Accurate PM definition
> - A user-problem story
> - Tech fluency plus business sense
> - Why your Ops angle helps
> - Awareness of PM challenges (influence without authority, ambiguity)

## 3. Why Operations Management?
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Passion for process excellence; CEAT/JBM real-world grounding

### Definition
Operations is where **strategy turns into delivery**: cost, quality, time and service are decided on the floor and in the network. A strong answer shows you like **process and flow**, enjoy **measurable improvement**, are comfortable with people and the shop floor, and see operations as a **career with growth** (plant head, supply-chain head, COO).

Evidence from your own experience (verify): internship at CEAT (identifying a bottleneck and cutting idle time using data) and JBM (turning legacy reporting into MIS dashboards). Link to concepts: Lean, Six Sigma, TOC (bottlenecks), OEE, S&OP, KPIs.

Show awareness of reality: shift work, plant locations, accountability for numbers, handling unions and vendors, and rapid change from automation and analytics. Position yourself as an operations manager who is **data-fluent**, which is a differentiator.

Structure: **trigger + proof + what you love + where you want to go**.

### Example
Skeleton: "At **[plant]** I saw that small flow problems, like **[idle time cause]**, had large cost effects. Solving it with data gave me a measurable result of **[verified metric]**. I like operations because results are visible and measurable, and I want to build a career improving **[process area]**."

### In the news
See news box. Operations roles increasingly ask for analytics skills, which is your stated edge.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why operations?" / "Would you be comfortable on a shop floor?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Real examples from internships
> - Clear understanding of day-to-day work
> - Link to continuous improvement concepts
> - Data and technology as an edge
> - Career growth path

## 4. Why Project Management?
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ CAPM prep; structured delivery; cross-functional coordination

### Definition
A **project manager** delivers a defined outcome within **scope, time, cost and quality**, managing risk, stakeholders and resources. Frameworks: PMI's PMBOK (process groups: initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, closing), Agile/Scrum, critical path method, earned value.

Key formulas for credibility: $CPI = EV/AC$ (cost performance), $SPI = EV/PV$ (schedule performance); values below 1 indicate over-cost or behind-schedule.

Your genuine evidence (verify): preparing for **CAPM** (PMI's Certified Associate in Project Management, tracker says in prep; do not claim you hold it until you do), and delivering a large event end to end (Pravaha) with plan, stakeholders and risks.

Show you understand the role: coordination across functions, planning, risk management, status reporting, and handling scope changes; not just "I am organised".

### Example
Skeleton: "I enjoy turning a goal into a plan and then delivering it with a team. For **[event/project]** I **[planned, assigned owners, managed risks]** and delivered **[result]**. I am preparing for CAPM to formalise this, and I want to build a career managing **[type of programmes]**."

### In the news
See news box. Strong consulting and operations recruiting sits alongside project roles, which value structured delivery.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why project management?" / "What is the difference between project and operations management?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Distinguishes project (temporary, unique) from operations (ongoing, repetitive)
> - Evidence of delivery and planning
> - Honest status on certification
> - Knowledge of scope-time-cost trade-offs
> - Stakeholder and risk management

## 5. Why This Company?
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Research values, culture, recent projects, leadership statements

### Definition
This question tests **preparation and genuine interest**. A generic answer ("great brand, learning") loses points. Build a **three-layer answer**:

1. **Company and industry facts:** business model, scale, recent developments (annual report, results, product launches, news, leadership interviews).
2. **Culture and values:** what they say and what employees and alumni describe.
3. **Personal fit:** how your skills and goals connect to a specific team, project or practice.

Research sources: company website and annual report, investor presentations, recent press, LinkedIn posts by leaders, alumni conversations, Glassdoor-style reviews (with caution). Prepare **one recent fact** and **one question** to ask.

Never criticise competitors; avoid salary or brand as the only reasons.

### Example
Skeleton: "I am drawn to **[company]** because of **[specific fact, e.g. recent project/launch/statement]**, its focus on **[value]**, and the chance to work on **[team/practice]**. My experience in **[proof]** fits **[need]**. I also spoke to **[alumnus/employee]**, who described **[culture insight]**."

### In the news
See news box for market context; for this question you must fetch the company's own latest news (results, announcements) before each interview and quote one verified fact.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why do you want to join us?" / "What do you know about us?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - A recent, specific fact
> - Culture and values link
> - Fit with your skills and goals
> - A thoughtful question back
> - No generic flattery

## 6. Short-Term & Long-Term Goals
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Realistic 3-year and 10-year vision aligned to role

### Definition
Interviewers want **ambition with realism and alignment** with what the role offers (and a signal you will stay and grow).

Framework:
- **First year:** learn the business, deliver reliably, build credibility.
- **3-year goal:** a specific, role-aligned milestone (e.g. lead a workstream or process area, earn a promotion to the next level, build deep expertise in a domain).
- **10-year vision:** a direction rather than a title (e.g. leading operations or supply chain for a business unit, or a product or practice area), showing growth into leadership and impact.

Make it **role-specific**: consulting (engagement manager track), PM (own a product line), operations (plant or supply-chain leadership), project management (programme management). Mention skills and certifications you will build.

Avoid: "I want your job", plans to leave soon (higher studies, startup) or vague "grow with the company".

### Example
Skeleton: "In the first year I want to master **[core skills]** and deliver **[outcome]**. In three years I see myself **[milestone]**. Over ten years I want to **[direction]**, ideally in **[industry/function]**, using **[strengths]**."

### In the news
See news box. With a competitive market, a goal that links to demonstrated strengths sounds credible.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Where do you see yourself in 3/5/10 years?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Role-aligned, realistic milestones
> - Skills you will build
> - Commitment to the company or sector
> - Ambition without arrogance
> - Flexibility

## 7. ⭐ Advanced: Handling "Why not X?" Follow-ups and Role Comparisons
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
When you apply for multiple role types, interviewers ask: "If you want operations, why apply here?" or "Why not consulting?". The answer is **one consistent thread** across roles, not different identities.

Common thread for your profile (verify it fits): **using data to improve how work flows**. Then show each role as a different way to do it:
- Consulting: diagnose and recommend across many clients.
- Product: build tools that change how users work.
- Operations: run and improve the process directly.
- Project: deliver change on time and budget.

Say which you rank first if asked, honestly, and why the other roles are still a fit. Never say "any job is fine".

### Example
"The thread in my work is making operations measurable and faster. In this role at **[company]**, I would do that by **[how]**. I also considered **[other role]**, but I chose this because **[specific reason]**."

### In the news
See news box.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why not consulting/PM instead?" / "Are you applying to other roles?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - A consistent core theme
> - Honest differentiation between roles
> - Clear reason for this role now
> - No disparagement of alternatives

## 8. ⭐ Advanced: Researching a Company in 30 Minutes
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
A fast, repeatable research routine:

1. **Business (10 min):** what they sell, to whom, scale, key segments, competitors; latest annual report highlights.
2. **News (5 min):** last six months: results, launches, partnerships, controversies.
3. **People (5 min):** interviewer's and leaders' LinkedIn, interviews, talks.
4. **Role (5 min):** the job description mapped to your stories.
5. **Questions (5 min):** two insightful questions (e.g. about team priorities, metrics of success).

Keep a one-page company sheet: 3 facts, 2 risks/challenges, 1 opportunity you could contribute to, your top 3 stories. Cross-check numbers from primary sources (company filings, press releases).

### Example
For a logistics firm: note its network size, a recent capacity investment, a competitor move and one challenge (e.g. cost per shipment). Prepare a question: "How does the team measure on-time performance and what is the biggest driver of misses?"

### In the news
See news box.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What do you know about us?" / "Do you have any questions for us?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Verified facts from primary sources
> - A fresh insight, not recitation
> - Questions showing curiosity about the work
> - Links to your skills
