## 1. How to Use Company Cases in Interviews
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Strategy-mechanism-numbers-lesson; fit between product and chain; case matrix

### Definition
A company case works in an interview only if it proves a **principle**. Use the **SMNL** frame: **S**trategy (what the company is optimising), **M**echanism (the operational design that delivers it), **N**umbers (two or three hard figures), **L**esson (the transferable rule), then one line on **where it breaks**. Match the case to Fisher's product-chain logic: functional, predictable products suit efficient chains; innovative, uncertain products suit responsive chains ([[112 Supply Chain Strategy - Fit, Segmentation & Maturity]]).

| Company | Strategy | Mechanism | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Toyota | Efficiency with quality | TPS, kanban, jidoka, keiretsu | Pull and stop-the-line; buffers on gating parts |
| Zara | Responsiveness | Near-home production, small batches, twice-weekly store drops | Speed beats forecast accuracy |
| Dell | Mass customisation | Build-to-order, direct sales | Postponement and negative cash cycle |
| Amazon | Convenience | Dense network, robots, prediction | Cost-to-serve through scale and data |
| Walmart | Low cost | Cross-docking, shared data, supplier scorecards | Logistics as competitive advantage |
| Apple | Premium control | Contract manufacturers, long-term component deals | Control of scarce inputs; diversification |
| Maruti Suzuki | Cost and volume | Supplier parks, JIT sequencing | Locality plus labour stability |
| Amul | Producer-owned scale | Three-tier cooperative, cold chain | Pooling small suppliers |
| Quick commerce | Speed | Dark stores, dense micro-fulfilment | Density economics |
| Tesla | Control | Vertical integration | Make versus buy under uncertainty |
| P&G | Brand scale | Demand-signal sharing with retailers | Bullwhip cure is information |
| IKEA | Low-cost design | Design for logistics, flat-pack | Cost fixed at design |

### Example
Asked "name a company that won through supply chain," an average answer says "Amazon." A strong one says: "Zara. It makes about half its products near home and designs about 12,000 new items a year from 40,000 sketches (S, M, N), so unsold-stock risk is tiny (L); the limit is higher labour cost and capacity for staples, which it outsources to Asia (where it breaks)." The case matches the *product* (fashion, unpredictable) to the *chain* (responsive).

### In the news
See news box. Quick commerce, Tata-Pegatron and Tesla's casting retreat give fresh, India-relevant hooks for the density, diversification and make-vs-buy principles.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Give me an example of a company whose supply chain strategy matches its business strategy."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - SMNL structure and two or three verified numbers
> - Explicit product-chain fit (efficient vs responsive)
> - A limit or failure mode of the model
> - A link to the interviewer's industry

## 2. Toyota: TPS and the 2011 Disaster
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ JIT and jidoka, kanban, heijunka, 78% fall, selective buffers

### Definition
The **Toyota Production System (TPS)** was developed by Taiichi Ohno and Eiji Toyoda between about 1948 and 1975 on the work of founder Kiichiro Toyoda. Its two pillars are **Just-in-Time** (make only what is needed, when needed, in the amount needed) and **jidoka** (automation with a human touch: stop and fix on any defect). Tools: **kanban** (a card or signal that authorises upstream production), **heijunka** (levelled production), **andon** (alert cord) and **kaizen**. Waste (muda) is classed into seven or eight types, with overproduction the worst ([[007 Lean Manufacturing]], [[006 Manufacturing Systems]]).

Supply chain features: close **keiretsu-style supplier relationships**, frequent small deliveries, sequenced supply to the assembly line. The weakness appeared on 11 March 2011, when the Tohoku earthquake damaged a tier-2 chip fab and Toyota's production fell **78% year on year in April 2011**. Toyota then set a business continuity plan with supplier mapping to find at-risk parts early and a policy of **2-6 months of extra inventory** for high-priority components at suppliers ([Supply Chain Dive](https://www.supplychaindive.com/news/toyota-semiconductor-shortage-earthquake-inventory-ihs-gartner-forecast-2022/600193/)). Full disruption detail is in [[141 Supply Chain Disruption Case Library (2011-2026)]].

### Example
Kanban count: demand 240 units/day, container size 20, lead time 0.5 day, safety factor 10%:
$$k = \frac{D \times L \times (1 + s)}{C} = \frac{240 \times 0.5 \times 1.1}{20} = 6.6 \Rightarrow 7 \text{ cards}$$
Reducing lead time to 0.25 day gives $k = 3.3 \Rightarrow 4$ cards, halving the WIP; each card removed forces a problem into view.

### In the news
Toyota cited its 2011 lessons when it said in 2021 that it expected limited impact from the chip shortage (Supply Chain Dive, above); the Kojima cyber stoppage of March 2022 (28 lines, about 13,000 vehicles) showed supplier IT is now a TPS node.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Is lean/JIT still right after COVID? Use Toyota."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - TPS = pull + jidoka, not just low inventory
> - 2011 and 2022 stoppages came from tier-2 and IT nodes
> - Toyota's response: map tiers, buffer selected parts, keep JIT elsewhere
> - Kanban or takt example to show command of mechanics

## 3. Zara / Inditex: Speed Over Forecast
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ 4-5 weeks design to store, ~50% near-home, 12,000 designs, twice weekly drops

### Definition
Zara (Inditex) optimises **responsiveness** over cost. Facts: Zara can get a new product to store in about **4-5 weeks** (modifications in as little as **2 weeks**) against an industry average closer to six months; it produces **over 450 million items a year**, drawn from about **40,000 designs of which roughly 12,000 are produced**; about **half** of merchandise is made in company-owned or near-home sites (Spain, Portugal, Turkey) while long-life basics go to Asia; all goods flow through a Spanish distribution centre and reach most stores within **48 hours**; the average customer visits a Zara store **17 times a year versus 3** for a typical Spanish high-street store; and Zara spends nothing on advertising ([Wikipedia: Zara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zara_(retailer))). Inditex FY2024 revenue was **€38.63 billion** with operating income €7.57 billion across 93 markets ([Wikipedia: Inditex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inditex)).

Mechanism: store managers' sales data drive small, frequent production runs; **postponement** (fabric dyed late, garments cut near the end) and **quick response** replace forecast accuracy ([[139 Product Design & Supply Chain - DFSC, Postponement & Complexity]], [[004 Demand Forecasting & Planning]]).

### Example
Selection ratio is 12,000 / 40,000 = 30%. Average items per design is 450 million / 12,000 = 37,500, small by mass-fashion standards, so a miss strands little stock. Store visit frequency of 17 against 3 is 5.7 times the high-street norm creates a "scarcity" culture where customers buy on sight, which cuts markdowns. Speed advantage: 26 weeks / 4.5 weeks = 5.8 times faster than the six-month norm.

### In the news
See news box. Fast-fashion economics are under scrutiny (EU waste rules, EPR; see [[135 Reverse Logistics, Remanufacturing & EPR in India]]).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why is Zara's supply chain better than a traditional apparel brand's, and could an Indian brand copy it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Speed over forecasts, with the 4-5 week and 12,000 designs facts
> - Cost trade-off: higher unit cost offset by lower markdowns and stock-outs
> - Pre-conditions: near-home capacity, data from stores, small batches, IT discipline
> - Limits: scaling and sustainability criticism

## 4. Dell: Build-to-Order and the Negative Cash Cycle
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Direct model, postponement, negative CCC, retail pivot 2007

### Definition
Michael Dell founded the firm in 1984 and sold computers directly to customers, building to customer specification rather than stocking for channel partners. Customers paid before Dell paid its suppliers, giving a **negative cash conversion cycle**. Revenue rose from $546 million (1990) to $5.3 billion (1995) and $25.3 billion (1999). In 2007 Dell began selling through retailers (Wal-Mart, Sam's Club, then Staples and Best Buy) as consumers wanted to touch products ([Wikipedia: Dell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dell)).

Mechanism: **configure-to-order assembly of standard modules**, supplier hubs near plants, very low component inventory and fast price pass-through when component prices fall ([[139 Product Design & Supply Chain - DFSC, Postponement & Complexity]], [[136 Supply Chain Finance & Working Capital]]).

$$CCC = DIO + DSO - DPO$$

### Example
Illustrative numbers: Dell-style DIO 5, DSO 30, DPO 45 gives CCC $= 5 + 30 - 45 = -10$ days. With COGS of ₹36,500 crore a year (₹100 crore a day) the model *releases* about ₹1,000 crore of supplier-funded cash. A channel-based rival at DIO 40, DSO 45, DPO 45 has CCC $= 40$ days and ties up about ₹4,000 crore. The difference is a **₹5,000 crore swing** in financing, without any cost advantage in the plant.

### In the news
Direct-to-consumer and configure-to-order models live on in laptops, modular furniture and made-to-order apparel; the retail pivot is the classic reminder that a supply chain model must follow how customers buy.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why did Dell's model work, and why did it stop being enough?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Build-to-order with modular design and direct sales; negative CCC
> - Weakness: long lead times for end customers, exposed to component shortages, no retail presence
> - Where the model works today (B2B servers, custom products) and where not (impulse purchases)
> - A CCC computation

## 5. Amazon: Fulfilment Network, Robots and Anticipatory Shipping
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Network density, Kiva $775m, FBA, predictive pre-positioning

### Definition
Amazon's advantage is a dense **fulfilment, sortation and delivery network** layered with data. It started with fulfilment centres in Seattle and Delaware (1997); by 2018 the US network had 75 fulfilment centres and 25 sorting centres. **Fulfilment by Amazon (FBA)** opened the network to third-party sellers in 2006. Amazon bought Kiva Systems for **$775 million in 2012** and had **over 200,000 robots** in warehouses by 2019. In 2020 it delivered about 56% of its own US last-mile volume (Rakuten Intelligence estimate) ([Wikipedia: Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_(company)); [Wikipedia: Amazon Robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Robotics)). A well-known patent describes **anticipatory shipping**: moving items towards a region before the order arrives, using predicted demand (see [[118 New-Product Forecasting, Demand Sensing & Demand Shaping]]).

Mechanisms: inventory placement by region, robot-assisted goods-to-person picking ([[128 Warehouse Labour, WES-WCS & Yard Management]]), and cost-to-serve discipline ([[138 Order Management, Customer Service & Cost-to-Serve]], [[129 E-commerce & Quick-Commerce Fulfilment]]).

### Example
Pre-position if expected benefit exceeds expected waste. Let benefit of a correct pre-position be ₹150 (faster delivery, expedite cost avoided) and cost of a wrong one be ₹60 (re-transfer). Pre-position when
$$p \times 150 > (1 - p) \times 60 \Rightarrow p > \frac{60}{150 + 60} = 28.6\%$$
Because the threshold is only 29%, even a moderately accurate forecast makes pre-positioning pay: the same logic as a newsvendor critical ratio ([[003 Inventory Management]]).

### In the news
See news box. Quick commerce in India is the compressed, urban version of the same dense-network logic.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How does Amazon's fulfilment network create advantage, and how would you replicate it in India?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Network density, inventory placement and automation as three levers
> - Cost-to-serve and the break-even threshold for pre-positioning
> - India specifics: pin-code serviceability, cash on delivery, return rates, seller onboarding
> - Capital intensity and utilisation risk as limits

## 6. Walmart: Cross-Docking, Data Sharing and OTIF
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Pioneered retail cross-docking, Retail Link, OTIF compliance, $713bn revenue

### Definition
Walmart made **logistics a source of low price**. It began applying cross-docking in retail in the **late 1980s**: goods move from inbound trucks to outbound trucks with little or no storage, cutting handling, inventory days and space ([Wikipedia: Cross-docking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-docking)). Flow design depends on door count: "I" shape up to about 150 doors, "T" for 150-200, "X" above 200. FY2025 revenue was **US$713.2 billion** through 10,373 stores and 601 warehouse clubs ([Wikipedia: Walmart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walmart)).

Mechanisms: **shared point-of-sale data** with suppliers through its Retail Link portal (a basis for collaborative replenishment and VMI), **everyday low price** (reduces promotion-driven bullwhip, see [[114 Bullwhip Effect, Beer Game & Information Sharing]]), and supplier compliance scorecards such as **On-Time-In-Full (OTIF)**, where non-compliant suppliers can be fined; the fine rate and targets have been changed over time, so check current supplier rules before quoting them (not verified in this note). Walmart bought 77% of Flipkart for $16 billion in 2018, applying this playbook to India ([[009 Logistics & Distribution]], [[010 Warehouse Management]]).

### Example
$$OTIF = \frac{\text{cases delivered on time AND in full}}{\text{cases ordered}}$$
Suppose 200 purchase orders: 190 on time, of which 184 complete. OTIF $= 184/200 = 92\%$ (below a 98% target). If an illustrative fine of 3% of the cost of non-compliant goods applied to ₹50 crore of annual purchases, the 8% failing share costs 0.03 × 0.08 × 50 = ₹0.12 crore (₹12 lakh). The real cost is smaller than the lost shelf availability, which is why Walmart-style penalties are about *enforcing flow discipline*.

### In the news
Walmart's Flipkart stake carries the model into India. See news box for quick commerce, where inbound OTIF directly drives shelf availability in dark stores.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How did Walmart turn its supply chain into a competitive advantage?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Cross-docking, EDLP, data sharing and supplier collaboration as one system
> - Why cross-docking works only with reliable inbound, tight scheduling and high volume
> - OTIF as a lever and risk (supplier cost-up)
> - Application: how to run a Walmart-like cross-dock for an Indian modern-trade chain

## 7. Apple: Tim Cook's Operations and the China-to-India Shift
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Months to days of inventory, contract manufacturing, long-term component deals, India assembly

### Definition
Tim Cook joined Apple in March 1998 as senior vice president for worldwide operations, **closed factories and warehouses, moved to contract manufacturers and cut inventory from months to days**; his team also signed **long-term and prepaid component deals** (for example flash memory from 2005) that secured scarce supply for iPod, iPhone and iPad ([Wikipedia: Tim Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Cook)). He became COO in 2007 and CEO in 2011. The model: Apple designs, controls key components and the process, and contract manufacturers assemble, with Foxconn's Zhengzhou site (about 200,000 workers in November 2022) producing the bulk of iPhones.

**Diversification to India.** Foxconn started iPhone production in Sriperumbudur near Chennai in 2017; Wistron's Narasapura plant had a labour riot in December 2020 (about 2,000 workers, damage estimated at $7 million), and Tata Electronics later acquired Wistron's iPhone unit (October 2023) and 60% of Pegatron India (January 2025) ([Wikipedia: Foxconn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxconn); [Wikipedia: Wistron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wistron); [Wikipedia: Tata Electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Electronics)). Drivers: tariff and geopolitical risk, India's production-linked incentives ([[145 India Manufacturing & Supply Chain Policy - PLI, Gati Shakti & NLP]]) and local demand. India's current share of iPhone output is not quoted here because it changes quickly: check a recent source. See [[134 Electronics & Semiconductor Supply Chain]].

### Example
Expected-loss view of concentration (illustrative): a 10% annual chance that the main site is out for 4 weeks. Weeks of unmet supply if the alternative site only covers its own share:
- 85% in China, 15% elsewhere: $4 \times 0.85 = 3.4$ weeks
- 75% / 25%: $4 \times 0.75 = 3.0$ weeks
- 60% / 40%: $4 \times 0.60 = 2.4$ weeks

Expected loss falls 29% (from 3.4 to 2.4) when 40% is outside China, with higher labour training and yield costs on the new site: a trade-off.

### In the news
See news box. Wistron's 2020 riot and Tata's takeovers show that the shift is as much about labour management and yield as capital.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why is Apple moving production to India, and is it a good idea?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Risk diversification (tariffs, geopolitics, concentration) plus PLI incentives and market access
> - Ecosystem gaps: component depth, yields, logistics and labour management
> - Tata-Foxconn-Pegatron roles
> - Quantified trade-off between cost and resilience

## 8. Maruti Suzuki: JIT, Supplier Parks and Manesar
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ ~2.25 million capacity, three plants, 2012 Manesar violence, takt time

### Definition
Maruti Suzuki, India's largest carmaker, has an annual production capacity of about **2.25 million vehicles** (Gurugram, Manesar, and Suzuki Motor Gujarat, whose 750,000 capacity is owned by Suzuki Motor Corporation). Manesar covers 600 acres with capacity of about 800,000 a year; Suzuki's stake grew from 26% (1982) to 56.21% (2013). FY2026 production was reported at about 2.42 million units and revenue at about ₹1.88 lakh crore ([Wikipedia: Maruti Suzuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maruti_Suzuki)).

Supply chain model: Japanese-style **supplier parks and tier-1 clusters next to the plants** with high-frequency, sequenced deliveries (JIT), strong vendor development, and a very high share of local content, which makes price and delivery performance co-dependent ([[131 Automotive Supply Chain - JIT, Tiers & EVs]], [[121 Supplier Quality & Automotive Core Tools (APQP, PPAP, 8D)]]). The weakness is **labour-relations risk in a tightly coupled plant**: on **18 July 2012** violence at Manesar killed one manager and injured about 100; the shutdown was reported to cost about **₹75 crore a day** (Wikipedia, above).

### Example
Takt time for Manesar (assumed 300 days, two shifts of 7.5 hours; illustrative):
$$\text{Takt} = \frac{300 \times 15 \times 3600}{800{,}000} = 20.25 \text{ seconds per car}$$
A 10-day stoppage at ₹75 crore a day costs about ₹750 crore: roughly the annual cost of a large supplier-development and labour-engagement programme, so prevention economics are favourable. Because every station is tied to 20 seconds, any supplier delay shows up within minutes.

### In the news
Not tied to the shared news box; EV transition and rising localisation make the supplier base a live issue ([[131 Automotive Supply Chain - JIT, Tiers & EVs]]).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Maruti runs JIT with Indian suppliers. What can go wrong and how would you mitigate it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Supplier-park proximity and sequencing benefits
> - Risks: single-source parts, strikes, road and rail delays, chip shortages
> - Mitigation: buffers on gating parts, dual sourcing, labour engagement, tier-2 visibility
> - Takt-time arithmetic to show how tight the system is

## 9. Amul: The Cooperative Supply Chain
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Three-tier cooperative, 3.6 million producers, ₹90,000 crore, Operation Flood

### Definition
Amul began in 1946 in Gujarat, built by Tribhuvandas Patel and, from 1949, Verghese Kurien, and became the model for **Operation Flood** from 1970, which helped make India the world's largest milk producer. The **three-tier cooperative** has village dairy cooperative societies collecting milk, district unions processing it, and the **GCMMF** marketing and distributing under the Amul brand. About **3.6 million milk producers** are in the network, and revenue reached about **₹90,000 crore in FY2025** (versus about ₹80,000 crore the year before) per Wikipedia ([Wikipedia: Amul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amul)). Amul entered the US (2024, with Michigan Milk Producers) and Spain (June 2025).

Mechanism: twice-daily **village collection with quick chilling**, a cold chain to processing, a wide portfolio (milk, butter, cheese, ice cream, curd) that balances **seasonal flush and lean** milk, and a distribution system that serves retailers directly. Milk is perishable, so the chain is designed around **time and temperature**, see [[133 Food, Agri & Perishables Supply Chain - India]] and [[130 FMCG & Retail Distribution - India Route-to-Market]]. Pooling lakhs of small suppliers solves two problems at once: scale for the processor and market access for the farmer ([[002 Procurement & Strategic Sourcing]]).

### Example
Revenue per producer-member is ₹90,000 crore / 3.6 million = ₹2.5 lakh a year (an average that includes processing value, not farmer income). During the flush season the surplus is converted to skimmed milk powder and butter (storable), and released in lean months: a seasonal-inventory strategy that evens plant utilisation. A 10% unused plant capacity in lean months has a cost of 10% of annual fixed processing cost, which is why portfolio diversification into storable products pays.

### In the news
See news box for India's quick-commerce growth: Amul's presence on Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart places a cooperative supply chain inside dark stores.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What can a corporate learn from Amul's supply chain?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Aggregation of small suppliers with assured procurement and fair price
> - Cold chain and collection frequency designed for perishability
> - Seasonality management through product mix
> - Governance risk and scaling, and how brand and supply chain reinforce each other

## 10. Flipkart and Zepto: E-commerce and Dark-Store Economics
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Big Billion Days, Ekart, Walmart 77%, 10-minute delivery, density

### Definition
**Flipkart.** Its logistics arm **Ekart** also serves other e-commerce players. The first **Big Billion Day (October 2014)** sold about **$100 million of goods in 10 hours**; Walmart agreed to buy **77% for $16 billion** (announced May 2018, completed August 2018); **Flipkart Minutes** (quick commerce) launched in **August 2024** ([Wikipedia: Flipkart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flipkart)). The model rests on **event-driven peak planning**, many fulfilment centres, and a cash-on-delivery and returns-heavy India context ([[129 E-commerce & Quick-Commerce Fulfilment]]).

**Zepto.** Launched July 2021 with a **10-minute promise** from **dark stores** (1,000+ by 2026 per Wikipedia), a network optimised for density: small assortments (thousands of SKUs), micro-warehouses in neighbourhoods, and rider batching. 2025 revenue was about ₹9,366 crore and it raised $450 million at a $7 billion valuation in October 2025; it has also faced criticism over pricing and "dark patterns" in its app ([Wikipedia: Zepto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zepto_(company))).

### Example
Illustrative dark-store break-even: average order value ₹450, gross margin 18% (₹81), delivery cost ₹40 per order, so contribution is ₹41. With monthly fixed cost (rent, staff, utilities) of ₹9 lakh:
$$\text{Break-even orders} = \frac{9{,}00{,}000}{41} \approx 21{,}950 \text{ per month} \approx 730 \text{ per day}$$
Orders per day is the lever: raising density (more orders per rider, per store) lowers delivery cost per order faster than any price change. A 10% rise in delivery cost (₹44) lifts break-even to 9,00,000 / 37 ≈ 24,300 orders a month.

### In the news
See news box for Zepto's valuation and for the ₹30,000 crore to ₹64,000 crore growth in Instamart and Blinkit orders; unit economics and regulation of gig labour are live debates.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Is quick commerce a sustainable business? Walk through the unit economics."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Contribution per order and fixed cost per store, break-even orders per day
> - Levers: AOV, basket mix (private label, ads), density, rider productivity, wastage on fresh items
> - Competitive moves (inventory-led, marketplace, partnerships)
> - Risks: gig-labour regulation, dark-store zoning, price scrutiny

## 11. Reliance Retail: Omnichannel and Kirana Integration
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ 18,836 stores, ₹3.31 lakh crore revenue, JioMart, Metro acquisition

### Definition
Reliance Retail is India's largest retailer by revenue: **18,836 locations across 7,000 towns (2024)** and **₹3,30,870 crore revenue (about $35 billion) in FY2025**, with formats in grocery (Fresh, Smart), electronics (Digital), apparel (Trends, Trends Footwear) and jewellery, and about 207,000 employees. It acquired **Metro Cash & Carry India for ₹2,850 crore in December 2022** (31 stores, over 3 million B2B customers) and grew **JioMart**, an online-offline model launched in December 2019, now in about 200 cities ([Wikipedia: Reliance Retail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliance_Retail)).

Mechanism: **multi-format store network with shared sourcing**, a back end of distribution centres and cold chains, B2B wholesale (Metro) used both to sell to small retailers and to build purchasing scale, and **new commerce** where the digital layer connects neighbourhood kirana stores. The supply chain issue is **assortment and inventory across formats** ([[130 FMCG & Retail Distribution - India Route-to-Market]], [[116 Inventory Valuation, Cycle Counting & Inventory Governance]]).

### Example
Average revenue per location is ₹3,30,870 crore / 18,836 ≈ ₹17.6 crore a year. This is a crude blend: a jewellery or electronics store earns several times a small-format store, which is why **sales density by format** (₹ per sq ft) and **inventory turns by format** are the metrics to benchmark. If a chain with ₹100 crore of annual cost of goods turns inventory at 4× it holds ₹25 crore of stock; improving to 5× cuts stock to ₹20 crore and frees ₹5 crore.

### In the news
See news box. Reliance's moves into quick commerce and consumer brands show the retail supply chain being rebuilt around speed.

> - Digital layer linking stores as mini fulfilment nodes

## 12. Tesla: Vertical Integration and Its Limits
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Make-vs-buy, 4680 cells, gigacasting reversal, 1.81m deliveries

### Definition
Traditional automakers outsource about **80% of components**; Tesla pulls key technologies in-house: batteries (the **4680 cell, introduced in 2021**, about five times bigger than the 2170 type, produced at Gigafactory Texas), motors, software and large **Giga Press** castings meant to simplify body-in-white. During the 2021 chip shortage its integration proved an advantage because it could redesign around available semiconductors. In 2024 it delivered about **1.81 million vehicles**, but in **May 2024** it abandoned next-generation gigacasting plans, reverting to three-piece underbody casting ([Wikipedia: Tesla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla,_Inc.)). Related: [[124 Outsourcing, Supplier Partnerships & Kraljic Strategies]], [[139 Product Design & Supply Chain - DFSC, Postponement & Complexity]], [[131 Automotive Supply Chain - JIT, Tiers & EVs]].

Logic: control technology that is **strategic and differentiating** (batteries, software), outsource commodity items. Risks: capital intensity, loss of supplier innovation, and inflexibility if the technology bet fails.

### Example
Make or buy a component: buy price ₹900; in-house variable cost ₹650 plus fixed investment of ₹5 crore a year.
$$Q^{*} = \frac{F}{P_{buy} - V_{make}} = \frac{5 \times 10^{7}}{900 - 650} = 2{,}00{,}000 \text{ units a year}$$
Above 2 lakh units a year, making is cheaper; below, buying is. Add strategic value (secured supply during shortages) and risk (volume forecast error) before deciding: at 150,000 units the variable saving is 250 × 150,000 = ₹3.75 crore, which does not cover the ₹5 crore fixed cost, a net loss of ₹1.25 crore: a 25% volume miss flips the decision.

### In the news
See news box for the May 2024 gigacasting retreat.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Should an EV startup in India integrate battery packs in-house?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Strategic vs commodity test; make-vs-buy break-even volume
> - Capability, capital and scale conditions
> - Hybrid options: joint ventures, long-term contracts, PLI-linked cell plants
> - Reversibility: Tesla's casting retreat as a cautionary example

## 13. P&G: Bullwhip and Information Sharing
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Pampers case, four causes, VMI, EDLP

### Definition
The **bullwhip effect**: order variability amplifies as one moves upstream from retailer to wholesaler to manufacturer to supplier. Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang (1997) identified four causes: **demand signal processing**, **order batching**, **price fluctuations** (promotions), and **rationing and gaming**. The well-known P&G example is Pampers: consumer demand for diapers was fairly steady, yet orders at the distributor, P&G and its suppliers swung widely ([Wikipedia: Bullwhip effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullwhip_effect) for the four causes; the Pampers illustration follows the 1997 paper). P&G's response was **sharing point-of-sale data**, **vendor-managed inventory** with big retailers, and shifting from heavy promotions to **everyday low pricing**. P&G FY2025 revenue was about US$84 billion ([Wikipedia: P&G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procter_%26_Gamble)). Full theory: [[114 Bullwhip Effect, Beer Game & Information Sharing]], [[001 SCM Introduction & Fundamentals]].

$$\frac{Var(Q)}{Var(D)} \ge 1 + \frac{2L}{p} + \frac{2L^2}{p^2}$$

### Example
With lead time $L = 2$ periods and a moving-average forecast window $p = 5$ periods:
$$1 + \frac{2 \times 2}{5} + \frac{2 \times 4}{25} = 1 + 0.8 + 0.32 = 2.12$$
Order variance is at least 2.12 times demand variance, so the standard deviation is amplified by $\sqrt{2.12} = 1.46$. Shortening the lead time to $L = 1$ gives $1 + 0.4 + 0.08 = 1.48$: lead-time cuts and information sharing, not bigger buffers, tame the whip. Each stage compounds the effect.

### In the news
Not in the shared news box; the 2020-22 shortages are widely explained as a bullwhip (see [[141 Supply Chain Disruption Case Library (2011-2026)]] sub-topic 7).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "A distributor's orders are far more volatile than retail sales. Why, and what do you do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Four causes, each tied to a fix (share POS data, smaller batches/consolidation, EDLP, allocation by past sales)
> - Quantitative intuition ($1 + 2L/p + 2L^2/p^2$)
> - VMI, CPFR and shorter lead time
> - Governance: who owns the forecast and the inventory

## 14. IKEA: Design for Logistics and Cost
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Flat-pack, design-to-cost, ~12,000 products, 504 stores

### Definition
IKEA fixes cost and logistics **at design stage**: furniture is designed to be shipped **unassembled** in flat packs so each shipment carries far more product, to be handled easily, and to be assembled by the customer. Scale: **504 stores in about 60 countries and territories (2025)**, around **12,000 products** on the website, and **€45.1 billion sales in FY2024**; its Swedwood subsidiary employs over 16,000 people across 50 sites in 10 countries and makes about 100 million furniture pieces a year ([Wikipedia: IKEA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IKEA)). Related: [[139 Product Design & Supply Chain - DFSC, Postponement & Complexity]], [[140 Packaging, Unitisation & Load Optimisation]], [[125 Transportation Management Deep Dive]].

Mechanism: designers work with logistics and supplier teams from day one (**design for logistics**), pallets and cartons sized to container modules, suppliers close to raw material, and a self-service warehouse in the store. **Postponement to the customer** (assembly) shifts labour out of the chain.

### Example
Illustrative: a 40 ft container usable volume of 67 m³ at 85% fill gives 57 m³. An assembled bookcase takes 1.2 m³; the flat pack 0.15 m³.
- Assembled: $57/1.2 \approx 47$ units
- Flat-packed: $57/0.15 = 380$ units: **8 times as many**

At a freight cost of ₹2.5 lakh per container, freight per unit falls from about ₹5,300 to about ₹660. For a low-price item this is the difference between viable and non-viable. The trade-off: more damaged-in-assembly claims, lower perceived value, and returns handling ([[135 Reverse Logistics, Remanufacturing & EPR in India]]).

### In the news
IKEA's India expansion (smaller-format stores and online) tests design-for-logistics with local sourcing and city-centre fulfilment; check current facts before quoting.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How can product design reduce supply chain cost? Use IKEA."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Flat-pack and modular design raising cube utilisation (with a numeric example)
> - Early involvement of logistics and suppliers in design
> - Trade-offs: assembly effort, damage and perceived quality
> - Link to standardisation, postponement and complexity reduction
