## 1. Why Business Excellence Models Exist and How They Differ from Standards
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ holistic frameworks; award vs self-assessment; ISO 9001 vs excellence

### Definition
A **business (organisational) excellence model** is a framework of criteria describing how a high-performing organisation is led and managed, used for **self-assessment**, benchmarking and, optionally, competing for an **award**. Main families:

| Model | Origin | Core logic |
|---|---|---|
| **Deming Prize** | Japan (JUSE), 1951 | TQM practice and the organisation's own improvement challenge |
| **Malcolm Baldrige criteria** | USA, law 1987, first award 1988 | 7 categories, 1,000 points, results-heavy |
| **EFQM Model** | Europe, foundation 1988/89 | Direction, Execution, Results; RADAR logic |
| **Shingo Model** | Utah State University, prize from 1988 | Principles and behaviours, culture first |
| **JIPM TPM Award** | Japan | Equipment and plant effectiveness via TPM |
| **National and regional awards** | India: Rajiv Gandhi NQA, CII-EXIM, Golden Peacock, etc. | Mostly Baldrige- or EFQM-derived |

Contrast with **ISO 9001**: a certification standard for a quality management *system* (pass/fail against requirements), whereas excellence models are **non-prescriptive**, score maturity and results, and reward going beyond conformance. ISO 9004 sits between the two (sub-topic 10). Common themes: leadership, customer focus, people, process management, fact-based decisions, innovation, social responsibility and sustained results. See [[011 Quality Management (TQM)]] for the philosophy behind them and [[008 Six Sigma & Quality Tools]] for tools used to meet the criteria.

### Example
Two plants: Plant A holds ISO 9001, IATF 16949 and has zero audit non-conformities; Plant B is ISO 9001 certified and scores 380/1,000 on a Baldrige-style self-assessment, with weak results trends. Plant A's **system** conforms, but conformance does not show whether customer satisfaction, supplier performance and cost are improving relative to peers. The excellence model reveals gaps ISO certification does not examine, such as results versus competitors and leadership involvement.

### In the news
See news box. The Baldrige and Deming developments show these frameworks are still evolving, with new categories, new operators and new framework editions.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What is the difference between ISO 9001 and the Baldrige or EFQM model?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - ISO 9001 is requirements-based certification; excellence models are maturity and results frameworks
> - Models are non-prescriptive and scored; awards are optional
> - Use ISO as a base, then a model to push performance and strategy alignment
> - Examples of models by region (Japan, USA, Europe, India)

## 2. The Deming Prize
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ JUSE, 1951; oldest; TQM; self-defined challenge; Indian winners

### Definition
The **Deming Prize** was set up in 1951 by the Japanese Union of Scientists and Engineers (JUSE) in honour of W. Edwards Deming and is regarded as the longest-running national quality award. Categories: the **Deming Prize** for organisations, the **Deming Prize for Individuals**, and (introduced October 2024) the **Deming Distinguished Practice Award**. It is open to organisations worldwide, including overseas companies.

Distinctive features:
- **No fixed scoring model or checklist.** The applicant defines its own management challenges and approach to TQM; the examiners assess whether the *organisation's own* improvement system, customer-oriented vision and results are effective.
- **Pass/fail**: any number of applicants can pass, or none, in a year.
- Heavy involvement of top management and, in practice, a multi-year preparation (several years is common for first-time applicants; no fixed duration).
- Emphasis on **hoshin kanri**, daily management, quality assurance and the QC mindset, with all levels involved ([[156 Lean Management Systems - A3, Hoshin Kanri & Standard Work]]).
For the Indian industry it has been a heavyweight benchmark, particularly among auto-component, engineering and process firms (see the news box for winners).

### Example
A tyre maker decides to apply. Steps: top-management declaration, management challenge defined (for instance cut customer complaints by 70% and lead time by 40% in three years), TQM promotion office created, cascaded hoshin plans, QC circles and daily management in all plants, preliminary JUSE reviews, then the on-site examination of the management system, the achievement of the stated challenges and executive interviews. Success shows in sustained KPIs, and the prize itself is a milestone rather than an end point.

### In the news
See news box. Indian companies such as Sundram Fasteners and CEAT have used the Deming route, and 2024's new category recognises specific practices rather than the whole organisation.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How does the Deming Prize differ from the Baldrige Award?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Deming: Japanese, no prescribed scoring, organisation sets its own challenge; pass/fail
> - Baldrige: scored 1,000-point criteria, categories and feedback report; limited number of awards
> - Both stress leadership, customers and results, and are TQM-based
> - A mention of Indian winners, if known, to show awareness

## 3. The Malcolm Baldrige Criteria
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ 7 categories; 1,000 points; results 450; core values

### Definition
The **Baldrige Excellence Framework** is the US national model, created by the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Improvement Act (signed 20 Aug 1987); first award November 1988. Run by NIST until the 2025 cycle (see news box). Sectors: manufacturing, service, small business, education, health care, non-profit. Core values include visionary leadership, customer focus, valuing people, organisational learning, systems perspective, innovation, management by fact, societal responsibility and ethics.

Seven categories with the traditional 1,000-point weights (2021 to 2023 editions; verify against the current 2026 framework before quoting):

| Category | Points |
|---|---|
| 1 Leadership | 120 |
| 2 Strategy | 85 |
| 3 Customers | 85 |
| 4 Measurement, analysis and knowledge management | 90 |
| 5 Workforce | 85 |
| 6 Operations | 85 |
| 7 Results | 450 |

Categories 1 to 6 are **process** (how); category 7 is **results** (what was achieved), 45% of the score. The framework is a **systems** view: leadership drives strategy; customers and workforce are the inputs; operations is where value is made; measurement and knowledge management are the base. Applicants write a limited-length application that examiners score, with site visits for top scorers; feedback reports are widely valued, even by non-winners.

### Example
A health-care applicant earns 520 points. Feedback shows strong process scores but results with few years of trend data and few comparisons. Since Results carries 450 points, three years of improved trends and benchmarking with comparable hospitals can add far more points than refining another process description. The applicant shifts effort to data systems, aligned with category 4 ([[012 Supply Chain Analytics & KPIs]] shows how KPI systems are built).

### In the news
See news box. The two 2025 recipients were hospitals, in line with health care's long record in Baldrige.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Name the Baldrige categories and which has the most weight."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Seven categories and 1,000 points, with Results at 450
> - Process vs results distinction
> - Core values (customer focus, management by fact, learning)
> - The caveat that criteria editions change and operations moved from NIST in 2025-26

## 4. Scoring Logic: ADLI and LeTCI
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ process = ADLI; results = LeTCI; bands

### Definition
Baldrige-style scoring treats process and results differently:

- **Process items (categories 1 to 6)** are judged on **ADLI**: **A**pproach (is the method systematic and appropriate?), **D**eployment (is it applied consistently across units and levels?), **L**earning (is it evaluated, improved and shared?), **I**ntegration (does it fit with strategy, other processes and measures?).
- **Results items (category 7)** are judged on **LeTCI**: **Le**vels (current performance), **T**rends (improvement over time, typically 3 or more data points), **C**omparisons (versus competitors or benchmarks), and **I**ntegration (are the important results reported and linked to strategy, segmented by customers and processes?).

Each item is placed in a **scoring band** (0 to 100% in steps of 5), from "no systematic approach, anecdotal" at the bottom, through "early systematic approach" (about 10 to 25%), "aligned and deployed with learning" (about 50 to 65%) to "fully integrated, role-model results" at the top. Category score = the weighted average of its items' percentages times the points. Scoring bands and wording differ in detail from edition to edition, so use them as an indicator.
In EFQM, an analogous logic is **RADAR** (Results, Approach, Deployment, Assessment and Refinement), which uses the same ideas of approach, deployment and refinement, with results assessed for relevance, usability, trends and targets (sub-topic 5).

### Example
Scoring "Operations" (85 points) for a manufacturer: its work processes are documented and linked to requirements (Approach 50%), but only 3 of 6 plants deploy them (Deployment 35%), there are no formal reviews (Learning 30%), and links to strategy are partial (Integration 40%). Overall about 40%: $0.40\times85=34$ points. For a Results item ("on-time delivery"), the level is 94%, the trend improved for 4 years (good), but no competitor comparison (weak): placing at about 45% for that item.

### In the news
See news box. A 2026 framework revision means that wording of the scoring guidelines can change, so always note the edition year when you quote a score.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you score a company on a Baldrige-type model? What do examiners look for in results?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - ADLI for processes, LeTCI for results
> - Evidence of systematic approach, deployment breadth, learning loops and alignment
> - Trends (3+ years), comparisons and segmentation in results
> - Caution: self-scoring bias; calibrate with an external examiner

## 5. EFQM Model 2020 and RADAR
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ Direction, Execution, Results; 7 criteria; stakeholders; RADAR

### Definition
The **EFQM Model** comes from the European Foundation for Quality Management (letter of intent on 15 Sept 1988; foundation established in Brussels in 1989 by 67 European companies). EFQM now serves roughly 50,000 organisations globally by its own account. The model is organised around three questions: **Direction** (why does the organisation exist, what purpose and strategy?), **Execution** (how does it deliver?) and **Results** (what has it achieved and what will it achieve?).

The 2020 model has seven criteria (names as published by EFQM; confirm with the current brochure):

| Part | Criterion |
|---|---|
| Direction | 1 Purpose, Vision and Strategy; 2 Organisational Culture and Leadership |
| Execution | 3 Engaging Stakeholders; 4 Creating Sustainable Value; 5 Driving Performance and Transformation |
| Results | 6 Stakeholder Perceptions; 7 Strategic and Operational Performance |

Distinct features compared with older EFQM versions (9 criteria with "enablers" and "results"): purpose-led, stakeholder-centric (customers, people, partners, society, investors), explicit transformation and sustainability emphasis with a link to the UN Sustainable Development Goals, and a **seven-box 1,000-point** scoring logic (precise weights not quoted here as they could not be verified in this session). Assessment uses **RADAR**: define **R**esults sought, plan and develop **A**pproaches, **D**eploy them, then **A**ssess and **R**efine. EFQM runs recognition levels for organisations and an annual **EFQM Global Award** (level names change by edition).

### Example
A company uses the EFQM structure for a gap review. Direction: purpose statement exists, but strategy is not translated into unit targets. Execution: stakeholder engagement strong for customers, weak for suppliers; no transformation portfolio management. Results: perception metrics (customer NPS, employee engagement) have 3-year trends but lack peer comparison. The review outputs three improvement projects: cascading strategy through hoshin, a supplier engagement programme, and a benchmarking club.

### In the news
See news box. EFQM and Baldrige remain the two main "framework" models, with Baldrige's governance now changing while EFQM remains a membership foundation.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What are the main elements of the EFQM Model and how does RADAR work?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Three parts: Direction, Execution, Results with seven criteria
> - RADAR logic as assessment and refinement cycle
> - Stakeholder and purpose focus, sustainability
> - Contrast with Baldrige: both are scored frameworks but differ in structure and terminology

## 6. The Shingo Model and Shingo Prize
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ principles; culture and behaviours; Bronze, Silver, Shingo Prize

### Definition
The **Shingo Prize** was established in 1988 at Utah State University (Shingo Institute, Huntsman School of Business), named after Shigeo Shingo of the Toyota Production System. It began as a manufacturing prize and, from 2008, shifted to a **principles and culture-based** model for all industries, a reaction to the concern that past winners had not sustained improvement. The change cut the number of annual recipients (from about 11 to about 2, per Wikipedia).

**Shingo Model:** *ideal results require ideal behaviours; purpose and systems drive behaviour; principles inform ideal behaviours.* Ten guiding principles across three dimensions:

| Dimension | Principles |
|---|---|
| Cultural enablers | Respect every individual; lead with humility |
| Continuous improvement | Assure quality at the source; improve flow and pull; seek perfection; embrace scientific thinking; focus on process |
| Enterprise alignment | Create value for the customer; create consistency of purpose; think systemically |

Recognition levels: **Bronze Medallion**, **Silver Medallion**, and the **Shingo Prize**, with evidence of at least three years of sustained improvement required for the formal assessment (the "Shingo Challenge"). Overlap with lean: see [[007 Lean Manufacturing]] and [[156 Lean Management Systems - A3, Hoshin Kanri & Standard Work]]. Indian recipients include Abbott Nutrition supply chain facilities and Visteon Electronics, per Wikipedia.

### Example
A plant claims "we do kaizen". The Shingo-style assessment asks for behavioural evidence: do operators stop the line to assure quality at source? Do leaders go to the gemba daily? Is flow improved across the value stream, and are results sustained for three years? Findings: 5S and kanban are strong (tools), but supervisors do not coach and results swing with leadership changes, so the plant would not reach the top level despite its tool maturity.

### In the news
See news box. In a year when quality-system lapses at a major manufacturer made headlines, Shingo's focus on behaviours and sustained results is relevant ([[155 Reliability Engineering & Maintenance Optimisation]] covers the Boeing door-plug facts).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why did the Shingo Prize change its model in 2008, and how is it different from tool-based lean?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Principles and behaviours determine sustainability, not tools
> - Three dimensions and examples of principles
> - Evidence requirement: sustained results and culture
> - Link to lean and to leader standard work

## 7. The JIPM TPM Award
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ plant effectiveness; staged awards; JIPM; OEE; Indian winners

### Definition
**TPM** (Seiichi Nakajima; first applied in 1971, with Nippon Denso as the first winner of the PM prize per Wikipedia) is promoted by the **Japan Institute of Plant Maintenance (JIPM)**, which runs the **TPM Awards**. The awards are staged and based on documented achievement of the eight TPM pillars (autonomous maintenance, focused improvement, planned maintenance, quality maintenance, early management, training, office TPM, and safety, health and environment), and outcomes such as OEE, breakdown frequency, defects and accident rates ([[022 Maintenance Management (TPM-RCM)]], [[018 Capacity Management & OEE]]). As commonly described, the award ladder runs from the **TPM Excellence Award** (first tier, a plant or division) through the **TPM Consistency Award** and **Special Award** to the **World Class TPM Award** for sustained advanced performance; names and levels are revised from time to time, so confirm on the JIPM site (the JIPM page could not be retrieved in this session). TPM is widely pursued by Indian auto, auto-component, tyre, steel and process companies.

### Example
A tyre plant targets the TPM award. Baselines: OEE 62%, breakdown 6 per month per line, customer complaints 18/month. Over 3 years: autonomous maintenance in all 7 steps, planned maintenance and 5S, kaizen on 6 big losses. Outcomes: OEE 78%, breakdowns 1.5/line/month, complaints down 60% (illustrative). The audit combines reviewing documents, going to the shop floor and examining team presentations. Passing proves plant-level discipline, not merely equipment condition.

### In the news
See news box (it has no TPM-specific item). For current plant-effectiveness context see [[155 Reliability Engineering & Maintenance Optimisation]], whose news box quantifies unplanned-downtime costs that TPM targets.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why would a company go for a TPM award, and what does it take?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Focus on losses, OEE and zero breakdown/defect/accident goals
> - Pillars, multi-year deployment and top-management commitment
> - Benefits beyond the trophy: discipline, teamwork, customer confidence
> - Pitfall: trophy-chasing and a cleaning-only programme

## 8. Indian Awards: Rajiv Gandhi NQA, CII-EXIM, Golden Peacock and Others
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ BIS RGNQA; CII-EXIM (EFQM-based); Golden Peacock (IoD); IMC RBNQA

### Definition
- **Rajiv Gandhi National Quality Award (RGNQA)**: administered by the **Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS)**, introduced 1991 (after Rajiv Gandhi's death) and first presented in 1992. Categories include large and small-scale manufacturing, large and small-scale service, and a "best of all" award, with commendation certificates for specific sectors. It recognises excellence in applying TQM principles.
- **CII-EXIM Bank Award for Business Excellence**: run by the Confederation of Indian Industry (CII) with EXIM Bank, using the EFQM model framework and assessing in stages (self-assessment, application, assessor review, site visit); recognition levels typically include Commendation for Strong Commitment, Commendation for Significant Achievement and the Award itself. (Stated from general knowledge; not re-verified in this session.)
- **Golden Peacock Awards**: given by the Institute of Directors (India) in categories such as business excellence, quality, innovation, eco-innovation, corporate governance and CSR (general knowledge; not re-verified here).
- **IMC Ramakrishna Bajaj National Quality Award (RBNQA)**: from the Indian Merchants' Chamber, Baldrige-based (general knowledge; not re-verified).
International recognition matters more to export-oriented firms; domestic awards matter in public-sector procurement and for brand building.

### Example
An Indian mid-sized auto supplier sequences: ISO 9001 and IATF 16949 first (customer requirement), then TPM and lean programmes, then the **CII-EXIM** journey as the structured self-assessment (EFQM-like), and finally an application to a national or Deming-type award. The firm benefits from the gap report each time, not only from the final recognition.

### In the news
See news box. Many Deming winners from India (Sundram Fasteners, CEAT, JSW Steel, ELGi) are also known on domestic award lists, showing the progression from domestic to international.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Name some Indian business excellence awards and what they assess."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - RGNQA (BIS), CII-EXIM (EFQM-based), Golden Peacock (IoD), RBNQA (IMC)
> - One line per award on origin and approach
> - How firms use them: self-assessment, benchmarking, brand

## 9. The Tata Business Excellence Model (TBEM)
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ Baldrige-based; group-wide assessment; JRD QV Award; corporate use

### Definition
Large Indian groups run their own excellence systems on top of public models. The **Tata Business Excellence Model (TBEM)** is the Tata Group's assessment framework, built on the Baldrige criteria and used across Tata companies for assessment, peer review and recognition, with the group's **JRD Quality Value (JRD QV) Award** as the associated recognition (general knowledge of the Tata system; the Tata newsroom page checked in this session did not contain details, so verify before quoting specifics). Typical mechanics of corporate excellence systems:

1. A common model and **scoring** (1,000-point style) so scores are comparable across companies.
2. Trained internal **assessors** who conduct reviews and site visits and write feedback reports.
3. **Annual cycle**: self-assessment, external assessment, gap analysis, improvement plans, review by the CEO and board.
4. **Maturity levels** (for example, "Emerging", "Progressing", "Mature", "Role model") that decide recognition.
5. Links to the balanced scorecard and executive incentives in some cases ([[225 Budgeting, Variance Analysis & Balanced Scorecard]]).
Benefits: shared language, internal benchmarking, development of assessors as future leaders. Risks: bureaucracy, "scoring games", and the focus on the application instead of business results.

### Example
A group company scores 480 in the first assessment with weak results trends and customer segmentation. Over three cycles it targets 600+, by systematising VOC (voice of the customer), adding a balanced KPI cascade and deploying supplier development. The CEO reviews the same gap list every quarter, so the model becomes part of the management cadence instead of an annual exercise. Score improvement of 120 points over 3 years is illustrative, not a Tata figure.

### In the news
See news box. The Baldrige changes in 2025-26 concern Baldrige-derived corporate models such as this one: the underlying criteria edition (2026) may need to be reflected in group models over time.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How can a large corporate use an excellence model without it becoming bureaucracy?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Link scoring to strategy and business results, not to the paperwork
> - Trained assessors, feedback reports and CEO ownership of gaps
> - Peer benchmarking across business units
> - Limit the effort for application writing; focus on actions

## 10. ISO 9004:2018 and Self-Assessment
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ sustained success; self-assessment tool; vs ISO 9001

### Definition
**ISO 9004:2018** (fourth edition, April 2018, confirmed in 2023) is titled *Quality management: Quality of an organization, Guidance to achieve sustained success*. Unlike ISO 9001, it contains **no certifiable requirements**; it gives guidance for an organisation's overall quality (including the needs and expectations of all interested parties, the context, strategy and leadership, resources, processes, monitoring and improvement), aligned with the quality management principles of ISO 9000:2015. It has a **self-assessment tool** with maturity levels for key elements, so organisations can rate their level of maturity and prioritise improvement. It sits conceptually between ISO 9001 (conformity) and excellence models (maturity and results), and it needs no external examiners. It can serve as the first step for firms not ready for Baldrige, EFQM or a national award. Context for the QMS standards: [[011 Quality Management (TQM)]], [[121 Supplier Quality & Automotive Core Tools (APQP, PPAP, 8D)]].

### Example
A mid-size electronics firm with ISO 9001 and ISO 14001 uses the ISO 9004 self-assessment across 10 elements, scoring itself on the standard's maturity levels. It finds strategy and leadership at level 4 but "monitoring, analysis and review" at level 2. It decides to invest in a data platform and an internal audit of KPIs (link to [[047 MIS & Dashboard Design]]) before attempting an external award.

### In the news
See news box. ISO's confirmation of ISO 9004:2018 in 2023 means it is current, but there is no certification, so its visibility is lower than ISO 9001's.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Is ISO 9004 a certification standard? How does it relate to ISO 9001?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - No, guidance only; ISO 9001 is the certifiable standard
> - 9004 covers sustained success and all interested parties, with self-assessment
> - Sits between ISO 9001 and the excellence models
> - Use as a low-cost first step in self-assessment

## 11. Self-Assessment, Gap Analysis and a Worked Scoring Example
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ category scoring; where the points are; action plan

### Definition
A practical **self-assessment** cycle: (1) pick a model and edition, (2) form a cross-functional team and train assessors, (3) collect evidence per criterion (documents, data, interviews, gemba walks), (4) **score** each item with the model's scale (ADLI/LeTCI or RADAR), (5) calibrate scores in a consensus meeting, (6) list **strengths and opportunities for improvement (OFIs)**, (7) prioritise by **impact on score and on business results**, (8) build an action plan integrated with hoshin and the budget, (9) reassess after 12 to 18 months. Weighted scoring: $\text{Score}=\sum_{c}P_c\times s_c$ where $P_c$ is the category point value and $s_c$ the band percentage. Self-assessment tends to over-score; peer or external calibration is needed.

### Example
A manufacturer's self-assessment on the Baldrige 1,000-point scale (2021 to 2023 weights):

| Category | Points | Band | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leadership | 120 | 50% | 60.0 |
| Strategy | 85 | 40% | 34.0 |
| Customers | 85 | 45% | 38.25 |
| Measurement, analysis and KM | 90 | 40% | 36.0 |
| Workforce | 85 | 35% | 29.75 |
| Operations | 85 | 45% | 38.25 |
| Results | 450 | 30% | 135.0 |
| **Total** | **1,000** | | **371.25** |

Where to act: Process categories (1 to 6) total 236.25 of 550 points (43%). Lifting all six process categories to 60% would add about **94 points** (12 + 17 + 12.75 + 18 + 21.25 + 12.75). Lifting **Results alone from 30% to 50%** adds $450\times0.20=\mathbf{90}$ points from one category, so the plan pursues both together: better data and benchmarks for results and deployment of process improvements to all plants. Results gains normally follow process improvements with a lag, so the plan should not promise the results gain in year one. The score is only a communication device; the real output is a prioritised project list ([[017 Process Management & Optimization]]).

### In the news
See news box. The Baldrige Foundation site reports that the Institute for Performance Excellence launched a National Assessment Center with 350+ customisable self-assessments, a sign that low-cost self-assessment is becoming a common way into these frameworks.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Your plant scored 371 on a Baldrige self-assessment. What would you do next?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Check that the score is calibrated; identify strengths and OFIs
> - Look where points are available: Results (45% of the weight), then process deployment
> - Link actions to business results and hoshin; set a reassessment date
> - Avoid chasing the score; use it as a diagnostic

## 12. Choosing a Model: Comparison and When to Use Which
> 🟡 Tier 3 · _Key points:_ fit to context; cost; recognition; combination

### Definition
| Dimension | Deming | Baldrige | EFQM 2020 | Shingo | JIPM TPM | ISO 9001 / 9004 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Type | Pass/fail prize | Scored criteria, award | Scored model, recognition | Principles-based assessment | Staged award | Certification / guidance |
| Scoring | No fixed score | 1,000 points | 1,000-point logic, RADAR | Behaviours and results | Pillar achievement | Conformity / maturity |
| Emphasis | TQM, hoshin | Leadership, results | Purpose, stakeholders | Culture, principles | Equipment and plant losses | System requirements |
| Typical user | Japanese and Asian manufacturers, Indian auto and engineering | US, health care, education, global firms | European, Middle East firms, CII-EXIM users in India | Lean-oriented plants | Process and assembly plants | Everyone |

Selection guide: start with ISO 9001 or IATF if customers require it; use ISO 9004 self-assessment for a first diagnostic; choose **TPM** for plant effectiveness problems; **Shingo** for a culture of lean; **Baldrige or EFQM** for whole-enterprise strategy and results, especially services and health care; **Deming** for deep TQM by organisations willing to spend years; a **national or group award** for local credibility. Models can be combined, since they are largely compatible.

### Example
A pharma manufacturer with USFDA and WHO-GMP regulation, low OEE and high changeover loss picks: TPM for plant effectiveness (OEE from 55% to 70%), ISO 9004 self-assessment for management-system gaps, and later the CII-EXIM route for enterprise-wide maturity. It does not pursue the Deming Prize now, because top management has not committed multi-year resources. The choice follows the problem, not the prestige.

See news box. Baldrige's governance change and Deming's new category show the landscape shifting; the selection logic stays the same.

> [!tip] Strong answer includes

## 13. ⭐ Advanced: Do Excellence Awards Pay Off? Evidence and Criticism
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
Evidence is positive but needs careful reading:
- A benefit-to-cost study cited by Wikipedia reports a **820 to 1** ratio for the US economy from the Baldrige programme, and awardee stock portfolios outperforming indices. These are programme-sponsored or event-study results, subject to **self-selection** (strong firms apply), survivorship and timing bias; treat them as indicative.
- The Shingo Prize's own history shows the **sustainability problem**: it moved to a culture and principles model in 2008 because past winners lost momentum, and the number of annual winners fell sharply.
- Academic critiques: awards can drive **application-writing industries**, "model chasing", over-documentation, and weak linkage to customer outcomes; high scores on a process-heavy model do not guarantee financial performance.
- Defences: the discipline of evidence-based self-assessment and the feedback report are valuable even without winning; the benefit is in the journey.
Goodhart's law applies: when the score becomes the target, it stops being a good measure. Good practice: tie the model to a small set of business results (e.g., customer retention, OEE, cost of poor quality) and check that these move.

### Example
A firm spends ₹60 lakh over three years on its excellence journey (consultants, assessor training, staff time) and wins a recognised award. Its business case: OEE up 8 points on lines worth ₹150 crore sales at 25% contribution, i.e., $8/62\times150\times0.25\approx₹4.8$ crore a year of contribution if the market takes the output, plus a complaint cost fall of ₹40 lakh. Even if only a fifth of the OEE gain is attributed to the programme, the payback is under a year; attribution is the weak point, so a sceptical CFO will ask which improvements would have happened anyway. (Figures illustrative; OEE base 62%.)

### In the news
See news box. Governance change at Baldrige is a reminder that even a mature programme depends on funding and sponsors, which is an argument for building internal capability rather than relying on an external prize.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Is applying for a quality award worth the cost? How would you decide?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Benefits: structured assessment, feedback, alignment, brand; costs: time, consultants, distraction
> - Evidence caveats: self-selection and sponsored studies
> - Decision rule: pursue only if it supports an existing improvement plan with measurable business results
> - Risk of Goodhart effects; sustain after the award
