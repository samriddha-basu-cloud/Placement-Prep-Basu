## 1. Competitive Priorities
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Cost, Quality, Speed, Flexibility, Innovation — trade-offs

### Definition
**Competitive priorities** are the capabilities operations must excel at to win customers. The usual set:
- **Cost:** low price through efficiency, scale, low waste.
- **Quality:** product (performance, reliability, conformance) and process (consistency).
- **Speed (time):** fast delivery and short lead time; also **dependability** (on-time).
- **Flexibility:** **volume flexibility** (change output levels) and **mix/product flexibility** (change products, customise).
- **Innovation:** speed and ability to launch new products/services.
- Increasingly: **sustainability** and **service**.

Priorities involve **trade-offs**: a plant cannot be best at everything at once with the same resources (cost vs flexibility; speed vs cost). The **sand-cone model** argues capabilities are built in order: quality first, then dependability, speed, and cost. Operations strategy selects 1–2 priorities that match the business strategy and builds the structure and infrastructure decisions (capacity, location, technology, workforce, quality systems, planning) accordingly.

### Example
Indian low-cost carriers (cost, dependability) vs Taj hotels (service quality) vs Zara (speed, flexibility). Maruti competes mainly on cost and quality (dependability) in mass-market cars.

### In the news
See news box. Maruti's scale expansion targets cost and volume flexibility; Nexperia shows what an exclusive cost focus can sacrifice (resilience).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What are the competitive priorities of company X?" or "Can a firm compete on cost and flexibility?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Five priorities with a company example each
> - Trade-offs and the sand-cone view (quality first)
> - Link to operations decisions (capacity, layout, sourcing)
> - Priorities change by segment and by product life-cycle stage

## 2. Order Winners vs Order Qualifiers
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Hill's framework; threshold vs differentiating criteria

### Definition
Terry Hill's framework asks which criteria get a customer to **consider** you vs **choose** you:
- **Order qualifiers (OQ):** minimum level needed to be in the game (threshold). Missing them loses the order, but beating them does not win it.
- **Order winners (OW):** the criteria that **differentiate** and win the order.
Over time winners can become qualifiers (e.g., quality was a winner for Japanese cars in the 1980s; now it is a qualifier). Also **less-important criteria**.

Hill's process: define corporate objectives → marketing strategy (segments) → **order winners/qualifiers per segment** → operations strategy (process choice, infrastructure) that supports the winners. Use customer surveys, loss analysis and sales-team inputs to classify; segment-specific.

### Example
Airline economy segment (domestic India): qualifiers = safety, punctuality threshold; winner = fare. Business traveller: qualifiers = safety, fare within reason; winners = schedule frequency and lounge/flexibility. Different segments, different operations.

### In the news
See news box. Auto buyers: reliability is a qualifier, while price/fuel economy and model features often win; Maruti's operations are built around cost and dependability.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you decide where operations should focus?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Definitions with a clear example
> - Segment-specific classification, from customer evidence
> - Winners drive investment; qualifiers need to be held at threshold
> - Dynamic: winners decay into qualifiers

## 3. Operational Excellence
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Continuous improvement culture; benchmarking

### Definition
Operational excellence (OpEx) is a **management philosophy** of executing core operations better than competitors through a culture of continuous improvement, disciplined standard work, leadership involvement and employee problem-solving. Elements: **Lean**, **Six Sigma**, TPM, TQM, visual management, daily management (gemba walks), and **kaizen**, aligned to strategic goals (e.g., Hoshin Kanri / policy deployment).

**Benchmarking:** compare performance and practices with others. Types: internal, competitive, functional (best-in-class in another industry), generic. Process: select what to benchmark → choose partners → collect data → analyse gaps → set targets → implement → repeat.

Frameworks: **Shingo Model**, **Baldrige**, **EFQM**, **Deming Prize**. KPIs: OEE, OTIF, first-pass yield, cost per unit, safety incidents, employee suggestions.

### Example
Toyota's Production System is the archetype: kaizen as everyday work, with suggestion systems and standard work. In India, Tata Steel is a well-known TQM adopter (it is widely reported to have won the Deming Prize in 2008).

### In the news
See news box. Reaching 1 million units a year from one site requires disciplined operations; scale by itself does not guarantee excellence (no performance metric is reported in the source).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you build a culture of continuous improvement?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Leadership, standard work, problem-solving skills, visual management
> - Benchmarking against internal/external best practice
> - KPI cascade and daily management
> - Sustain: recognise, audit, avoid "programme of the month"

## 4. Productivity Measurement
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Single factor, multi-factor, total factor productivity

### Definition
**Productivity** $= \dfrac{\text{Outputs}}{\text{Inputs}}$.
- **Single-factor (partial):** output per one input (units per labour hour, kWh per unit).
- **Multifactor:** output divided by several inputs combined in common units (₹): $MFP = \dfrac{\text{Output}}{\text{Labour} + \text{Material} + \text{Energy} + \dots}$.
- **Total factor productivity (TFP):** output relative to *all* inputs (labour, capital, materials, energy), often as growth: $\dfrac{\Delta TFP}{TFP} \approx \dfrac{\Delta Y}{Y} - \alpha\dfrac{\Delta L}{L} - (1-\alpha)\dfrac{\Delta K}{K}$ (Solow residual).
Use constant-price (real) values so inflation does not distort. Productivity change $= \dfrac{P_{new} - P_{old}}{P_{old}}$.

Cautions: partial measures can be gamed (replace labour with costly machines: labour productivity up, total down).

### Example
Last year: 5,000 units, 250 labour hours → 20 units/hr. This year: 5,400 units, 240 hours → 22.5 units/hr; change $= (22.5 - 20)/20 = 12.5\%$. Multifactor: output ₹10,00,000; labour ₹2,00,000, materials ₹3,00,000, energy ₹1,00,000, capital ₹2,00,000. MFP $= 10/(2 + 3 + 1 + 2) = 1.25$. If output rises to ₹11,00,000 with inputs ₹7,80,000: $11/7.8 = 1.41$, up 12.8%.

### In the news
See news box. Large plants aim for productivity through scale; comparing per-employee or per-capex figures needs full inputs, which the sources do not provide.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you measure productivity of a plant/warehouse? Why isn't labour productivity enough?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Single, multi- and total factor definitions
> - Real vs nominal values
> - Example with arithmetic
> - Warn about partial measures and gaming

## 5. Cost Leadership Strategy
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Scale, standardization, vertical integration levers

### Definition
Porter's **cost leadership** aims to be the lowest-cost producer for an acceptable quality, then either price at parity for higher margin or undercut. Operations levers:
- **Economies of scale** (spread fixed cost; bigger, specialised assets).
- **Experience / learning curve:** each doubling of cumulative volume reduces unit cost by a constant percentage; an "80% curve" means cost falls to 80% on doubling.
- **Standardisation** (fewer variants, modular platforms, common parts).
- **Process efficiency:** lean, high OEE, capacity utilisation.
- **Vertical integration** or tight supplier cost management (target costing).
- **Location and labour** advantages; low overheads.
Risks: imitation, technology shifts, loss of differentiation, resilience (lean, single-sourced chains).

$$\text{Unit cost}_n = \text{Unit cost}_1 \times n^{b},\ b = \frac{\ln(\text{learning rate})}{\ln 2}$$

### Example
80% curve: first unit takes 100 hours. Each doubling: unit 2 → 80 h, unit 4 → 64 h, unit 8 → 51.2 h. Check by formula: $b = \ln 0.8/\ln 2 = -0.322$; $100 \times 8^{-0.322} = 100 \times 0.512 = 51.2$ ✓. Maruti's cost position in compact cars rests on volume, localisation of parts and platform sharing (general strategy description).

### In the news
See news box. Maruti's build-out to 2.9 million units a year is scale economics; Nexperia is the downside of a lean, concentrated cost-optimised supply base.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How can this company reduce costs by 15% without hurting quality?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Levers: scale, standardisation, design-to-cost, procurement, lean/OEE, location
> - Quantify and prioritise by cost structure
> - Risks: resilience, innovation
> - Cost leadership is not just cheapest price

## 6. Differentiation Strategy
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Customization, quality, speed as differentiators

### Definition
Porter's **differentiation** gives customers something unique that they value enough to pay a **premium** larger than the extra cost of providing it. Operations differentiators:
- **Customisation** (mass customisation, postponement, modular design).
- **Superior quality** and reliability.
- **Speed and dependability** (fast delivery, short lead time).
- **Service and innovation** (new-product speed, after-sales).
Operations implications: flexible processes, skilled workforce, close supplier and customer integration, higher cost tolerance. The premium must exceed the cost: $\text{Premium} > \Delta\text{Cost}$. Differentiators must be sustainable (hard to copy) and tied to winners in the target segment.

### Example
Titan watches (design and variety), Zara (speed: new designs in weeks), Dell (build-to-order customisation through postponement). A premium-quality example: Apple's design, software and tightly managed supply base.

### In the news
See news box. Micron-type advanced packaging plants differentiate on technology capability rather than low cost alone; national policy attaches strategic value to such capability.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How can operations help the company differentiate?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Link to a customer-valued attribute (order winner)
> - Which operations capability delivers it (flexibility, speed, quality)
> - Cost-to-serve vs premium
> - Defensibility

## 7. Focused Operations
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Skinner's 'focused factory'; simplicity principle

### Definition
Wickham **Skinner (1974)** argued that a factory that tries to do everything (many products, technologies, volumes) performs worse than one **focused** on a narrow set of products, customers or tasks; simplicity and repetition give better performance. A "plant-within-a-plant" (PWP) lets one site host several focused units, each with its own organisation, layout and rules.

Focus dimensions: **product** (family), **market** (segment), **process/technology**, **volume**, **geography**. Benefits: clear priorities, learning and experience curve effects, simpler control, lower overhead. Risks: loss of scale in shared services, vulnerability if the focus market shrinks, need for coordination.

### Example
A hospital: Narayana Health's cardiac-surgery focus and high volume improves cost per surgery and outcomes (widely cited focus example). An auto firm separating an EV line from ICE lines within the same site is a plant-within-a-plant.

### In the news
See news box. Hansalpur is a very large site focused on high-volume passenger cars; Micron's plant focuses on one step of the chip value chain (assembly, test, marking and packaging), a focused-operations choice.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Should the company make everything in one plant or split into focused plants?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Skinner's argument and the plant-within-a-plant option
> - Dimensions of focus (product, process, market)
> - Trade-off: scale and flexibility vs simplicity
> - Concrete decision criteria: volume per family, process conflict, demand outlook

## 8. Operations-Strategy Alignment
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Fitting SC strategy to product life cycle

### Definition
Operations strategy must **fit** (a) the business strategy and (b) the product's stage in its life cycle. Typical mapping:

| Stage | Demand / competition | Priority | Operations / supply chain posture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Introduction** | Low volume, uncertain | Innovation, quality, flexibility | Flexible, responsive; small batches, close supplier ties |
| **Growth** | Rapid growth | Speed, capacity, quality consistency | Scale up capacity, build supply, forecasts, availability |
| **Maturity** | Stable, price competition | Cost, dependability | Efficient, lean; standardisation, low inventory |
| **Decline** | Falling | Cost, harvesting | Consolidate, reduce capacity, minimise inventory |

Link with **Fisher's framework** (functional products need an efficient chain; innovative products need a responsive chain) and **strategic fit** (Chopra & Meindl): the chain's responsiveness should match demand uncertainty. Check consistency across *structural* (capacity, facilities, technology, vertical integration) and *infrastructural* (planning systems, quality, HR, organisation) decisions. See also [[001 SCM Introduction & Fundamentals]].

### Example
A smartphone launch: use flexible assembly and air freight for the first months (responsive, high margin); at maturity, shift to dedicated lines, ocean freight and low inventory (efficient). A staple like Parle-G is in maturity: cost and dependability.

### In the news
See news box. Apple-style India assembly and Micron's plant show structural choices of where to build as products and policies change; Nexperia shows an efficiency-aligned chain breaking when conditions shifted.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How should supply chain strategy change as a product matures?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Life-cycle table with priorities
> - Fisher / strategic fit link
> - Structural vs infrastructural consistency
> - Modern addition: resilience across all stages

## 9. ⭐ Advanced: Hayes–Wheelwright Four Stages of Operations Contribution
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
Hayes & Wheelwright describe how operations' role in strategy evolves:
1. **Stage 1: Internally neutral:** minimise operations' negative impact; reactive, outsourced fixes.
2. **Stage 2: Externally neutral:** match competitors; follow industry best practice, benchmark.
3. **Stage 3: Internally supportive:** support the business strategy; operations is aligned to chosen priorities.
4. **Stage 4: Externally supportive:** operations capabilities **drive** strategy; they are a source of competitive advantage and may open new markets.

Slack's **four perspectives**: top-down (corporate strategy drives operations), bottom-up (improvements drive strategy), market requirements, and **operations resources** (the resource-based view: valuable, rare, inimitable, organised: VRIO). A good strategy combines market and resources.

### Example
Toyota, Zara, and Amazon are classic Stage-4 operations: the supply chain itself is the offering (speed of design to shelf for Zara, delivery promise for Amazon). A firm that treats plants as cost centres with no say in product decisions is Stage 1-2.

### In the news
See news box. India's chip and auto expansions show manufacturing capability treated as strategic (Stage 3-4 thinking at national and corporate level).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How can operations be a source of competitive advantage?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Four stages with an example
> - Resource-based view: capability that is hard to copy
> - Market-driven vs resource-driven strategy
> - Concrete capability (speed, flexibility) mapped to customer value

## 10. ⭐ Advanced: Make-or-Buy and Global Operations Footprint Strategy
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
Strategic operations decisions include **what to make in-house (vertical integration) vs outsource**, and **where to produce**. Make-or-buy criteria: strategic importance and core competence, cost (including transaction cost), capacity, quality/IP risk, supplier capability, asset specificity, and supply risk. Cost break-even:
$$Q^* = \frac{F_{make}}{c_{buy} - v_{make}}$$
(Make if volume $> Q^*$ when making has higher fixed cost and lower variable cost.)

Footprint: global vs local, **China+1**, nearshoring, offsets and incentives, tariffs, risk. Consider total landed cost, time-to-market, flexibility, and resilience (see [[015 Supply Chain Risk & Resilience]] and [[019 Facility Layout & Location]]).

### Example
Buy price ₹120 per part. Making needs ₹30 lakh fixed and ₹70 variable. $Q^* = 3{,}000{,}000/(120 - 70) = 60{,}000$ parts. At 80,000 parts: make cost $= 30{,}00{,}000 + 70 \times 80{,}000 = ₹86$ lakh vs buy $= 120 \times 80{,}000 = ₹96$ lakh; making saves ₹10 lakh, if capacity and quality are secured.

### In the news
See news box. Micron's plant and Maruti's expansion are footprint decisions supported by policy and market scale; Nexperia is a make-or-buy/sourcing risk lesson.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Should the company manufacture a component itself or buy it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Break-even volume plus strategic criteria
> - Core vs non-core capability, IP and quality risk
> - Supplier market and dependency
> - Hybrid options: partial make, dual source
