## 1. Cost-Reduction Case Structure
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Baseline → cost tree → benchmark → levers → savings vs one-off cost → risks

### Definition
A cost-reduction case asks how a client should lower costs, by how much, how fast and without breaking the business. Where [[025 Case Interview — Profitability]] diagnoses *why* profit fell, this case assumes the target is cost and designs the programme. Structure (the generic version is in [[024 Consulting Frameworks]]):

1. **Clarify:** target (a ₹ amount, a margin, a percentage), time frame, and constraints (service levels, no plant closures, union agreements, brand).
2. **Baseline and cost tree:** where does the money go? Split by cost type, fixed versus variable, and activity.
3. **Benchmark:** where is the cost out of line against peers, history and best practice? The gap sizes the prize.
4. **Levers:** a prioritised long list of actions per cost line, with impact, effort and time.
5. **Business case:** run-rate saving, phasing, one-off costs to achieve, payback and NPV.
6. **Risks and sustainability:** what could break (quality, service, morale), how to lock in savings and stop them leaking back.
7. **Recommendation and roadmap:** quick wins, the structural moves, owners and tracking.

$$\text{Saving needed}=\text{Target margin}\times\text{Revenue}-\text{Current profit}\quad(\text{revenue constant})$$

Always state the **order of magnitude first**: 1 percentage point of margin on ₹1,000 crore revenue is ₹10 crore; a 10% cost cut on a 90% cost base is a 9-point margin gain.

### Example
An auto-component maker has revenue ₹1,000 crore and costs ₹900 crore (EBIT margin 10%). The board wants a 15% margin. Required saving = 15% × 1,000 − 100 = **₹50 crore**, or 5.6% of the cost base. Your first sentence: "We need roughly ₹50 crore a year; I will test whether a benchmarked gap of that size exists, which levers close it, and what it costs to get there."

### In the news
See news box. Intel's plan follows the same sequence: a headcount and layer target (levers), capacity and project cancellations (footprint), and a restructuring charge of $1.5 billion in one quarter (the one-off cost).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Our client's margins have fallen to 8% and the CEO wants them back to 12% within two years. How would you approach it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Clarifies the target, time frame and constraints, and sizes the gap in ₹
> - Tree, benchmark, levers, business case, risks, in that order
> - States an early hypothesis (for example "materials and labour productivity are the biggest pools")
> - Mentions one-off costs and sustainability before being asked

## 2. Cost Tree and Baseline
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Pareto of cost lines; fixed vs variable; cost per unit and % of revenue; activity view

### Definition
Build the cost tree along the dimension that is most actionable, then **Pareto** it: the biggest 3–4 lines usually hold 70–80% of the cost.

- **By nature:** materials, direct labour, energy, logistics, plant overheads, selling, general and administrative (SG&A), depreciation.
- **By behaviour:** variable (moves with volume), semi-variable, fixed (see [[110 Cost Accounting for Operations]]).
- **By activity or value chain:** procurement, production, distribution, selling, support functions.
- **By controllability:** what management can change (usage, staffing, supplier mix) versus what it cannot (commodity price, regulated tariffs, statutory levies).

Compute for each line: absolute ₹, **% of revenue**, **cost per unit** and its trend. Separate the **price effect** (rate), the **volume effect** and the **efficiency effect** (usage per unit, yield, scrap, overtime). Check what is hidden inside a line: expedited freight, rework, inventory write-offs, and **complexity cost** from too many SKUs, suppliers or variants ([[139 Product Design & Supply Chain - DFSC, Postponement & Complexity]]).

### Example
Auto-component maker (illustrative), revenue ₹1,000 crore:

| Cost line | ₹ crore | % of revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | 500 | 50.0% |
| Direct labour | 150 | 15.0% |
| Energy | 60 | 6.0% |
| Logistics | 50 | 5.0% |
| Plant overheads | 80 | 8.0% |
| SG&A | 60 | 6.0% |
| **Total** | **900** | **90.0%** |

Materials plus labour are 650 of 900, or 72% of cost: that is where the case will be won. Drill into materials: steel about half of the line, bought spot, with three suppliers for the same grade: a procurement hypothesis. Drill into labour: overtime 12% of hours: a scheduling and productivity hypothesis.

### In the news
See news box. Intel's cuts illustrate the activity view: the savings come from headcount (labour), management layers (overhead), and avoiding capital spend (capex), not a uniform trim across lines.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Here is the client's cost breakdown. Where would you start?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Uses Pareto: biggest, most controllable lines first
> - Separates price, volume and efficiency effects
> - Looks at cost per unit and % of revenue over time, not only absolutes
> - Names a hidden driver (complexity, rework, expediting)

## 3. Benchmarking and Gap Sizing
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Internal, peer, best-in-class; normalise; gap × capture rate = prize

### Definition
**Benchmarking** compares the client's cost (as % of revenue, per unit, per employee, per tonne-km) with:
- **Internal benchmarks:** best plant, region or shift versus the rest (usually the most credible, because the context matches).
- **Peer benchmarks:** listed competitors' annual reports, industry bodies, consultant databases.
- **Best-in-class benchmarks:** leaders from any industry for a function (warehouse productivity, procurement cost).

**Normalise** before comparing: product mix, scale, degree of vertical integration, outsourcing, labour cost region, accounting definitions, capacity utilisation. A gap that disappears after normalisation is not an opportunity.

$$\text{Prize}=\sum_i(\text{Cost}_i-\text{Benchmark}_i)\times\text{Capture rate}$$

Capture rate (how much of the gap is realistically closable) is usually 50–80% for operations and lower where the gap reflects structural differences. Use [[012 Supply Chain Analytics & KPIs]] and [[018 Capacity Management & OEE]] for operational KPIs, and [[122 Spend Analysis, Savings & Procurement Maturity]] for procurement gap work.

### Example
Same client; peer or best-plant benchmarks as % of revenue: materials 46%, labour 13%, energy 5%, logistics 4%, plant overheads 7%, SG&A 5%. Gap in ₹ crore: materials 50 − 46 = **40**; labour 15 − 13 = **20**; energy **10**; logistics **10**; plant overheads **10**; SG&A **10**; total gap = **₹100 crore** (10 points of margin). At a 60% capture rate the **run-rate prize is ₹60 crore**, which comfortably covers the ₹50 crore target: the case is feasible, but with only ₹10 crore of cushion, so every lever matters.

### In the news
See news box. Airtel's statement that ARPU of ₹300 was needed versus about ₹210 is a benchmark in reverse: it is a required level of return on spectrum and network investment, used to justify the price move rather than a cost cut.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The client's cost per unit is 12% above the industry. Is that a real opportunity?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Normalises for mix, scale, integration and region before sizing the gap
> - Uses internal best-practice as well as external peers
> - Applies a capture rate to turn a gap into a prize
> - Asks what *causes* the gap, not only how large it is

## 4. Cost Levers: Procurement, Operations, Footprint, Complexity and Overheads
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Spend, make, move, sell, support; quick wins vs structural

### Definition
Organise levers by cost line so the list stays MECE, and tag each as **quick win** (under 6 months, low investment), **medium** (6–18 months) or **structural** (needs capex or restructuring).

| Area | Typical levers |
|---|---|
| **Procurement (materials, services)** | Spend consolidation, competitive tendering, specification change and value engineering ([[154 Product & Service Design - QFD, DFMA & Value Engineering]]), index-linked contracts ([[123 Commodity Price Risk, Hedging & Contract Pricing Mechanisms]]), make-or-buy ([[124 Outsourcing, Supplier Partnerships & Kraljic Strategies]]) |
| **Operations and labour** | Yield and scrap reduction, OEE and downtime ([[018 Capacity Management & OEE]]), lean and standard work ([[007 Lean Manufacturing]], [[156 Lean Management Systems - A3, Hoshin Kanri & Standard Work]]), shift and overtime redesign, automation |
| **Energy and utilities** | Efficiency audits, power-purchase agreements, captive or renewable power, waste-heat recovery |
| **Logistics and distribution** | Network redesign ([[113 Network Design & Facility Location Modelling]]), load optimisation, mode shift, carrier tendering ([[125 Transportation Management Deep Dive]]), cost-to-serve pruning ([[138 Order Management, Customer Service & Cost-to-Serve]]) |
| **Footprint** | Plant and warehouse consolidation, relocation, closing sub-scale sites |
| **Complexity** | SKU, supplier and variant rationalisation; platform standardisation |
| **Overheads and SG&A** | Spans and layers, shared services, outsourcing of non-core functions, zero-based budgeting (sub-topic 13), vendor and contractor spend |
| **Revenue-side cost levers** | Price and mix management, discount discipline, trade-spend effectiveness |

Prioritise levers on **impact (₹) versus ease (time, investment, risk)**; show an impact–effort 2×2 and name the top five. Avoid the **across-the-board cut**: it hits profitable and unprofitable activities alike and rarely holds.

### Example
For the ₹60 crore prize: materials ₹24 crore (tender steel across three suppliers plus index-linked contract, 4% on the 50% of materials that is steel = ₹10 crore; specification change on two components ₹6 crore; yield and scrap ₹8 crore); labour ₹12 crore (overtime from 12% to 6% of hours, line rebalancing); energy ₹6 crore (power-purchase agreement); logistics ₹6 crore (full-truck-load consolidation); overheads and SG&A ₹12 crore (spans and layers, shared services). Total: **₹60 crore** (24 + 12 + 6 + 6 + 12), of which about ₹20 crore are quick wins (tendering, overtime, energy contract).

### In the news
See news box. Intel's actions map to the table: footprint (cancelled plants in Germany and Poland, Costa Rica consolidation), overheads (management layers cut by half), and capex (slowed Ohio build).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "List the ways a consumer-goods manufacturer could cut ₹100 crore of cost."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Levers grouped by cost line (not a random list)
> - Quick wins separated from structural changes
> - Impact and effort ranking, with a rough ₹ per lever
> - Avoids an across-the-board cut; targets by value pool

## 5. Savings vs One-Off Costs: Run-Rate, Phasing, NPV and Payback
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Run-rate vs in-year; cost to achieve; payback; NPV

### Definition
Distinguish **run-rate savings** (annualised steady-state), **in-year savings** (what lands in the P&L this year, depending on timing) and **one-off costs to achieve** (severance, plant closure, systems, consultants, penalties, contract exit). A rough rule of thumb (it varies widely): one-off cost of about **0.5–1.5 times the annual run-rate** for structural programmes, much lower for procurement. Evaluate as a project: see [[109 Valuation Basics (NPV, IRR, DCF)]] and [[224 Capital Budgeting for Operations - Capex, Lease vs Buy & Replacement]].

$$NPV=\sum_t\frac{\text{Savings}_t-\text{One-off costs}_t}{(1+r)^t},\qquad \text{Payback}=\text{years until cumulative net cash turns positive}$$

Also: **stranded costs** (costs left behind after an activity is removed), **cost avoidance** versus **cost reduction** (avoiding a future increase is not a reduction in today's P&L), and **tax**. Count only savings that appear in the P&L or cash flow and are signed off by Finance.

### Example
Prize ₹60 crore run-rate, phasing 30% / 70% / 100% / 100% / 100% in years 1 to 5; one-off cost ₹75 crore (1.25x run-rate), spent ₹50 crore in year 1 and ₹25 crore in year 2; discount rate 12%, pre-tax.

| Year | Savings | One-off | Net cash | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 18 | 50 | −32 | −32 |
| 2 | 42 | 25 | +17 | −15 |
| 3 | 60 | 0 | +60 | +45 |
| 4 | 60 | 0 | +60 | +105 |
| 5 | 60 | 0 | +60 | +165 |

NPV (pre-tax, 12%) ≈ **₹100 crore**; after tax at 25% ≈ **₹75 crore**. Payback occurs in year 3 (about 2.25 years). Margin impact: EBIT margin rises from 10% to 16% at run-rate, but **year-1 EBIT improves by only ₹18 crore minus the one-off bill**: say it explicitly. If the CFO asks "when will the board see 15%?", the answer is year 3, not year 1.

### In the news
See news box. Intel's $1.5 billion restructuring charge in the same quarter in which it announced the headcount reduction is the one-off cost made visible: the P&L got worse before it got better.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The programme will save ₹60 crore a year but costs ₹75 crore to implement. Is it worth it, and when do we see the benefit?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Separates run-rate, in-year saving and one-off cost
> - Computes payback and NPV (even approximately)
> - Mentions tax, stranded costs and cost avoidance vs reduction
> - Says when the target margin will actually be reached

## 6. Risks, Trade-offs and Sustaining the Savings
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Service and quality, people, supplier and customer reaction, rebound; governance

### Definition
Group the risks, then give each a mitigation and an early-warning metric:

1. **Service and quality risk:** cutting inspection, maintenance or inventory can raise defects, downtime and stock-outs (see [[022 Maintenance Management (TPM-RCM)]], [[011 Quality Management (TQM)]]).
2. **People risk:** attrition of the wrong people, morale, union and legal constraints (retrenchment rules in India differ by establishment size and state), loss of tacit knowledge.
3. **Supplier risk:** over-squeezing suppliers, single-sourcing, supplier distress ([[015 Supply Chain Risk & Resilience]]).
4. **Customer and revenue risk:** price or service changes that lose customers; revenue loss can exceed savings.
5. **Execution risk:** unrealistic targets, too many initiatives at once, no owners.
6. **Rebound risk:** costs creep back within 12–24 months unless budgets, incentives and processes change.

Mitigation: **stage gates**, a **transformation office** with a savings tracker signed off by Finance, targets embedded in budgets and in managers' scorecards ([[225 Budgeting, Variance Analysis & Balanced Scorecard]]), **guardrail KPIs** (on-time delivery, defect rate, customer satisfaction) alongside the savings KPI, and a pilot before roll-out.

### Example
Plan to cut maintenance spend by ₹6 crore. Guardrails: unplanned downtime must stay below 4%; if breakdown cost rises more than ₹2 crore, the cut is rolled back. A cut that saves ₹6 crore but raises downtime from 3% to 6% on a ₹1,000 crore business can cost far more in lost sales: if each point of downtime is about ₹4 crore of lost contribution, the net result is −₹6 crore. (Illustrative figures; the point is that an unguarded saving can be negative.)

### In the news
See news box. Intel's CEO said earlier capacity investments had been "well ahead of demand", an over-investment the plan reverses; note that the cuts were aimed at excess capacity and layers rather than a uniform trim.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What could go wrong with this cost-cutting plan, and how would you make the savings stick?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Categories of risk (service and quality, people, supplier, customer, execution)
> - Guardrail KPIs and stage gates
> - Finance-validated savings tracking and budget embedding
> - Explicit rebound risk and how to counter it

## 7. Turnaround Diagnostics: Liquidity First
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Cash runway and 13-week cash flow before strategy; working capital; lenders

### Definition
A **turnaround** case involves a company in distress: sustained losses, falling cash, covenant breach or missed payments. The first question is **survival**, then strategy. Order:

1. **Liquidity and runway:** cash on hand + undrawn facilities ÷ monthly cash burn. Build a **13-week cash flow forecast** (weekly receipts and payments) and manage cash daily.
2. **Cause of distress:** revenue collapse, cost structure, over-leverage, one bad contract, fraud, industry downturn? Split operational causes from financial ones.
3. **Stakeholders:** lenders, suppliers, employees, regulators; what each can do (call a loan, stop supply).
4. **Stabilise:** stop cash leaks, free working capital (receivables, inventory, payables), cut non-essential spend, protect the revenue that earns cash.
5. **Restructure the business** (sub-topic 8) and **the balance sheet** (debt rescheduling, equity infusion, asset sale, or a formal process such as the insolvency resolution route under India's Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code if the business is not viable out of court).

$$\text{Runway (months)}=\frac{\text{Cash}+\text{Undrawn committed lines}}{\text{Monthly cash burn}}$$

Burn is **cash**, not EBITDA: include interest, capex, tax and working-capital movement ([[136 Supply Chain Finance & Working Capital]]).

### Example
Distressed manufacturer: revenue ₹600 crore, EBITDA −₹60 crore, interest ₹40 crore, maintenance capex ₹20 crore, cash ₹90 crore, no undrawn lines. Annual cash burn = 60 + 40 + 20 = ₹120 crore, or **₹10 crore a month; runway = 90 / 10 = 9 months**. Levers: cut DSO (receivable days) from 60 to 45: release = 600 / 365 × 15 = **₹24.7 crore**; sell a non-core plot for **₹70 crore**. New runway = (90 + 24.7 + 70) / 10 = **18.5 months**, but this only buys time; the real fix must cut the ₹120 crore burn. Note the one-time nature of working-capital release.

### In the news
See news box. Intel's actions illustrate stabilising a business with a still-strong balance sheet: slowing the $28 billion Ohio project preserves cash; a smaller firm in distress would have no such room and would start with the 13-week forecast.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "A manufacturer is losing money and has covenant issues. How do you decide what to do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Liquidity and runway first, with a 13-week cash view
> - Distinguishes operational causes from financial structure
> - Quick cash levers (working capital, non-core assets), then structural fixes
> - Considers lenders, suppliers and employees as stakeholders

## 8. Core vs Non-Core and the Stabilise–Restructure–Grow Sequence
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Contribution vs fully-allocated profit; stranded costs; exit/fix/invest; sequencing

### Definition
After stabilising cash, decide **what to keep**. For each business, product, customer or plant, ask:

- **Strategic role:** is it core to the competitive advantage and the customer value proposition?
- **Economics on a contribution basis:** contribution margin (price − variable cost) and the *avoidable* fixed costs, not the fully-allocated profit; see [[110 Cost Accounting for Operations]].
- **Fixability:** can it reach target returns, and how fast?
- **Value to others:** is it worth more to a buyer than to us?

Decision: **fix** (turn around), **invest** (grow), **harvest** (cash, minimal investment) or **exit** (sell, close, run off). Beware the **allocation trap**: a unit that looks loss-making after allocated overheads may be contribution-positive; closing it strands the overhead on the rest.

**Sequence:** *Stabilise* (0–3 months: cash, quick wins, leadership and communication) → *Restructure* (3–12 months: exit non-core, cost reset, fix the balance sheet) → *Re-grow* (12+ months: invest in the core, new products, pricing). Dealing with the balance sheet and operations in parallel is typical.

### Example
Division B: revenue ₹150 crore, variable cost ₹125 crore, so contribution ₹25 crore. Allocated fixed overhead ₹70 crore, so fully-loaded EBITDA = −₹45 crore. But only ₹20 crore of the overhead is avoidable if B is closed. **Exit impact on group EBITDA = −25 (lost contribution) + 20 (avoided cost) = −₹5 crore**, i.e. closing B makes the group *worse* off unless the sale proceeds or released capital justify it, or the stranded ₹50 crore is also restructured. A sale of B for a good price, with the overhead cut in parallel, is a different (better) answer.

### In the news
See news box. Intel's scrapping of European fabs and consolidation of operations is an exit-or-defer decision on non-core capacity; the key question the interviewer would ask is the same: what overhead is avoidable and what remains.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "One of the client's three divisions shows a large loss. Should it be shut?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Contribution margin and avoidable costs, not allocated profit
> - Strategic role and value to a buyer, with fix/invest/harvest/exit options
> - Stranded-cost mitigation and sequencing (stabilise, restructure, re-grow)
> - Stakeholder impact (employees, customers, lenders)

## 9. Pricing Case Structure: Cost, Competition and Value
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Cost floor, competitor reference, customer-value ceiling; segments; objective

### Definition
A pricing case asks for the price of a new product, a price change, or a response to a competitor. First **clarify the objective** (profit, share, volume, cash, positioning, deterring entry), then use the three anchors (see also the pricing framework in [[024 Consulting Frameworks]]):

| Anchor | Question | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | What is the unit variable cost and the full cost? | Floor; **cost-plus** pricing ignores demand and leaves money on the table |
| **Competition** | What do close substitutes charge? What will they do if we move? | Reference; **competitor-based** pricing ignores differences in value |
| **Customer value** | What is the product worth to the customer (**willingness to pay, WTP**)? | Ceiling; **value-based** pricing: economic value to customer (EVC) = reference-product price + value of differentiation |

Then: **segments** (who values it most, and can we separate them?), **price structure** (list, discounts, tiers, subscription, usage-based), **strategy** (skimming versus penetration), and **reaction and regulation** (competitor response, regulatory price controls, perception).

### Example
New fertiliser additive; the closest substitute costs farmers ₹400 per acre per season. The additive raises yield by 3%, worth ₹900 per acre of extra revenue. EVC = 400 (reference) + 900 (differentiation) = ₹1,300 as the **ceiling**. Unit cost is ₹250. A value-based price captures part of the surplus: ₹800 gives the farmer ₹500 of benefit (a clear reason to buy) and the maker a ₹550 unit margin. A cost-plus price at 30% markup (₹325) would leave about ₹475 per unit on the table; a price of ₹1,300 would give the farmer nothing and fail.

### In the news
See news box. Telcos priced against value and the market: Jio's entry plan went from ₹155 to ₹189 (+21.9%) and the 1 GB/day plan from ₹209 to ₹249 (+19.1%), still low in absolute terms, with extra benefits (unlimited 5G on higher plans) and rivals moving in step.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "A pharma company has a new drug with a modest benefit over an existing one. How should it price it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Objective first, then cost floor, competitor reference, value ceiling
> - Segment by WTP and consider access, payers and regulation
> - Explains why cost-plus and pure competitor pricing are weaker
> - Considers competitor reaction and positioning

## 10. Price Elasticity and Break-Even Maths
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Elasticity; break-even volume for a price change; optimal price; do not cut price blindly

### Definition
**Price elasticity of demand** $\varepsilon=\dfrac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P}$ (negative for normal goods; |ε| > 1 is elastic). For a price change $\Delta P$ from price $P$ with unit variable cost $VC$ (contribution $CM=P-VC$):

$$\text{Break-even volume change}=\frac{-\Delta P}{CM+\Delta P}$$

Interpretation: a price cut is only worth it if volume rises by more than this; a price rise is safe if volume falls by less than this. The **actual** volume change is $\varepsilon\times\%\Delta P$ (a linear approximation, good for small changes). The profit-maximising price under constant elasticity is

$$P^*=\frac{VC\cdot|\varepsilon|}{|\varepsilon|-1}$$

(needs |ε| > 1). Higher contribution margins make price *cuts* more attractive and price *rises* riskier; low margins do the opposite. Elasticity differs by segment, channel and time; estimate it from past price changes, experiments (A/B or geo tests) or survey methods (conjoint, Van Westendorp).

### Example
Price ₹200, VC ₹120, volume 50,000 units: contribution ₹80 × 50,000 = ₹40 lakh. **Price cut 10% to ₹180:** CM falls to ₹60; break-even volume = 40,00,000 / 60 = 66,667 (+33.3%). If ε = −1.5, volume rises 15% to 57,500 and profit = 57,500 × 60 = **₹34.5 lakh (−13.8%)**; only if ε = −4 (+40% volume, 70,000 units) does profit rise to ₹42 lakh (+5%). **Price rise 10% to ₹220:** CM ₹100; break-even volume = 40,000 (−20%); with ε = −1.5, volume −15% to 42,500, profit **₹42.5 lakh (+6.3%)**; with ε = −2.5, volume falls 25% to 37,500 and profit is 37,500 × 100 = ₹37.5 lakh (−6.3%). Optimal price for ε = −2.5 is 120 × 2.5 / 1.5 = **₹200**, i.e. the current price is already optimal; for ε = −1.5 it would be ₹360 (a signal that the constant-elasticity model is not trustworthy over that range).

### In the news
See news box. For a telecom operator with high contribution margin and low variable cost, the break-even loss of subscribers from a ~20% price rise is small: with ARPU ₹200 and variable cost ₹20, a ₹40 rise breaks even until about 18% of subscribers leave (40 / (180 + 40)) (my illustration, not company data). That arithmetic is why a coordinated sector hike, with low churn because of high switching costs, can be so profitable.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The client sells at ₹200 with a variable cost of ₹120. A competitor cut price by 10%. Should the client match?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Break-even volume formula applied with the right contribution margin
> - Compares break-even with plausible elasticity, by segment
> - Considers non-price responses (value, bundles, targeted discounts) and the risk of a price war
> - Asks how to measure elasticity (tests, history)

## 11. Bundling, Price Discrimination and Pricing Architecture
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Capture surplus; bundle when WTPs are negatively correlated; good-better-best; versioning

### Definition
Different customers have different WTP; the aim is to capture more of the surplus without losing the price-sensitive segment.

- **Price discrimination:** by segment (students, seniors), time (peak pricing), quantity (volume discounts), geography or channel; needs **separability** (customers cannot resell or switch) and fairness.
- **Versioning / good-better-best:** tiers designed so customers self-select; the middle tier is the anchor.
- **Bundling:** pure (only the bundle) or mixed (bundle plus individual items). It works best when customers' valuations of the items are **negatively correlated**: bundle prices reduce the variance of WTP.
- **Freemium and subscription:** low-cost entry then upgrade; see unit economics in [[031 Product Metrics & Analytics]].
- **Dynamic pricing:** change price with demand, with fairness and regulatory limits.
- **Penetration vs skimming:** penetration for network effects and scale economies; skimming for new, differentiated products with inelastic early adopters.

### Example
Two products X and Y, VC ₹20 each, two customers. Customer A: WTP X ₹100, Y ₹40 (bundle ₹140). Customer B: WTP X ₹60, Y ₹90 (bundle ₹150). **Selling separately:** best prices are X at ₹100 (A only) or ₹60 (both): profit ₹80 either way; Y at ₹90 (B only): ₹70; total **₹150**. **Bundle at ₹140** (both customers buy both): profit = 2 × (140 − 40) = **₹200**, an increase of ₹50 (+33%), because the WTPs for X and Y are negatively correlated.

### In the news
See news box. Jio keeping unlimited 5G data on plans of 2 GB/day and above while raising prices (per Business Standard and TechCrunch) is a versioning logic: a benefit is attached to the higher tiers, which, arguably softens the price rise for heavy users and nudges others upward.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "A streaming service wants to grow revenue per user. What pricing options would you consider?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Segment by WTP; tier or bundle so customers self-select
> - Explains when bundling works (negatively correlated valuations)
> - Considers fairness, cannibalisation and regulation
> - Quantifies at least one option

## 12. Delivering the Recommendation and Frequent Traps
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Answer first; number, timing and risk; common mistakes across these case types

### Definition
Structure the close as: **recommendation, why (2–3 reasons with numbers), risks and mitigations, next steps**. For these case types state: the **size of the prize**, the **cost and timing**, and the **one thing that could change the answer**. Practise in 30–60 seconds (see [[162 Structured Communication - SCQA, Storylines & Case Delivery]]).

**Frequent traps:**
1. **Savings without a cost to achieve:** quoting run-rate savings and ignoring severance, systems and the time to get there.
2. **Allocation trap:** closing a "loss-making" unit that is contribution-positive, stranding fixed cost.
3. **Across-the-board cut:** hitting profitable and critical activities alike.
4. **EBITDA vs cash:** a distressed company runs out of cash, not of EBITDA; ignoring interest, capex and working capital.
5. **Cost-plus and price-match reflexes:** ignoring value and elasticity; matching a competitor's price cut without a break-even check.
6. **Revenue-synergy optimism:** treating price rises or cross-sell as certain, with no churn.
7. **Ignoring the stakeholders:** lenders, suppliers, unions, regulators.
8. **Static thinking:** ignoring competitor reaction, or that cost savings leak back.
9. **Arithmetic slips:** percentage points vs percent; per-unit vs total; one-off vs recurring.

### Example
**Cost-case close (30 seconds):** "I recommend a ₹60 crore programme. The gap to benchmark is ₹100 crore; at a 60% capture rate we get ₹60 crore, above the ₹50 crore target. About ₹24 crore comes from materials (tendering, specifications, scrap) and ₹12 crore from labour and overtime, with a third being quick wins. It costs about ₹75 crore one-off, so payback is about 2.25 years and the margin target is reached in year 3, not year 1. The main risks are quality and service, so I would put guardrails on defect rate and on-time delivery. Next steps: validate the benchmark, launch the procurement tender, and set up a savings tracker owned by Finance."

**Pricing-case close:** "Do not cut the price 10%: it needs +33% volume to break even and realistic elasticity gives +15%, a 14% profit loss. Instead, protect the core segment and use a targeted offer for the price-sensitive one."

### In the news
See news box. The two stories carry opposite lessons for delivery: the Jio and Airtel hikes show a price move that worked because competitors moved together; the Intel restructuring shows that cost savings arrive with a visible one-off bill.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Wrap up. What do you recommend, and what are the biggest risks?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Recommendation first, then 2–3 numbered reasons with ₹ figures
> - Timing and cost to achieve; when the benefit shows up
> - Top risk with a mitigation; next steps with owners
> - Avoids the traps above (especially allocation, cash vs EBITDA, price matching)

## 13. ⭐ Advanced: Price Waterfall, Should-Cost and Zero-Based Budgeting
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
**Price waterfall (pocket-price analysis):** list price minus on-invoice discounts, off-invoice rebates, payment-terms cost, freight and service concessions gives the **pocket price**; the real margin is pocket margin, and the spread between customers is often wide. A cost-and-price case often ends with "tighten discount governance".

**Should-cost analysis:** a bottom-up estimate of what a part ought to cost (material at current index × usage + scrap + conversion + overhead + reasonable margin), used to challenge supplier quotes and set negotiation targets ([[122 Spend Analysis, Savings & Procurement Maturity]]).

**Zero-based budgeting (ZBB):** every cost must be justified from zero each budget cycle rather than set as last year plus a percentage; effective for overheads and discretionary spend, but resource-intensive, so apply it by cost pool and repeat on a rolling basis. Learning-curve effects ([[152 Learning Curves, Work Measurement & Productivity]]) also give an expectation for unit-cost decline with cumulative volume.

### Example
**Waterfall:** list price ₹100; on-invoice discount ₹8; volume rebate ₹4; payment-terms cost ₹2; freight absorbed ₹3 → **pocket price ₹83**. With VC ₹60, margin at list is ₹40 but pocket margin only **₹23** (42.5% lower); a customer with pocket price ₹75 is barely profitable, which is a discount-governance case, not a cost case.

**Should-cost:** casting needs 1.2 kg of steel at ₹60/kg = ₹72; scrap allowance 5% = ₹3.6; conversion 12 minutes at ₹2.5/min = ₹30; overhead 10% of (72 + 3.6 + 30) = ₹10.56; supplier margin 8% on the total ₹116.16 = ₹9.29 → **should-cost ≈ ₹125.5**. The supplier quotes ₹140: the gap is ₹14.5 (10.4% of the quote); a negotiation target of ₹128–130 allows for supplier realities.

### In the news
See news box. Intel's halving of management layers is a structural, ZBB-like reset of overhead; and the sector-wide telecom tariff action is a price-governance exercise at scale, where the waterfall (list tariff versus realised ARPU) is what the market reads.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Revenue is growing but margins are shrinking. Where could price leakage be?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Builds a price waterfall to find where list price leaks to pocket price
> - Compares customers or segments on pocket margin, not list margin
> - Uses should-cost to challenge supplier prices
> - Proposes governance (discount approval, rebate rules) and tracks pocket price
