## 1. Why Enter This Market?
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Attractiveness: size, growth, competition, profitability

### Definition
Assess **market attractiveness** before anything else. Standard cuts:
- **Size** (value, volume) and **growth** (CAGR = (End/Start)^(1/n) − 1)
- **Profitability** of the industry (Porter's Five Forces: rivalry, entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power)
- **Trends** and regulation, **customer needs** unmet today
- **Strategic fit:** does it use our capabilities, brand, or cost advantage?

Output: a clear yes/no on attractiveness, with the 2–3 numbers that matter.

### Example
Market of ₹10,000 cr growing at 12% CAGR; five-year size = 10,000 × 1.12⁵ ≈ **₹17,600 cr**. If industry EBITDA margin is 8% and incumbents hold 70% share, a new entrant targeting 5% share in year 5 reaches ~₹880 cr revenue and ~₹70 cr EBITDA at industry margins.

### In the news
See news box. Quick commerce attracted entrants because of growth (large, fast-growing GOV), even though unit economics were thin: growth ≠ profitability, so state both.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "A global beverage firm wants to enter India's packaged-water/energy-drink market. Should it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Structure up front (market, competition, capabilities, financials, entry mode)
> - Size and growth with a sanity-checked estimate
> - Industry profitability, not just size
> - Hypothesis first, then test

## 2. Where to Enter?
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Geography, segment, channel choice

### Definition
Prioritise **which slice**: geography (tier-1 metros vs tier-2/3 cities), customer segment (premium, mass, B2B), channel (modern trade, general trade, e-commerce, D2C). Score options on attractiveness (size, growth, margin) vs ability to win (access, fit, competition). Pick a **beachhead** and sequence expansion.

### Example
A premium coffee chain: score 6 metros on income density, café penetration and rent. Enter Bengaluru and Mumbai first (highest density), defer Tier-2 until unit economics are proven.

### In the news
See news box. Quick-commerce chains opened dark stores first in dense metro pin codes, then extended outward.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Which city / segment should we launch in first?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Explicit criteria and weights; a simple scoring table
> - A beachhead logic and a rollout sequence
> - Link to the company's capabilities (distribution reach, brand)

## 3. How to Enter?
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Organic, M&A, JV, licensing, franchising — trade-offs

### Definition
| Mode | Speed | Control | Capital | Risk | Best when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organic / greenfield | Slow | High | Medium–High | Execution | Strong capabilities, time available |
| Acquisition (M&A) | Fast | High | High | Integration, overpayment | Need share/capabilities quickly |
| Joint venture | Medium | Shared | Shared | Partner conflict | Regulation, local knowledge needed |
| Licensing / franchising | Fast | Low | Low | Brand dilution | Asset-light scale |
| Exporting / distributor | Fast | Low | Low | Dependence | Test demand |

Decide by speed, control, capital, capability gap, regulatory limits (FDI caps).

### Example
Walmart bought a majority stake in Flipkart (2018, ~$16 bn) to enter Indian e-commerce rather than build organically, because multi-brand retail FDI limits and capability gaps made greenfield hard.

### In the news
See news box. Apple chose *contract manufacturing* partners (Foxconn, Tata) plus PLI incentives rather than owning plants, an asset-light entry.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Should the client build, buy, or partner?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Compare 3 modes against criteria (speed, control, cost, risk)
> - Tie recommendation to the client's gap (capability or time)
> - Regulatory constraints and exit options

## 4. Competitive Response
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Incumbent reactions; first-mover vs fast-follower

### Definition
Predict how incumbents react: price cuts, promotion, exclusive supply deals, product proliferation, legal/regulatory moves. Reaction depends on their **cost position, market share at stake, and capacity**. **First-mover** advantages: brand, scale, switching costs, scarce resources. **Fast-follower** advantages: learn from errors, lower cost, sharper positioning.

### Example
If an incumbent has 70% share and 30% margin, a price war is credible. If price falls 10% (price 100 → 90, variable cost 70), contribution per unit drops from 30 to 20, so volume must rise **50%** just to hold total contribution ($30/20 - 1 = 50\%$).

### In the news
See news box. In quick commerce, HSBC's expectation that players move from rapid expansion to "optimising capacities" shows how an arms race on store count can end in a capacity glut: a risk to flag when you recommend entry.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How will competitors respond if we enter?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Names the most likely reaction and why (cost/share logic)
> - A plan to pre-empt (differentiation, niche entry, partnerships)
> - Quantifies the impact where possible

## 5. Financial Feasibility
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Investment, payback, NPV, IRR basics for cases

### Definition
- **Payback** = Investment / annual cash inflow
- **NPV** = Σ CFₜ/(1+r)ᵗ − I₀; accept if NPV > 0
- **IRR**: rate at which NPV = 0; accept if IRR > hurdle rate
- **Break-even volume** = Fixed cost / (Price − Variable cost per unit)
Steps in a case: revenue build (market × share × price), cost build, investment, timeline, sensitivities.

### Example
Investment ₹100 cr; years 1–5 net cash flows ₹10, 25, 35, 40, 45 cr; discount rate 12%. NPV ≈ 10/1.12 + 25/1.2544 + 35/1.4049 + 40/1.5735 + 45/1.7623 − 100 = 8.93 + 19.93 + 24.91 + 25.42 + 25.53 − 100 ≈ **₹4.7 cr** (marginally positive). Payback: cumulative 10, 35, 70, 110 → between year 3 and 4 (≈ 3.75 years).

### In the news
See news box. Quick-commerce players' payback depends on orders/store/day; HSBC noted a shift to "optimising capacity", i.e. unit economics over expansion.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Will this be profitable, and when do we recover the investment?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Clean revenue and cost build with stated assumptions
> - Payback/NPV (don't over-engineer)
> - Sensitivity on the 1–2 variables that matter (price, share)

## 6. Go/No-Go Recommendation
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Synthesize attractiveness + capability + financials

### Definition
Close with a crisp answer-first recommendation: **Recommend / Don't recommend**, 2–3 supporting reasons (attractiveness, ability to win, financials), the **key risks** with mitigations, and **next steps**. Structure: *"I recommend X because A, B, C. The main risk is R, which we mitigate by M. Next steps: 1, 2, 3."*

### Example
"I recommend entering Bengaluru and Mumbai via a JV with a local distributor. The market is ₹10,000 cr growing 12%, we can reach 5% share by year 5, NPV is positive at 12%. Main risk: a price war from the incumbent; mitigate by premium positioning. Next: pilot in one city, validate unit economics."

### In the news
See news box. Real decisions combine all three (e.g. Apple's India shift balances cost, tariffs, scale and risk).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Time's up; what's your recommendation?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Answer first, in under 30 seconds
> - Three reasons, one risk, next steps
> - Honest about uncertainty, no new analysis in the close
