## 1. M&A Case Structure
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Why → what is it worth → synergies → risks → integration

### Definition
An M&A case asks whether a client (the **acquirer**) should buy, merge with, or sell a business (the **target**), and at what price. A standard five-block structure keeps the answer MECE (see [[024 Consulting Frameworks]] for the generic M&A framework and [[027 Case Interview — Market Entry]] for the build/buy/partner logic):

1. **Strategic rationale:** why this deal, and why buy rather than build or partner? What does the client gain: scale, capability, customers, geography, technology, or defence?
2. **Target assessment and standalone value:** market attractiveness, competitive position, financial health, valuation using multiples or DCF.
3. **Synergies:** cost and revenue, how large, how certain, how fast, at what one-off cost.
4. **Price, funding and financial impact:** maximum price, premium, cash/debt/stock mix, EPS and ROIC effect, balance-sheet capacity.
5. **Risks and integration:** diligence red flags, regulatory approval, culture and talent, customer attrition, IT and operations integration plan.

The core valuation identity that ties blocks 2 to 4 together:

$$\text{Maximum price}=\text{Standalone value}+\text{PV of realisable synergies}-\text{PV of integration costs}$$

Pay more than that and the acquirer's shareholders lose value; the seller wants to capture as much of the synergy as possible, so the acquirer's goal is to keep part of it.

Clarify first: which side is the client (buy-side, sell-side, or a financial investor), the **objective** (growth, cost, capability, defensive), the budget and funding constraints, and the timeline. Then state the structure aloud in 30 seconds.

### Example
Prompt: "An Indian packaged-foods company is considering buying a regional snacks brand. Should it?" Opening structure: "I would look at four things. Strategic fit: does the brand fill a gap in our portfolio or geography? Value: what is it worth standalone and what synergies can we realise? Price and financing: how much can we pay before we give away all the synergy? Risks and integration: what could destroy the value? My initial hypothesis is that it is attractive if distribution overlap gives real cost synergies and the price leaves us at least a third of the synergy value. Can I ask about the target's size and growth first?"

### In the news
See news box. Each headline deal fits the five blocks: UP–NS (rationale: first coast-to-coast rail network; synergy $2.75 billion a year; regulatory risk is the gating item), Tata–Iveco (rationale: complementary footprints; risk: separation of the defence business and approvals).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Our client, a mid-sized pharma company, is thinking of acquiring a competitor. How would you decide whether to proceed?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Clarifies side, objective and constraints before structuring
> - Five blocks: rationale, standalone value, synergies, price and funding, risks and integration
> - States the walk-away logic (standalone value plus realisable synergies minus costs)
> - Gives an early hypothesis and the single most important data request

## 2. Strategic Rationale and Deal Types
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Horizontal, vertical, conglomerate, capability; buy vs build vs partner

### Definition
Classify the deal first because the rationale and the synergy type follow from it:

| Deal type | What is bought | Typical synergy | Typical risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal** (competitor) | Market share, scale | Cost: overhead, procurement, network; some pricing power | Antitrust review, customer attrition |
| **Vertical** (supplier or distributor) | Control of the value chain | Margin capture, supply security, lower transaction costs | Loss of focus, supplier lock-in, rival customers leave |
| **Adjacent / market-extension** | New geography or segment | Revenue: cross-sell, new customers | Local-market knowledge, overpaying for growth |
| **Capability / tech (acqui-hire)** | Technology, talent, IP | Speed to market | Talent retention, culture clash |
| **Conglomerate / diversification** | Unrelated business | Mostly financial (diversification) | Weak synergies; usually punished by markets |
| **Distressed / turnaround** | Assets at a discount | Asset value, tax shield | Hidden liabilities; see [[160 Case Interview - Cost Reduction, Turnaround & Pricing]] |

**Build vs buy vs partner:** build when there is time and an internal capability; partner or joint venture when risk and capital need to be shared (the Reliance–Disney joint venture merged two media businesses without a full takeover); buy when speed, scarce assets or a customer base matter more than cost. Test whether the rationale is a genuine **strategic gap** (the client cannot get there alone) or just **size for its own sake**, which rarely creates value. See [[023 Business Fundamentals & Strategy]] for the strategy lenses (Porter, Ansoff, core competence) behind this.

Also ask what the deal prevents: a defensive rationale (stopping a rival or a foreign entrant from buying the asset) is legitimate but should be stated as such and priced accordingly.

### Example
A cement company buys a ready-mix concrete firm (vertical): rationale is a captive demand outlet, giving volume certainty for its plants. Synergy is not the cement margin itself (that is an internal transfer) but better capacity utilisation and avoided distributor margin. A cement company buying a hotel chain (conglomerate) has no operating synergy: ask why shareholders could not diversify on their own.

### In the news
See news box. Tata–Iveco is positioned as a **market-extension deal** with "substantially no overlap" in industrial and geographic footprints; UP–NS is **horizontal end-to-end** (the networks join rather than overlap), a distinction that matters for regulators; the HDFC merger was **vertical-plus-adjacent** (a mortgage originator into a bank with a larger funding base).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why would a steel company buy a mining firm, and what would worry you about the deal?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Names the deal type and the synergy that follows (vertical: secure supply and capture margin)
> - Compares buy with build and partner
> - Flags the type-specific risk (customer or supplier conflict, antitrust)
> - Separates real strategic gaps from growth-for-growth's-sake

## 3. Standalone Valuation and Multiples Quick Maths
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ EV vs equity value bridge; EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/Sales; comps vs transactions

### Definition
**Enterprise value (EV)** is the value of the operating business; **equity value** is what belongs to shareholders:

$$\text{Equity value}=EV-\text{Net debt}-\text{Minorities}-\text{Debt-like items}+\text{Non-operating assets}$$

Debt-like items include unfunded pension and gratuity liabilities, lease liabilities (depending on accounting standard), and contingent tax or legal claims. Common multiples:

$$\frac{EV}{EBITDA},\quad \frac{EV}{Sales},\quad \frac{P}{E}=\frac{\text{Price per share}}{EPS}=\frac{\text{Equity value}}{\text{Net profit}}$$

- Use **EV multiples** (EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales) when comparing firms with different leverage; use **P/E** only for equity-level comparisons.
- **Trading comparables** reflect minority, market-day prices; **transaction comparables** include a control premium (often 20–40%) and so usually run higher.
- Use **EV/Sales** for loss-making growth firms; **EV/EBITDA** for mature ones; adjust for growth and margin differences (a higher growth target deserves a higher multiple).
- Cross-check with a **DCF** ([[109 Valuation Basics (NPV, IRR, DCF)]]) and sanity-check the implied multiple against growth and returns. For cost of capital see [[226 Corporate Finance Essentials - Capital Structure & Cost of Capital]]; for statements and ratios see [[108 Financial Statements & Ratios]].

Mental shortcuts: a 10x EV/EBITDA is a 10% EBITDA yield on EV, and a change in EBITDA of ₹1 changes EV by the multiple.

### Example
Target EBITDA ₹120 crore; peers trade at 9x EV/EBITDA, so EV = 9 × 120 = **₹1,080 crore**. Net debt ₹200 crore, no minorities: equity value = 1,080 − 200 = **₹880 crore**; with 10 crore shares, ₹88 per share. If the same company is valued at a transaction multiple of 10x: EV ₹1,200 crore and equity ₹1,000 crore, so a 10x transaction multiple implies a **13.6% premium over the trading value of equity** (1,000 / 880). Fast check: EV/Sales = 1,200 / 800 = 1.5x for revenue of ₹800 crore.

### In the news
See news box. UP–NS shows how premiums are quoted: the **25% premium to the 30-day VWAP** is measured against an unaffected price; Tata–Iveco gives two premia (22–25% on cash alone, 34–41% including the dividend), showing why you must always ask what the premium is measured against.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "A firm with EBITDA of ₹120 crore and net debt of ₹200 crore; peers trade at 9x EBITDA. What is it worth to shareholders?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Computes EV first, then bridges to equity (net debt, minorities, debt-like items)
> - Picks the multiple to suit the situation and adjusts for growth and margin
> - Distinguishes trading from transaction multiples (control premium)
> - Cross-checks with a second method (DCF or a transaction comparable)

## 4. Synergy Quantification: Cost, Revenue and Dis-synergies
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Cost vs revenue synergies; phasing; haircut; dis-synergies; one-off costs

### Definition
**Synergy** is value created by combining that neither firm could achieve alone. Size it bottom-up, line by line.

| Type | Examples | Reliability | Typical haircut in a base case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost synergies** | Procurement scale, duplicate overhead, network and logistics consolidation, plant rationalisation, IT | High | 0–20% |
| **Revenue synergies** | Cross-selling, wider distribution, pricing, new products | Low | 50% or more |
| **Financial synergies** | Lower cost of funding, tax shield, better working capital | Medium | Case-specific |
| **Dis-synergies** | Customer attrition, lost key staff, channel conflict, management distraction | Often missed | Add explicitly |

Quantify: **run-rate** (steady state), **phasing** (for example 40% in year 1, 80% in year 2, 100% from year 3) and **one-off costs to achieve** (severance, plant closure, systems, rebranding), which are often **1.0–1.5x the annual run-rate cost savings** as a rule of thumb. Convert **revenue synergies to profit at contribution margin**, not at revenue. Tax-effect everything.

$$PV_{synergies}=\sum_t \frac{(\text{Run-rate}\times\text{Phasing}_t)(1-\text{tax})}{(1+r)^t}-PV(\text{one-off costs})$$

See [[122 Spend Analysis, Savings & Procurement Maturity]] for how procurement savings are built up and [[110 Cost Accounting for Operations]] for the fixed-versus-variable treatment of overhead savings.

### Example
Target revenue ₹800 crore, COGS 60% (₹480 crore). Bottom-up synergies: procurement saving 3% of target COGS = **₹14.4 crore**; logistics consolidation ₹18 crore; overhead reduction ₹12 crore, so cost synergies = **₹44.4 crore**. Revenue synergy: cross-selling adds 5% of target revenue = ₹40 crore of sales at 30% contribution margin = **₹12 crore**. Dis-synergy: 2% customer attrition = ₹16 crore of sales × 30% = **₹4.8 crore**. Headline synergies are 44.4 + 12 = ₹56.4 crore; **risk-adjusted** (cost at 90%, revenue at 50%, less dis-synergy) = 39.96 + 6.0 − 4.8 = **₹41.2 crore** pre-tax run-rate. One-off cost: ₹55 crore (about 1.24x the cost run-rate). The risk-adjusted number is about 27% lower than the headline: that is the figure to take to the price discussion.

### In the news
See news box. UP–NS quantifies **$2.75 billion a year** of run-rate synergies, which at a simple 10x capitalisation would be about $27.5 billion before costs, consistent in scale with the claimed "over $30 billion" of value creation (derived arithmetic, not a company figure; the company's own methodology is not given in the release). The Reliance–Disney valuation was explicitly "excluding synergies", so any synergy is upside to be shared.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The two companies have overlapping distribution. How big are the synergies, and how much would you trust them?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Builds synergies bottom-up by line and separates cost from revenue
> - Applies haircuts and phasing; converts revenue to contribution margin
> - Names dis-synergies and one-off costs, tax-effects the result
> - Concludes with a risk-adjusted number and the sensitivity of the price to it

## 5. How Much to Pay: Premium and Value Sharing
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Walk-away price; premium; share of synergy paid to the seller

### Definition
The acquirer should compute a **walk-away price** and an **opening bid** below it. The premium is the amount paid above the target's unaffected market value; it is a transfer of synergy value to the seller.

$$\text{Buyer NPV}=\text{PV of net synergies}-\text{Premium paid}$$

$$\text{Share of synergy paid away}=\frac{\text{Premium paid}}{\text{PV of net synergies}}$$

Guidelines: paying away **more than about half to two-thirds** of a risk-adjusted synergy value leaves little margin for execution error; paying more than 100% destroys value. Consider **structures** that share risk: earn-outs tied to the target's results, contingent value rights, stock instead of cash (the seller then shares in the synergy and the risk), and an escrow against diligence findings. Compare also with **alternatives** (acquiring a different target, organic build, partnership) and with a **buyback** of the same cash.

### Example
Continuing the snacks-brand case: standalone equity value ₹1,000 crore; after tax and risk-adjustment, PV of net synergies = ₹258.9 crore less PV of one-off costs ₹35.5 crore, so **net synergy value ≈ ₹223.5 crore**; the walk-away equity price is 1,000 + 223.5 = **₹1,223.5 crore**, a maximum premium of 22.3%. The seller asks 30%, which is ₹1,300 crore: premium ₹300 crore = **134% of the synergy**, so buyer NPV = 223.5 − 300 = **−₹76.5 crore**. A 15% premium (₹1,150 crore) pays away 67% and leaves the buyer **+₹73.5 crore**. Recommendation: bid about 10–15%, walk away above about 22%, and offer an earn-out for anything beyond that.

### In the news
See news box. In Tata–Iveco and UP–NS, premia of roughly 22–25% to unaffected prices are the headline; the pertinent question for an analyst is what fraction of the claimed synergy each premium represents (for UP–NS, the stock-plus-cash structure also shares synergy risk with Norfolk Southern holders, who receive about 27% of the combined company).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The seller wants a 30% premium. Should we agree?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Computes the walk-away price from standalone value plus risk-adjusted net synergies
> - Expresses the premium as a share of synergy value
> - Suggests structures (earn-out, stock, escrow) to bridge a valuation gap
> - Names alternatives and a walk-away threshold

## 6. Financing and EPS Accretion or Dilution
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Cash vs debt vs stock; P/E comparison; EPS maths; break-even synergies

### Definition
Funding choices: **cash** (balance sheet), **new debt** (cheap after tax, raises leverage), **stock** (preserves cash, dilutes ownership, shares risk). Quick test for an all-stock deal: if the **acquirer's P/E is higher than the price P/E paid**, the deal is EPS-accretive before synergies; if lower, dilutive. For debt-funded deals, compare the target's **earnings yield** (1 / P/E paid) with the **after-tax cost of debt**.

$$\text{Pro forma EPS}=\frac{NI_{buyer}+NI_{target}-\text{After-tax interest on new debt}+\text{After-tax synergies}}{\text{Old shares}+\text{New shares}}$$

Accretion is **not** value creation: a deal can be EPS-accretive and still destroy value if the return on the purchase price is below the cost of capital. Use **ROIC** (NOPAT of the target plus synergies divided by the price paid plus costs) against WACC ([[226 Corporate Finance Essentials - Capital Structure & Cost of Capital]]). Check leverage: net debt / EBITDA of the combined company, covenant headroom and rating impact. For model mechanics see [[188 Financial Modelling in Excel]].

### Example
Buyer: net profit ₹600 crore, 100 crore shares, EPS ₹6, share price ₹120 (P/E 20x). Target net profit ₹54 crore (EBITDA 120, D&A 30, interest 18 on ₹200 crore of debt, tax 25%). Price **₹1,150 crore** (P/E paid 21.3x); funded 50% new debt at 9% and 50% new shares at ₹120. New debt ₹575 crore, after-tax interest 575 × 9% × 0.75 = **₹38.8 crore**; new shares 575 / 120 = **4.79 crore**. Pro forma EPS before synergies = (600 + 54 − 38.8) / 104.79 = **₹5.87 (−2.2%, dilutive)**. Run-rate pre-tax synergies of ₹41.2 crore (after-tax 30.9) take EPS to **₹6.17 (+2.8%)**. The **break-even synergy** for zero change in EPS is about **₹18.1 crore pre-tax**. At the seller's ₹1,300 crore price, break-even rises to about ₹29.8 crore and, as shown in sub-topic 5, the deal destroys value even though EPS can still look accretive with full synergies (+1.3%): a classic trap.

### In the news
See news box. UP–NS is a cash-and-stock structure: management states pro forma debt to EBITDA of about 3.3x at closing and about $7 billion of free cash flow, the leverage and cash-generation metrics an interviewer would ask you to check. HDFC–HDFC Bank was all-stock, so no new acquisition debt at all; the question there is the exchange ratio (42 for 25, i.e. 1.68 bank shares per HDFC share).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The deal is EPS-accretive in year 1. Does that mean we should do it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Explains accretion mechanics (P/E comparison, earnings yield vs cost of debt)
> - Says accretion is not value creation; returns vs cost of capital decide
> - Computes or estimates the break-even synergy
> - Checks leverage, covenant and rating consequences

## 7. Due Diligence Workstreams
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Commercial, financial, operational, legal/tax, HR/culture, IT; confirm the thesis; price adjustments

### Definition
**Due diligence (DD)** tests the deal thesis and finds what could change the price or stop the deal. It is risk-prioritised: start from the thesis and the largest value drivers.

| Workstream | Core question | Key analyses and red flags |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial** | Is the market and the target's position what the plan says? | Market size and growth, customer interviews, concentration, churn, pricing power, competitor response, forecast reasonableness |
| **Financial** | Are the earnings and net debt real? | **Quality of earnings** (normalise EBITDA), working-capital seasonality and peg, net debt and debt-like items, cash conversion, off-balance-sheet items; see [[108 Financial Statements & Ratios]] |
| **Operational** | Can the target deliver and can it be integrated? | Capacity and utilisation, cost position, supply chain and supplier concentration, quality record, capex backlog, benchmarking; see [[026 Case Interview — Operations Cases]] |
| **Legal and regulatory** | Are there liabilities or approvals that block the deal? | Contracts (change-of-control clauses), litigation, licences, IP ownership, competition approval |
| **Tax** | Are there exposures or structuring benefits? | Disputed assessments, transfer pricing, carried-forward losses |
| **HR and culture** | Will key people stay and will the cultures work? | Key-person dependency, retention packages, pay parity, union issues, attrition |
| **IT and data** | Can systems be combined, and is data secure? | ERP landscape, technical debt, cyber incidents, data-privacy compliance |
| **ESG and reputation** | Are there hidden environmental, governance or safety liabilities? | Pollution, safety record, related-party transactions, promoter conduct |

DD outcomes: **price reduction**, **indemnities or escrow**, **conditions precedent**, **a changed integration plan**, or **walk away**. In Indian deals, also check promoter holdings and pledges, related-party dealings, and approvals (competition, sector regulators, and for listed targets, the takeover code and delisting rules).

### Example
Target reported EBITDA ₹120 crore at a 10x multiple. The quality-of-earnings review finds an **₹8 crore one-off insurance gain** and **₹6 crore of costs borne by a promoter-owned entity** that the buyer would have to bear: adjusted EBITDA = 120 − 8 − 6 = **₹106 crore**, so EV = 10 × 106 = ₹1,060 crore (₹140 crore lower). Debt-like items found: unfunded gratuity ₹12 crore and a disputed tax demand of ₹25 crore. Equity value = 1,060 − 200 − 12 − 25 = **₹823 crore**, **₹177 crore** below the original ₹1,000 crore. Each ₹1 of EBITDA found to be non-recurring costs ₹10 of price at a 10x multiple.

### In the news
See news box. In the HDFC merger the diligence emphasis was balance-sheet and regulatory (a mortgage lender folding into a bank brings reserve and priority-sector obligations to the larger loan book) rather than operational; in Tata–Iveco, the separation of the defence business was a condition precedent to closing, a good example of a DD-driven carve-out.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "You are leading due diligence on a target in the auto-components industry. What would you look at in the first two weeks?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Starts from the deal thesis and ranks workstreams by value at risk
> - Covers commercial, financial (quality of earnings, net debt), operational, legal and tax, HR, IT
> - Links findings to price, structure and walk-away
> - Mentions customer concentration, key people and change-of-control clauses

## 8. Risks, Deal-Breakers and Regulatory Approval
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Overpayment, synergy shortfall, regulation, culture, talent, funding; probability-weighted view

### Definition
Group risks so that the answer is MECE:

1. **Valuation risk:** overpaying; synergies below plan; forecasts too optimistic. Mitigate with sensitivity tables and earn-outs.
2. **Regulatory and political risk:** competition authority review, sector regulators, foreign-investment or national-security approvals. A long approval period is itself a cost (delay, uncertainty, key-staff attrition). In India, large combinations need **Competition Commission of India (CCI)** clearance; the 2023 amendment to the Competition Act is reported to have added a deal-value test (around ₹2,000 crore for deals with substantial Indian operations): confirm current thresholds before citing them.
3. **Execution and integration risk:** systems, supply chain, sales force, brand, and culture.
4. **Commercial risk:** customer defections, supplier reaction, competitor retaliation.
5. **People risk:** key-person loss, morale, retention costs.
6. **Financial and funding risk:** leverage, interest-rate, currency (for cross-border deals), covenant breach.
7. **Reputational and legal risk:** hidden liabilities, governance, ESG.

Rank by **probability × impact** and tie each to a **mitigation and an owner**. Stress-test the model: a standard sensitivity is synergy realisation (50%, 75%, 100%) against price (premium 10%, 20%, 30%). The cleanest recommendations specify **deal-breakers**: for example "walk away if diligence shows customer concentration above 40% in two accounts" or "if approval requires divesting more than a quarter of the combined capacity".

### Example
For the snacks case, net synergy value ₹223.5 crore assumed 90% cost and 50% revenue realisation. At 50% of cost synergies and zero revenue synergies the run-rate falls to 22.2 − 4.8 = ₹17.4 crore pre-tax, so net synergy value drops to roughly **₹74 crore** (same phasing, tax and discount rate as sub-topic 11): a **15% premium (₹150 crore) would then be value-destroying**. So the recommendation depends on a confirmatory diligence finding about distribution overlap: the one fact that decides the deal.

### In the news
See news box. UP–NS illustrates regulatory risk at the extreme: a 2027 target close reflects the Surface Transportation Board review; Tata–Iveco has seen repeated closing delays (an August 2026 report counts the fourth) while approvals and the defence separation were still pending, a reminder that timing risk is real.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What are the main reasons mergers fail, and how would you reduce them in this deal?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Structured risk categories, ranked by probability and impact
> - Names the specific risk for this deal rather than a generic list
> - Mitigations: price discipline, earn-out, early integration planning, retention packages
> - Clear deal-breakers and a recommendation conditional on diligence

## 9. Post-Merger Integration: Day 1 and the First 100 Days
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Integration management office; Day 1 readiness; 100-day plan; synergy tracking; retain people and customers

### Definition
Most value is made or lost in integration. A typical structure:

- **Pre-close (sign to close):** clean-team planning, integration management office (IMO) set-up, synergy baseline agreed, leadership appointments announced, regulatory-permitted preparation only (no gun-jumping before approval).
- **Day 1:** legal and statutory readiness, payroll and benefits continuity, one message to customers, suppliers and employees, systems that must work on day one (billing, order-to-cash), clear decision rights, and retention agreements signed for critical talent.
- **First 100 days:** confirm the target operating model and org structure, launch quick-win synergies (procurement, overhead), design the harder ones (plant, IT, sales force), decide the brand architecture, align KPIs and incentives, and start culture integration.
- **Months 4–24:** execute system migration, network and plant changes, report synergy delivery against the baseline monthly.

Governance: an **IMO** with a steering committee, functional workstreams, a **synergy tracker** with named owners and finance sign-off, and a risk log. Principles: protect the base business first (customers, key staff), then cost synergies, then revenue synergies. McKinsey's post-close research cites that acquirers with outperforming shareholder returns captured roughly half of their publicly committed synergies within the first 12 months, and that market verdicts become reliable only after about 18 months. Link to [[040 Risk & Stakeholder Management]] and [[170 Programme, Portfolio & PMO Management]] for the programme-management mechanics.

### Example
Snacks case integration plan: **Day 1:** same prices and sales terms for retailers, no change in the target's field team structure, retention bonuses for the 15 key managers. **Day 30:** procurement team starts joint negotiations on the top 10 raw materials (the ₹14.4 crore saving). **Day 100:** decide the shared-distribution model, which unlocks the ₹18 crore logistics saving, and the overhead structure (₹12 crore). **Tracking:** with run-rate synergies of ₹41.2 crore and the 40/80/100% phasing, year-1 delivery should be about ₹16.5 crore; below that, escalate.

### In the news
See news box. The Tata–Iveco release stresses "substantially no overlap" in footprints, so integration is likely to be about platforms and procurement scale rather than plant consolidation (my inference); in the HDFC merger CNBC noted that 70% of HDFC's customers had no HDFC Bank account, so cross-sell is the revenue-synergy prize.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The deal closes next month. What would you do in the first 100 days?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Sets up an IMO and synergy baseline before close
> - Day 1 priorities: continuity, communication, critical talent
> - Quick wins first, with named owners and a synergy tracker
> - Protects customers and people before chasing revenue synergies

## 10. Recent Deals as Case Examples
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ HDFC merger, Tata–Corus (a cautionary tale), Reliance–Disney, Tata–Iveco, UP–NS; what to say about each

### Definition
Use deals as one-line illustrations, not as story-telling; state the rationale, one number, and the lesson.

| Deal | Number to remember | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **HDFC Ltd–HDFC Bank (2023)** | $40 billion all-stock; 42 HDFC Bank shares per 25 HDFC; 5% of bank customers held a mortgage | Cross-sell and cheaper funding for a mortgage lender; regulatory-capital and integration scale |
| **Tata Steel–Corus (2007)** | 608 pence a share, £6.2 billion ($12 billion), debt-funded; the UK long-products business was later sold for a nominal £1 in 2016 | Auction winner's curse, leverage and cyclicality; the cautionary tale of paying for scale at the top of the cycle (Tata Steel's press release gives the price; the later history is from Founding Fuel's account) |
| **Reliance–Viacom18–Disney (2024)** | $8.5 billion (₹70,352 crore) post-money, excluding synergies; pro forma revenue about ₹26,000 crore | A merger of rivals to consolidate content, sports rights and two streaming platforms; shows the use of a JV when a full takeover is not feasible |
| **Tata Motors–Iveco (announced 2025)** | €3.8 billion equity value; €14.1 cash plus €5.5–6.0 expected dividend | Market-extension with a carve-out (defence); timing risk from approvals |
| **Union Pacific–Norfolk Southern (2025)** | $85 billion EV; $2.75 billion synergies; early-2027 target close | Regulatory approval as the key risk; end-to-end network synergies |

For an Indian interviewer, be ready to discuss one **inbound** (foreign firm buys Indian firm), one **outbound** (Indian firm buys abroad: Tata–Iveco, Tata–Corus), and one **domestic consolidation** (HDFC, Reliance–Disney). Pair each with the frame: rationale, price, synergies, integration, and outcome (known or pending).

### Example
**Mini-answer on Tata–Corus (60 seconds):** "In 2007 Tata Steel bought Corus at 608 pence a share, about £6.2 billion, financed largely with debt. The strategic logic was real: a European, high-value-added steelmaker complementing a low-cost Indian producer, and combined production of about 27 million tonnes. But the price reflected a competitive auction at a cyclical peak, and the leverage turned the 2008 downturn and weak European demand into a long drag, with the UK long-products business eventually sold for a nominal pound. The lesson: a good strategic rationale can still be a bad deal if the price and the funding structure leave no room for the cycle." (Verify any figure you add beyond the ones in the table.)

### Interview angle

> "Tell me about a recent acquisition you found interesting, and whether you think it will create value."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Names the deal, the rationale, one or two numbers, and a view
> - Applies the framework (fit, price, synergies, risk) rather than recounting the news
> - Notes what is still unknown (synergy delivery, approvals)
> - Avoids invented figures: says "roughly" and states the source

## 11. Full Worked Case: Buying a Regional Snacks Brand
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ End-to-end numbers: valuation, synergies, price, EPS, recommendation

### Definition
**Prompt (illustrative figures):** "Surya Foods, an Indian packaged-foods company with net profit ₹600 crore and 100 crore shares (price ₹120), is considering buying Annapurna Snacks, a regional brand. Should it, and at what price?"

**Data given on request:** Annapurna revenue ₹800 crore, EBITDA margin 15%, D&A ₹30 crore, net debt ₹200 crore at 9% interest, tax 25%; comparable deals at 10x EV/EBITDA.

**Solution path (each step feeds the next):**
1. **Standalone value:** EBITDA 800 × 15% = ₹120 crore; EV = 10 × 120 = ₹1,200 crore; equity = 1,200 − 200 = **₹1,000 crore**.
2. **Synergies (run-rate, pre-tax):** cost ₹44.4 crore, revenue ₹12 crore, dis-synergy −₹4.8 crore; risk-adjusted **₹41.2 crore**; phasing 40/80/100%; one-off cost ₹55 crore.
3. **Value of synergies:** WACC 11%, tax 25%, years 1–3 phased, then perpetuity with no growth: PV = ₹258.9 crore; PV of one-off costs after tax = ₹35.5 crore; **net ₹223.5 crore**.
4. **Walk-away price:** 1,000 + 223.5 = **₹1,223.5 crore** (22.3% premium).
5. **Bid:** open at 10%, settle at about 15% (₹1,150 crore, P/E 21.3x): pays away 67% of net synergy value; buyer NPV about **+₹73.5 crore**.
6. **EPS:** 50% debt (9%), 50% shares: EPS ₹6.00 → **₹5.87 before synergies, ₹6.17 at full synergies**; break-even synergies ₹18.1 crore.
7. **Risks and diligence priorities:** distribution overlap (the main synergy driver), customer concentration, quality-of-earnings adjustments (one-offs and related-party costs), key-person retention.

### Example
**Delivery of the recommendation (30 seconds):** "I recommend proceeding, but only up to about a 15% premium, ₹1,150 crore. The target is worth about ₹1,000 crore standalone; after risk-adjusting and tax, net synergies are worth about ₹220 crore, so our walk-away price is about ₹1,220 crore. At ₹1,150 crore we keep roughly ₹75 crore of value. EPS is about 2% dilutive before synergies and about 3% accretive at full run-rate synergies. The deal rests on the cost synergies from shared distribution, so my first diligence step is to validate the overlap and the target's quality of earnings; if the earnings are overstated by ₹14 crore, the equity value falls by about ₹177 crore and the deal no longer works at this price. Next steps are management meetings, a confirmatory diligence plan and a retention plan for key managers." Compare with [[025 Case Interview — Profitability]] to see how the target's margin story would be tested first.

### In the news
See news box. Compare the case to UP–NS: a 25% premium and $2.75 billion of synergies are the same logic at 100x scale: the question is always how much of the synergy is paid away.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Walk me through your recommendation, with numbers, in under a minute."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Recommendation first, then value, synergies, price, risk
> - Numbers consistent across steps (standalone → synergy → walk-away → bid)
> - One key uncertainty and the diligence that resolves it
> - Concrete next steps; see [[162 Structured Communication - SCQA, Storylines & Case Delivery]]

## 12. Interviewer-Style Questions and Model Answers
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Rapid-fire follow-ups; what a good answer sounds like

### Definition
Common M&A follow-ups and compact answers:

| Question | Model answer (short) |
|---|---|
| Why do most acquisitions fail to create value? | Overpayment (the winner's curse), overestimated revenue synergies, poor integration, culture and talent loss, and distraction. |
| Cost or revenue synergies: which to trust? | Cost: within management control and measurable. Revenue: depends on customers and competitors, so haircut 50% or more. |
| What is a control premium? | The excess over the unaffected price paid for control and synergies; often 20–40%. |
| What if the target is loss-making? | Value on EV/Sales or a DCF with a path to profit; the thesis is a turnaround, so check cash runway and see [[160 Case Interview - Cost Reduction, Turnaround & Pricing]]. |
| How would you finance it? | Match the funding to cash flow and leverage capacity: debt if net debt / EBITDA stays within covenant, stock if the buyer's P/E is high or leverage is tight. |
| What if the seller will not move on price? | Bridge with an earn-out, stock, or deferred payment; or walk away and pursue the next-best alternative. |
| How do you know the synergies are real? | A bottom-up plan with owners, benchmarked against comparable deals, and confirmed in DD. |
| Would you buy or partner? | Partner or JV if capability or market access is needed without the full risk; buy if control and speed matter. |
| What is the single biggest risk? | The one tied to the thesis: for a cost-synergy deal, that the overlap is smaller than assumed; for a growth deal, customer retention. |

Answer style: lead with the answer, give two reasons, state the number, and end with the implication ([[162 Structured Communication - SCQA, Storylines & Case Delivery]]).

### Example
**Question:** "Your synergy estimate is ₹41 crore. The CFO thinks it is too high. What do you do?" **Answer:** "I would split the estimate into its components: 44 crore of cost synergies, 12 crore of revenue synergies and −5 crore of dis-synergies. I would ask the CFO which line is in doubt. If it is the revenue synergy, dropping it entirely leaves about 35 crore a year (90% of the cost synergy, 40 crore, less 5), which is still worth about ₹186 crore net of one-off costs against a ₹150 crore premium, so the deal still works at 15%. If it is procurement or logistics, I would test the overlap with the buyer's own data and a benchmark. So my answer is robust to the revenue-synergy objection, less so to the logistics one."

### In the news
See news box. For each news-box deal, prepare the answer to "what is the one number that decides whether this deal works?" (synergy realisation for UP–NS, closing approvals for Tata–Iveco).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why do most acquisitions destroy value, and how is your plan different?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Names two or three evidence-based reasons (price, synergy over-estimation, integration)
> - Shows how the proposed plan counters each (price discipline, haircuts, IMO)
> - Is concise: answer, reasons, number, implication
> - Admits uncertainty and names the test that would resolve it

## 13. ⭐ Advanced: Leveraged Buyout (LBO) Quick Maths and Sell-Side Cases
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
A **leveraged buyout** acquires a company with a large share of debt, repaid from the company's cash flow; returns come from three levers: **EBITDA growth, multiple expansion, and debt paydown (deleveraging)**.

$$MoM=\frac{\text{Exit equity}}{\text{Entry equity}},\qquad IRR=MoM^{1/n}-1$$

where exit equity = exit EV − net debt at exit. **Value-creation bridge:** exit equity − entry equity = (ΔEBITDA × entry multiple) + (Δmultiple × exit EBITDA) + net debt paydown. In a private-equity case the interviewer tests whether the business has **stable cash flow to service debt**, an identifiable value-creation plan, and a credible exit (strategic sale, IPO, secondary buyout).

**Sell-side cases** ask the reverse: whether to sell, and to whom. Test the **standalone alternative** (what is the value if we hold and improve?), the **buyer universe** (strategic buyers who can pay for synergies versus financial buyers who pay for cash flow), and **how to prepare the asset** (carve out non-core, clean financials, vendor DD). A **divestiture** is justified when the business is worth more to someone else than to the owner (it is non-core, or its capital is better used elsewhere); the Tata Motors demerger and Iveco's separation of its defence business before sale are examples of simplifying a structure to unlock value.

### Example
PE firm buys a company with EBITDA ₹100 crore at 8x: EV = **₹800 crore**; funds 5x EBITDA with debt (₹500 crore) and **₹300 crore of equity**. After 5 years EBITDA is ₹140 crore, the exit multiple is 8x, and cash flow has repaid debt to ₹250 crore. Exit EV = 140 × 8 = ₹1,120 crore; exit equity = 1,120 − 250 = **₹870 crore**; **MoM = 2.9x**, **IRR = 2.9^(1/5) − 1 = 23.7%**. Bridge: EBITDA growth (140 − 100) × 8 = ₹320 crore + debt paydown ₹250 crore = ₹570 crore = 870 − 300 ✓. If the exit multiple falls to 7x: exit equity = 140 × 7 − 250 = ₹730 crore; MoM 2.43x; IRR 19.5%: the multiple compression costs about 4 points of IRR.

### In the news
See news box. A 2025 global M&A value of about $3.0 trillion with 39 megadeals shows a market in which strategic buyers and financial sponsors compete for the same assets; the sponsor's price discipline (multiple and leverage) is the interview-ready contrast with the strategic buyer's synergy-based logic.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "A PE firm wants to buy our client's subsidiary for ₹800 crore. Should the client sell?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Compares the offer with hold-and-improve and with other buyers
> - Considers who can pay for synergies versus who pays for cash flow
> - Names the LBO levers (EBITDA, multiple, deleveraging) and the sponsor's likely IRR target
> - Addresses separation costs, stranded costs, tax and transition services
