## 1. Answer-First Communication and the Pyramid Principle
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Governing thought first; 3 supports; each backed by data; top-down

### Definition
The **Pyramid Principle** (Minto) organises a message as a hierarchy: one **governing thought** (the answer) at the top, supported by 2–4 **key lines** (reasons), each supported by evidence. Three rules:

1. **Ideas at any level summarise the ideas below** (the answer is a summary of the reasons).
2. **Ideas in a group are of the same kind** (all reasons, all steps, all causes).
3. **Ideas in a group follow a logical order** (time, structure or importance).

Answer-first is the opposite of how you *discover* a problem (chronological) and of how most people *narrate* ("first I looked at X, then Y..."). The reader's question is "so what?"; the first sentence must answer it. For the case interview this is the **hypothesis-driven** style in [[024 Consulting Frameworks]] (sub-topic on structuring skills): state the answer, then test it.

$$\text{Message}=\underbrace{\text{Answer}}_{\text{1 sentence}}+\underbrace{\text{2–4 reasons}}_{\text{MECE}}+\underbrace{\text{evidence}}_{\text{numbers}}$$

Time budget: 30 seconds of speech is about 75 words at 150 words per minute, enough for an answer and three supports, not for the story of your analysis.

### Example
**Before (chronological):** "I looked at revenue first, then I went to cost, then I noticed materials had gone up, so I checked steel prices and they rose, and then I looked at the competitors..." **After (answer first):** "Profit fell mainly because steel costs rose about 8% while we could not pass it on. Two things confirm it: materials are 75% of the cost increase, and our price has been flat for 18 months. I recommend an index-linked price clause and a second steel source."

### In the news
See news box. Minto's description (ideas organised under a single point) is the same instruction the interviewer gives in different words: "What is your answer so far?"

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Can you summarise where you have got to?" or "What do you think is going on?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - The answer in the first sentence
> - Two to four MECE reasons, each with a number
> - A "so what" for the client and a next step
> - No chronological narration of the work

## 2. Grouping and Logical Order: MECE, Deductive and Inductive Arguments
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Same-kind groups; ordering logic; deductive vs inductive; vertical and horizontal logic

### Definition
Every group in the pyramid needs a reason for being together and for being in that order.

- **Grouping tests:** can every item in the group be given the same plural name ("three causes", "four options")? If not, regroup.
- **Ordering logic:** **time** (steps of a process), **structure** (parts of a whole: regions, segments, functions), **degree** (most to least important). Use degree for recommendations (biggest impact first).
- **Deductive argument:** a chain: rule, observation, conclusion ("Margins below 10% need action; ours is 8%; so we need action"). Use for diagnoses where each step depends on the one before.
- **Inductive argument:** parallel reasons that jointly support a conclusion ("We should enter because the market is growing, the margin is attractive, and we have a capability edge"). Use for recommendations.
- **Vertical logic:** each level answers "why?" or "how?" for the level above; **horizontal logic:** items at one level are MECE and parallel.

Check a structure by reading it top-down ("so what?" asks for the answer above) and bottom-up ("why do you say that?" asks for the evidence below). The MECE ideas also drive issue trees ([[024 Consulting Frameworks]]) and the profitability tree in [[025 Case Interview — Profitability]].

### Example
Weak group: "Reasons to cut the SKU range: low-volume items, handling complexity, our competitor has fewer SKUs, we need to invest in IT." (Items are of different kinds.) Fixed group, ordered by impact: "We should cut 30% of SKUs because (1) they generate 3% of sales but 12% of handling cost, (2) forecast accuracy on them is 30 points worse, (3) they tie up ₹18 crore of inventory." Three parallel reasons, each a number, ordered by size.

### In the news
See news box. Minto's rule (cited on Wikipedia) that you cannot derive an idea from a grouping unless the items are logically the same and in logical order is a one-line test for any case structure.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why did you structure it that way?" or "Are those buckets mutually exclusive?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Names the logic of the structure (process, parts, or importance)
> - Same-kind buckets, MECE, with a quick check for overlap
> - Distinguishes deductive diagnosis from inductive recommendation
> - Prioritises the biggest bucket first

## 3. SCQA: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Context everyone agrees on; the tension; the question it raises; the answer

### Definition
**SCQA** frames the introduction so the reader sees why the answer matters. Minto's own framework is **SCQ** (the answer then follows); SCQA adds the answer explicitly.

| Element | Role | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Stable, uncontroversial context the reader already accepts | Would the client nod? |
| **Complication** | What changed or the tension; why action is needed | Does it create urgency? |
| **Question** | The question this raises in the reader's mind | Is it the question the answer actually answers? |
| **Answer** | Your governing thought | One sentence, specific |

Variants: **start with the Answer** when the reader is senior and impatient (answer, then SCQ as backup); **use the Complication first** for a problem-driven audience. The SCQ is also the opening of the executive summary and the first slide of a storyline (sub-topics 4 and 5). Keep it short: 3–4 sentences, the Situation not longer than the Complication.

### Example
**Situation:** The client's plant makes 2,000 SKUs for 40 distributors. **Complication:** Inventory has grown from ₹300 crore to ₹425 crore in two years while service level is flat at 92% (illustrative figures). **Question:** How can the client cut inventory without lowering service? **Answer:** By segmenting SKUs into three classes and moving to class-specific stock policies and a monthly S&OP, which we estimate releases about ₹80 crore. Note the Question follows directly from the Complication; if the Question could be asked without the Complication, the SCQ is not doing work.

### In the news
See news box. Minto's site names the SCQ framework as central to finding the question in the reader's mind, which is the reason interview answers should not start with the analysis.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "You have one minute with the CEO in a lift. What do you say?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - A one-sentence Situation the CEO accepts, then the complication with a number
> - The question stated or implied clearly
> - The answer with the main number and the ask
> - Stops; does not narrate the analysis

## 4. Executive Summary Writing
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ One page; SCQA opening; answer, reasons, numbers, asks; test by reading only the first sentences

### Definition
An **executive summary** lets a busy reader act without reading the rest. Structure:

1. **SCQA opening** (3–4 lines).
2. **Recommendation** (the governing thought) with the headline number.
3. **2–4 supporting findings**, each starting with a bold sentence containing a number.
4. **Risks and what we need from the reader** (decision, resources, timing).

Style: active voice, specific numbers, no jargon, short paragraphs, **bold lead-ins** that can be read alone. Test: if someone reads only the first sentence of each paragraph, do they get the story? Put the **ask** first for a decision document. For emails use the same pattern: subject line with the answer, first sentence with the ask, three bullets, next step.

### Example
**Before:** "This document summarises the work done on inventory over the past eight weeks. We analysed data from SAP and interviewed 25 people. Many findings emerged, such as that inventory is high and that planning is manual..."

**After:** "**Recommendation: release about ₹82 crore of inventory by cutting days of inventory from 62 to 50, without lowering service.** Inventory has grown from ₹300 crore to ₹425 crore in two years on COGS of ₹2,500 crore (62 days) while service is flat at 92%. Three causes: (1) long-tail SKUs hold 35% of stock for 4% of sales; (2) safety stock is set by rule of thumb, not variability; (3) there is no monthly demand-supply review. Fixes: SKU segmentation, class-based policies and an S&OP cadence ([[120 Integrated Business Planning (IBP) & S&OP Maturity]]). Releasing 12 days of inventory frees 2,500 / 365 × 12 = **₹82 crore** of cash and about ₹15 crore a year at an 18% carrying cost. **Decision needed:** approve a three-month pilot in two plants."

### In the news
See news box. In a world where AI tools can summarise long documents instantly, a human-written executive summary is judged on whether it takes a position and states the ask.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Write (or say) the executive summary of your case in four sentences."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Recommendation and headline number in the first sentence
> - Two or three bolded, numbered findings
> - Risks and a clear ask or decision
> - Readable by its first sentences alone

## 5. Storyline and Ghost Deck
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Dot-dash storyline; ghost slides; hypotheses to prove; iterate with data

### Definition
A **storyline** is the answer and its supporting argument written as the sequence of slide titles *before* the analysis is done. A **dot-dash** storyline lists the main message (dot) and sub-messages (dashes). A **ghost deck** turns each title into a blank slide with a sketch of the chart or table that would prove it ("ghost" because no real data yet).

Why: it forces you to state **hypotheses**, guides the analysis plan (what to compute, which data to request), enables early feedback from the manager and client, and avoids "analysis for its own sake". Process: (1) write the answer, (2) write 3–5 key lines, (3) write supporting titles, (4) sketch the chart for each, (5) list the data needed, (6) collect data, replace ghosts with real charts, **rewrite titles when the data disagrees**. A good deck reads end to end through its titles alone.

### Example
Storyline for the inventory case: **Dot:** Inventory can be cut by ₹80 crore without hurting service. **Dash 1:** Inventory has risen 42% in two years while service is flat (ghost: line chart, inventory vs service level). **Dash 2:** The long tail drives the build-up (ghost: Pareto of SKUs by sales vs by stock). **Dash 3:** Policy and process gaps explain it (ghost: table of safety-stock rule vs variability). **Dash 4:** Class-based policies release ₹82 crore over 12 months (ghost: waterfall by class). **Dash 5:** A three-month pilot and S&OP cadence de-risk the roll-out (ghost: timeline). If the Pareto shows the tail is only 10% of stock, rewrite Dash 2 and the answer.

### In the news
See news box. AI tools make slide production cheaper, which raises the value of the storyline: the sequence of messages is the deliverable that the human has to own.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "You have two days to prepare a steering committee deck. How do you start?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Starts with the answer and a dot-dash storyline, not with data collection
> - Ghost slides drive the data requests
> - Rewrites hypotheses when data disagrees
> - Gets manager feedback on the storyline before building slides

## 6. Slide Action Titles
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Title = the message (full sentence with a number); one message per slide; read-through test

### Definition
An **action title** is a complete sentence stating the "so what" of the slide, normally two lines or less, with a number where possible. A **topic title** ("Inventory trend") only labels the content. Rules:

- One message per slide; the body proves the title.
- Contains a **verb** and a **number or comparison**.
- Consistent with neighbours: reading all titles gives the storyline.
- Avoid questions, labels and vague words ("overview", "analysis", "issues").
- Chart annotations (arrows, call-outs, highlighted bar) point at the evidence for the title.

### Example

| Topic title (weak) | Action title (strong) |
|---|---|
| Revenue by region | South drives the whole 10% revenue decline: -25% while other regions are flat |
| Inventory trend | Inventory has risen 42% in two years while service level stayed flat at 92% |
| Supplier analysis | Three suppliers hold 70% of spend but only one is under contract |
| Pricing options | A targeted 10% promotion to price-sensitive segments adds more profit than a blanket 10% cut |
| Next steps | A three-month pilot in two plants can de-risk the ₹82 crore release |

Check: revenue ₹500 crore to ₹450 crore is -10%, and South ₹200 crore to ₹150 crore is -25%, so South alone explains the whole ₹50 crore fall.

### In the news
See news box. The same principle underlies the "so what" that Minto's method requires at every level; slide titles are the visible form of it.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Here is a chart. What is the title of this slide?" (a frequent case or written-exercise question)

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - A full sentence with the insight and a number
> - Specific to the data in the chart, not generic
> - Reads in sequence with the other titles
> - Calls out what is surprising or actionable

## 7. Chart Choice
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Message first; comparison, trend, composition, distribution, relationship; avoid clutter

### Definition
Choose the chart from the **message**, not from the data format (see also [[029 Data Interpretation & Charts]], [[065 Data Visualization (Matplotlib-Seaborn-Plotly)]] and [[076 Charts, Dashboards & Form Controls]]):

| Message | Typical chart | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Compare categories | Horizontal or vertical bar | Sort by size; start at zero |
| Trend over time | Line (or column for few periods) | Annotate the turning point |
| Composition (parts of 100%) | Stacked 100% bar, or pie for 2–4 slices | Avoid many slices |
| Change bridge (what moved the total) | **Waterfall** | Good for profit and cost bridges |
| Ranking and cumulative share | **Pareto** (bars plus line) | 80/20 messages |
| Relationship of two variables | Scatter | Add a trend line; label outliers |
| Distribution | Histogram or box plot | Shows spread, not only average |
| Flow or process | Flow diagram, Sankey | Use sparingly |
| Many numbers, exact values | Table | Highlight the key row |
| Two-dimension prioritisation | 2×2 matrix | Axes must be labelled with criteria |

Rules: one message per chart; remove gridlines and clutter; label directly; use a consistent colour for the same item; show units and source; never truncate a bar axis to exaggerate; one highlight colour for the point of the slide ([[047 MIS & Dashboard Design]]).

### Example
Quarterly sales (₹ crore): 310, 340, 355, 420 for Q1–Q4. The message "Q4 jumped 18%" (420 / 355 − 1 = 18.3%; the earlier quarters grew 9.7% and 4.4%) is a **line or column chart with Q4 highlighted**; the message "Q4 growth came from two customers" needs a **stacked bar or waterfall** by customer; a pie of the four quarters is the wrong chart because quarters are not parts of a whole of interest.

### In the news
See news box. Lilli-type tools can generate charts quickly; the human task is to choose the chart that makes the message undeniable and to check the underlying numbers.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Which chart would you use to show the client why profit fell from ₹100 crore to ₹90 crore?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Chooses by message (bridge or waterfall for a profit change)
> - Names the key design choices (sorted, labelled, highlighted)
> - Warns about misleading axes and clutter
> - Offers an alternative for a different message

## 8. Synthesising Case Results in 30 Seconds
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Recommendation, 2–3 reasons with numbers, risks, next steps; no replay of analysis

### Definition
When the interviewer says "Summarise" or "What is your recommendation?", use this template (about 75 words):

1. **Recommendation** (one sentence, specific).
2. **Reasons** (two or three, each with a number).
3. **Risks** (one or two, with mitigation).
4. **Next steps** (one or two actions).

Rules: do not restate the prompt; do not re-narrate the analysis; do not introduce new analysis; use the exact numbers you derived; stop after the next steps. Mid-case "mini-synthesis" after each analysis block ("So the problem is in the South; I will now test whether it is price or volume") keeps the interviewer with you. Final synthesis for cost cases: see [[160 Case Interview - Cost Reduction, Turnaround & Pricing]]; for M&A: [[159 Case Interview - M&A & Due Diligence]].

### Example
**Before (rambling, 60+ seconds):** "So we looked at profit and revenue was down and cost was also up and then we found that in the South there was an issue and I think maybe a competitor entered but I am not sure, so overall I think it could be a pricing issue or a volume issue..."

**After (30 seconds):** "I recommend recovering the South region's volume rather than cutting costs. Revenue is down 10% from ₹500 crore to ₹450 crore, and the entire ₹50 crore decline is in the South, where revenue fell 25%, because volume dropped 30% after a competitor's entry while our price rose 7%. The main risk is that matching price erodes margin, so I would target the lost distributors first and test a limited promotion. Next, I would validate the competitor's footprint and run a two-month pilot in two districts."

### In the news
See news box. Both the interview and the AI-assisted workplace reward the same skill: converting many findings into a short, decision-ready message.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The CEO walks in. You have 30 seconds. What is your recommendation?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Recommendation first, then reasons with numbers, then risk and next step
> - Exactly the numbers from the analysis, rounded sensibly
> - Under 45 seconds, ends cleanly
> - No new analysis and no apologies

## 9. Saying Numbers Well
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Round; compare; one number per sentence; units; source; sanity check

### Definition
Spoken numbers must be few, rounded and meaningful.
- **Round** intelligently: 4,382 of 17,950 is "about a quarter" (24.4%), not "24.41%".
- **Compare**: a number needs a benchmark (last year, peer, target): "inventory 62 days against a peer median of 45".
- **One number per sentence**, with the unit and period (₹ crore, per year, per unit).
- **Signpost** before a calculation: "I will do a quick calculation: revenue times margin."
- **Sanity-check** every result against intuition; say it aloud ("20 crore on 500 crore revenue is 4%: plausible").
- **Percentage vs percentage points**: margin up from 10% to 12% is 2 points (20% relative increase).
- **Write the calculation** on paper in a visible way; recompute if a result looks odd; state corrections openly.

Link: [[029 Data Interpretation & Charts]] and the arithmetic shortcuts in [[028 Guesstimates & Market Sizing]].

### Example
Weak: "The margin is 0.1264 and the growth is 14.236 percent." Strong: "Margin is about 13%, up roughly 2 points on last year, which is about 1.5 points above the peer median." After deriving 24.4% say, "about a quarter, so the top two customers are roughly half of the portfolio". If you realise a slip: "Correction: I divided by the wrong base; the correct figure is 30%, which strengthens my point."

### In the news
See news box. The interview guidance to ask the interviewer to repeat unclear questions applies to numbers too: confirm the data point before using it.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Walk me through your calculation." or "Does that number seem reasonable to you?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Round, compare, and sanity-check
> - States units and the base for percentages
> - Corrects errors openly and calmly
> - Connects the number to the decision

## 10. Handling Pushback, Challenge and Silence
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Listen, restate, test, update or hold; signposted pauses; never freeze

### Definition
Interviewers (and clients) challenge to see how you reason under pressure.

**Pushback ("Are you sure?"):**
1. **Listen and restate** the challenge in one sentence.
2. **Test it:** is it a new fact, a logic gap or a preference?
3. **Update openly** if the point is right ("That changes my view; I will revise the estimate to...").
4. **Hold your position** with evidence if it is not ("I understand the concern; the data shows X, so I would still recommend Y, and I would test it by...").
5. Stay curious, not defensive.

**Silence:** use it. Say "Let me take 30 seconds to structure my thoughts" and then write the structure. A signposted pause reads as control; silent staring reads as panic. If stuck, return to the question, state what you know and what you need, or propose a way forward ("I would start with the biggest cost line").

**Hints:** treat as a gift; acknowledge and use them. **Interruptions:** answer the question asked, then offer to return to your point. Practise with a partner who challenges and with feedback as in [[161 Consulting Firms & Recruitment Landscape in India]].

### Example
Interviewer: "I do not think the South is the problem; competitors' prices are the same." Candidate: "Fair challenge. If competitor prices are equal, price is not the driver. Then the 30% volume loss must come from distribution or availability. That makes the next question: did we lose distributors or stock-outs? Can I look at the distributor count by region?" The candidate updated the hypothesis, kept structure and asked for data.

### In the news
See news box. McKinsey's guidance to ask the interviewer to repeat unclear questions legitimises clarification as part of the process; Bain's reported scripted behavioural interview evaluates listening and empathy, which includes how you take a challenge.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "I do not agree with your recommendation. Convince me." (or a long silence after your answer)

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Restates and tests the challenge, not defence reflex
> - Updates when the point is valid; holds when evidence supports
> - Signposted pauses; never freezes
> - Returns to the structure and the decision

## 11. Whiteboard Hygiene and Note Structure
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Structure page; numbered sections; boxes for key numbers; calculation area; legible

### Definition
Your paper is your working memory and part of what the interviewer sees (or can ask for).

- **Page 1 (structure):** the issue tree or framework drawn once, large, with numbered branches; keep it visible and tick off branches as you go.
- **Page 2+ (data):** one block per analysis with a heading, the data in a small table, and the "so what" boxed.
- **Calculation area:** one corner for arithmetic, always with units; keep formulas written out ("Revenue = price × volume").
- **Key numbers** (target, baseline) in a margin box, so you do not re-ask.
- **Symbols:** consistent abbreviations; arrows for causation; a star for insights; a question mark for open items.
- **Legibility:** large handwriting; space between items; no scribbles over earlier work; one case per page set.
- **Physical whiteboard:** write the structure on the left third, calculations in the middle, the recommendation box on the right; stand to the side, face the interviewer; do not talk to the board.
- **Using it live:** read the structure from the page rather than from memory; show a tree rather than a list.

### Example
Layout for a cost case: top of page 1, "Goal: ₹50 crore saving in 2 years"; left, a tree (Materials, Labour, Energy, Logistics, Overheads, SG&A with ₹ and % of revenue); right, a box "Gap to benchmark = ₹100 crore; capture 60% = ₹60 crore". On page 2, the lever list with impact and ease scores. At the end you can read your recommendation from the final box, with numbers consistent with the earlier pages.

### In the news
See news box. In virtual cases the same discipline applies to a shared document or paper on camera; McKinsey's guidance to keep cameras on means your notes and structure may be visible.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Can you share your notes or structure?" or "Walk me through what you have written."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - A visible, numbered structure and a boxed summary of key numbers
> - Neat calculations with units
> - Uses the page to drive the conversation
> - No chaotic scribbles or missing labels

## 12. Virtual Case Delivery
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Audio and camera; screen sharing; pacing; verbal signposting; avoid AI tools; recover from glitches

### Definition
Virtual cases remove cues (body language, pauses) and add technology risk.

- **Setup:** quiet, private space; stable network and a backup (mobile hotspot); camera at eye level, face lit; headphones; notebook and pens on desk; pre-test the platform; keep a glass of water.
- **Rules from the firm:** McKinsey's page tells virtual candidates to keep the camera on, disable AI software and ask the interviewer to repeat unclear questions; follow the specific instructions you are given on tool use and screen sharing.
- **Verbal signposting:** more than in person ("First, my structure: three buckets; let me pause for 20 seconds to write them"), because the interviewer cannot see you thinking.
- **Showing work:** hold up your page to the camera or screen-share a document if allowed; read your structure aloud; label numbers verbally.
- **Pacing:** slightly slower, clear, deliberate; check in ("Does this make sense?") at natural breaks, not constantly.
- **Eye contact:** look at the camera when making key points.
- **Technical glitches:** state the problem, repeat the last point, continue; do not apologise at length.
- **Energy:** a bit higher than in person: smile, vary tone.

### Example
Opening a virtual case: "Thanks. Before I structure, can I confirm the objective is to return margin to 12% within two years? ... Great. I will take 30 seconds to organise my thoughts." Then, camera-aware: "I will share my structure: first, the margin gap to the target; second, cost by line; third, levers and risks." When the connection drops: "I lost you after 'revenue by region'; shall I repeat my last point?"

### In the news
See news box. McKinsey's own advice for virtual interviews (quiet, camera on, no AI software, ask for repetition) and Bain's reported interviewer-led format mean that you follow the interviewer's lead and signpost carefully.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you adapt your case approach for a virtual interview?" (a mentor or mock-interview prompt)

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Practical setup and a backup plan
> - More signposting and verbal clarity than in person
> - Compliance with the firm's stated rules
> - Calm recovery from technical issues

## 13. ⭐ Advanced: Structured Communication Beyond Consulting (PM, Operations, Project Roles)
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
The same techniques apply across the roles you are targeting:

- **Product management:** product-sense answers, PRDs and roadmap reviews are answer-first documents ([[163 PM Interview Types & Answer Frameworks]], [[167 PRDs, Stakeholder Management & Product Operations]]).
- **Operations and supply chain:** weekly business reviews, S&OP packs and escalation notes lead with the decision and the exposure ([[120 Integrated Business Planning (IBP) & S&OP Maturity]], [[012 Supply Chain Analytics & KPIs]]).
- **Project management:** status reports use RAG with the ask first, then the evidence ([[040 Risk & Stakeholder Management]], [[170 Programme, Portfolio & PMO Management]]).
- **Behavioural rounds:** the STAR structure is a pyramid for a story: result first, then situation, task and action ([[049 STAR Stories — Leadership & Conflict]], [[048 Tell Me About Yourself]]).
- **Email and chat:** BLUF (bottom line up front): a subject line with the decision, the ask in the first sentence, a short list of reasons, a deadline.
- **Escalation (SBAR-style):** Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation: a close cousin of SCQA for incident communication.

Principle: **say what you want the reader to do, why, and by when, in the first three lines.**

### Example
Operations escalation, before: "We have had some issues with the supplier of component X..." After: "**Decision needed by Friday: approve air freight of 400 units (₹6 lakh) to avoid a line stoppage on 14th, which would cost about ₹40 lakh a day.** The supplier's shipment is 9 days late; safety stock covers 5 days; air freight closes the gap. Alternative: slow the line from Monday (costing about ₹12 lakh a day). Recommendation: air freight." Reasons, alternative and recommendation in under 70 words.

### In the news
See news box. As AI drafts routine reports, a manager's value is in the decision framing; the same Minto structure applies to PRDs, status reports and escalation notes.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Tell me about a time you had to explain a complex problem to a senior stakeholder."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Led with the decision or recommendation, not the background
> - Tailored the message and depth to the audience
> - Used numbers and a clear ask, with a deadline
> - The outcome and what you would do differently
