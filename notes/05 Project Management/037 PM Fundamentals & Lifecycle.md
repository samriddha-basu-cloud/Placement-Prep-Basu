## 1. Project Definition
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Temporary endeavor; unique output; progressive elaboration

### Definition
PMI defines a **project** as *a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service or result*.
- **Temporary:** definite start and end (the end is reached when objectives are met, funding stops, or the project is cancelled); the *result* may last for decades.
- **Unique:** the deliverable differs in some distinguishing way even if similar ones exist (two bridges are never identical).
- **Progressive elaboration:** details are refined as information becomes available (a rough estimate at initiation becomes a definitive plan).
- Projects drive **change** and deliver **business value**; they are authorised because of a market demand, strategic opportunity, customer request, technology advance or legal requirement.

**Not projects:** ongoing operations (running a plant daily, monthly payroll) are repetitive and continuous. Characteristics to remember: defined objectives, constraints (scope, time, cost, quality, resources, risk), cross-functional team, uncertainty.

### Example
Building the Navi Mumbai airport terminal is a project (temporary, unique, defined scope), whereas running the airport after opening is operations. A bank migrating to a new core-banking system is a project; processing the daily transactions on it is operations.

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai Airport shows the "temporary" end is only the opening (8 Oct 2025 inauguration, then commercial start); the *project* ended, the operation began, and the project took from 1997 to 2025 to deliver Phase 1.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What is a project and how is it different from operations?" or "Is launching a monthly report a project?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - PMI definition in one sentence with temporary, unique, progressive elaboration
> - Contrast with operations (ongoing, repetitive)
> - An example from the student's domain (supply chain network redesign, ERP rollout)
> - Note that temporary does not mean short, and outputs can be permanent

## 2. Project vs Program vs Portfolio
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Scope hierarchy; benefits realization

### Definition
| Level | Definition | Focus | Success measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Project** | Temporary effort for a unique deliverable | Deliver outputs on time, cost and scope | On-time, on-budget, quality |
| **Program** | Group of related projects managed together to obtain benefits not available individually | Coordination, interdependencies, **benefits realisation** | Benefits delivered |
| **Portfolio** | Collection of projects/programs/operations aligned to strategy | Selection, prioritisation, resource allocation, risk balance | Strategic alignment, ROI, value |

Hierarchy: Portfolio contains programs and projects; programs contain projects (and subprograms). **Portfolio managers** decide *which* work to do; **program managers** ensure related projects deliver integrated benefits; **project managers** deliver the specific output. A **PMO** (Project Management Office) standardises governance, templates and reporting. Governance rises with level: portfolio review boards decide to start, continue, pause or kill.

### Example
An FMCG firm's portfolio: "Digital Transformation" program = ERP rollout project + e-commerce platform project + data-warehouse project; separate portfolio items: new-plant construction, brand relaunch. A portfolio review might defer the brand relaunch to free scarce engineering and finance capacity for the program.

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai Airport is a megaproject whose benefits (capacity relief for Mumbai, with the PM noting the city now has more than one international airport, and regional trade and employment) depend on connected road, rail and operator readiness outside the airport boundary: a programme-level view, not a single-project one.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Explain the difference between a program and a portfolio with an example."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Three-level hierarchy with the "benefits" and "strategy" distinctions
> - Who manages each and what each decides
> - Realistic example from a company
> - Role of PMO and governance

## 3. Project Lifecycle Phases
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Initiating → Planning → Executing → Monitoring → Closing

### Definition
PMI's five **process groups**, often used as phases:
1. **Initiating:** define the project, get authorisation (charter), identify stakeholders.
2. **Planning:** scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, risk, communications, procurement plans; baselines.
3. **Executing:** do the work, manage the team, procure, communicate.
4. **Monitoring & Controlling:** track performance vs baseline, manage changes, take corrective action (runs *throughout*).
5. **Closing:** formal acceptance, handover, release resources, lessons learned.

Resources and cost follow an **S-curve** over time; **ability to influence** scope and cost is highest early while the **cost of changes** rises later. Do not confuse the *process groups* (what you do) with the *development life cycle* (predictive/waterfall, iterative, incremental, agile, hybrid: how the product is built). In PMBOK 8 the groups reappear as "focus areas".

### Example
A warehouse automation project: Initiating (business case, charter, sponsor approval), Planning (vendor selection, schedule, budget baseline ₹12 crore), Executing (install conveyors and WMS), Monitoring (weekly earned-value review), Closing (acceptance test, SOP handover, lessons learned).

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai Airport's timeline: groundbreaking in Feb 2018, but actual construction only started in Aug 2021, showing that a long gap between "initiating" and genuine "executing" can arise from unresolved planning-stage issues like land and developer change.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Walk me through the project lifecycle and where most failures originate."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Five groups with the key output of each
> - Monitoring and controlling as continuous, overlapping
> - Difference between process groups and development life cycle (predictive vs agile)
> - Most failures: weak initiation or planning (unclear scope, unrealistic estimates)

## 4. Project Charter
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Authorization document; objectives, scope, sponsor, PM authority

### Definition
The **project charter** is the document issued by the sponsor or initiator that **formally authorises** the project and gives the project manager authority to apply organisational resources. It is created in Initiating (PMBOK: "Develop Project Charter"), from the **business case** and **benefits management plan**.

**Typical contents:** project purpose and justification, measurable objectives and success criteria, high-level requirements and scope, key deliverables, summary milestone schedule, summary budget, high-level risks, assumptions and constraints, key stakeholders, approval requirements, **named PM and their authority level**, and sponsor signature.

**Charter vs project plan:** the charter is short (1–3 pages), high-level and stable; the plan is detailed and evolves. The **project scope statement** comes later in planning. A charter prevents ambiguity about *who owns what* and gives a reference to defend against scope creep.

### Example
Charter for "Reduce order-to-delivery lead time": Purpose: lift on-time delivery from 82% to 95% in 9 months; budget ₹3 crore; milestones: design (month 2), pilot in Pune DC (month 5), rollout (month 9); PM: named manager with authority to approve spend up to ₹10 lakh; sponsor: COO.

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai Airport changed developer hands (GVK bid in 2017, Adani in 2021): a reminder that the charter's authority, sponsor and funding assumptions must be reconfirmed when ownership changes.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What does a project charter contain and why is it important?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Authorisation + authority of PM as the central purposes
> - Key contents (objectives, scope, milestones, budget, risks, stakeholders)
> - Who issues it (sponsor, outside the PM's control) vs who prepares it (often PM)
> - Charter vs plan vs scope statement

## 5. WBS (Work Breakdown Structure)
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Deliverable-oriented decomposition; 100% rule

### Definition
The **WBS** is a hierarchical, **deliverable-oriented decomposition** of the total scope into smaller, manageable components down to **work packages**, the lowest level, where cost and time are estimated and responsibility assigned.

**Rules:**
- **100% rule:** the WBS includes all of the work (and only that work) defined in the scope; the child elements sum to 100% of the parent; this applies at every level.
- Elements are **nouns (deliverables)**, not verbs; mutually exclusive (no overlap).
- Work packages sit within a common-sense size (rule of thumb: 8/80: 8–80 hours).
- **WBS dictionary** documents each element (description, owner, acceptance criteria, cost).
- Foundation for the schedule (activities), cost estimates, resource assignments and risk identification. **Control accounts** link WBS with the cost accounts (EVM).

Formats: outline, tree/organisation chart, or sheet. Decomposition can be by deliverable, phase or sub-project.

### Example
WBS for a warehouse automation project (Level 1 → 2): 1 Warehouse Automation; 1.1 Design (1.1.1 Layout, 1.1.2 Integration spec); 1.2 Equipment (1.2.1 Conveyors, 1.2.2 Sorters); 1.3 Software (1.3.1 WMS configuration, 1.3.2 Interfaces to ERP); 1.4 Installation; 1.5 Testing and Training; 1.6 Project Management. Check: 1.1 to 1.6 together represent 100% of the project scope; if "Customer training" appears nowhere, the 100% rule is broken.

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai Airport scope included land acquisition and resettlement of 2,786 households; a WBS that treats such enabling work as a first-class deliverable (not an assumption) exposes critical-path risk early.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you build a WBS for [event/ERP/plant]?" or "What is the 100% rule?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Deliverable-based decomposition to work packages
> - 100% rule, no overlap, nouns not verbs
> - WBS dictionary, and how it feeds schedule, cost and risk
> - Include project management and enabling work, not only the "product"

## 6. Scope Creep
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Uncontrolled expansion; prevention via change control

### Definition
**Scope creep** is the **uncontrolled** addition of features or work without corresponding adjustments to time, cost or resources. It is distinct from approved **changes** and from **gold plating** (the team adds extras nobody asked for). Causes: vague requirements, no baseline, weak sponsor or change control, stakeholder pressure, unclear acceptance criteria, poor communication.

**Prevention and control:**
- Clear, signed **scope statement** and **WBS** baseline (scope baseline).
- Formal **Integrated Change Control:** change request → impact analysis (scope, schedule, cost, risk, quality) → **Change Control Board (CCB)** decision → update baselines.
- Requirements traceability matrix; sign-off at gates; "yes, and here is the impact" culture.
- Reserve for approved changes; manage expectations via regular reviews.
- **Agile** handles change through backlog reprioritisation within fixed time-boxes.

### Example
A client asks the team "also to add 5 more reports" mid-project. Impact analysis: 25 extra person-days at ₹10,000/day = ₹2.5 lakh and 2 weeks delay. The sponsor can approve the funding and date shift through the CCB, or defer the reports to phase 2. Doing it silently is scope creep.

### In the news
See news box. Large infrastructure projects like Navi Mumbai are exposed to creep and delay from the interface of design, land and finance; Wikipedia shows total estimates in the ₹167 billion range for the full project, with disclosure that cost figures differ across sources, which itself shows why a controlled baseline matters.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The client keeps adding requirements. What do you do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Acknowledge the request, then assess impact on triple constraint
> - Use formal change control and sponsor/CCB approval
> - Offer options: trade off scope, add budget/time, defer
> - Prevention: baseline, acceptance criteria, stakeholder communication

## 7. Triple Constraint (Iron Triangle)
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Scope, Time, Cost — balance and trade-offs

### Definition
The **triple constraint** says scope, time and cost are interdependent: changing one affects at least one other, with **quality** (and customer satisfaction) at the centre. A change in scope usually needs more time and/or cost; compressing time without reducing scope increases cost or risk; cutting cost can cut scope or quality.

PMBOK 6 expanded to **six constraints**: scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources and risk. PMBOK 7 and 8 stress **value delivery** over the triangle, since a project can meet all three and still fail to deliver benefits. Practical priority approach: ask the sponsor to rank which constraint is **fixed**, which **flexible**, which **accepted** to move. In agile, time and cost are fixed per sprint and **scope flexes**.

Compression levers: crashing, fast tracking, reducing scope, quality trade-offs (see [[039 Scheduling Tools (CPM-PERT-Gantt)]]).

### Example
A project has ₹10 crore budget and 12 months. The sponsor demands delivery in 9 months. Options: add resources (cost up, say +₹1.2 crore, via crashing), fast-track (risk up), cut non-critical scope (scope down). The PM presents the three options with impacts and lets the sponsor choose.

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai Airport started with a mid-2020 target and opened in Oct 2025: the schedule constraint slipped, and the cost estimates have been reported at different levels; opening in phases (Phase 1 with 20 million passengers a year) is a scope-for-time trade.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "You are asked to deliver faster without more money. What do you say?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - The triangle with quality at centre
> - Show trade-offs quantitatively, not "impossible"
> - Ask which constraint is fixed, which flexible
> - Mention value and risk beyond the triangle

## 8. Stakeholder Analysis
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Power-interest grid; engagement strategy per quadrant

### Definition
A **stakeholder** is anyone who can affect or be affected by the project. Analysis steps: **identify** (sponsor, customers, users, team, vendors, regulators, community), **assess** (power, interest, influence, attitude) and **plan engagement**.

**Power-interest grid:**
| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | **Keep satisfied** | **Manage closely** |
| **Low power** | **Monitor** | **Keep informed** |

Other tools: Power-influence grid, **salience model** (power, legitimacy, urgency), **stakeholder engagement assessment matrix** (unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading: current vs desired), RACI. Document in a **stakeholder register** and an engagement plan with communication frequency and owner. Re-assess as the project evolves.

### Example
ERP rollout: CFO (high power, high interest) = manage closely; regulators/auditors (high power, low interest) = keep satisfied with compliance updates; floor supervisors (low power, high interest, but crucial for adoption) = keep informed with training; vendor's account staff = monitor.

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai needed resettlement of 2,786 households across ten villages, and Wikipedia records local opposition and protests: stakeholders with low formal power can still delay a project, so analysis must include affected communities.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you deal with a powerful stakeholder who opposes the project?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Identify and map using the power-interest grid
> - Understand motives, tailor engagement and communication
> - Use the sponsor for escalation; seek win-win
> - Track engagement over time

## 9. Project Governance
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Steering committee; escalation path; decision rights

### Definition
**Governance** is the framework of authority, decision-making and accountability by which a project is directed, aligned to organisational strategy. Elements:
- **Steering committee / Project board:** senior stakeholders who approve scope changes, budget, stage gates and resolve escalations.
- **Roles and decision rights:** sponsor, PM, PMO, CCB, product owner; clear approval thresholds (e.g. PM approves up to ₹10 lakh, steering committee above).
- **Escalation path:** issue → PM → sponsor → steering committee, with time limits.
- **Stage gates / phase reviews:** go/no-go decisions against criteria.
- **Reporting and assurance:** status reports, dashboards, audits, independent reviews.
- **Policies:** change control, risk appetite, compliance.

Good governance is proportionate: too heavy slows delivery, too light lets projects drift. PMBOK 8 elevates "governance" to the first performance domain.

### Example
A ₹50 crore plant expansion: monthly steering committee (CEO, CFO, COO, PM); stage gates after design, procurement and commissioning; changes above 2% of budget need committee approval; critical issues unresolved for 5 days escalate to the sponsor.

### In the news
See news box. A change of developer and prolonged timeline at Navi Mumbai Airport (GVK to Adani, 2017 to 2021) is a governance event; PMBOK 8 makes governance a performance domain, reflecting its importance.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you set up governance for a multi-vendor transformation programme?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Steering committee, roles, decision rights
> - Escalation ladder with timeframes
> - Stage gates and KPIs
> - Proportionate governance and a single source of truth for status

## 10. Lessons Learned
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Knowledge capture; retrospective; organizational learning

### Definition
**Lessons learned** are knowledge gained from the project experience (what worked, what didn't, why) captured in a **lessons-learned register** and shared into **organisational process assets**. PMI says they should be captured **throughout** the project, not just at the end.

**Methods:** retrospectives (agile: what went well, what to improve, actions), after-action reviews (what was supposed to happen, what happened, why, what next), post-implementation reviews, interviews, surveys, workshops. Good lessons are specific, actionable, with a root cause and recommendation, and **assigned to someone** to embed in templates, checklists and estimates. Barriers: blame culture, no time, no repository, no follow-up. A **blameless post-mortem** culture improves honesty.

### Example
After an ERP go-live slipped three weeks: root cause = data migration cleansing started late. Lesson: begin data cleansing in month 2; action: add a cleansing milestone and 20% migration contingency to the organisation's project template; owner: PMO.

### In the news
See news box. After AWS's Oct 2025 incident, AWS published a post-event summary and announced corrective actions (disable the automation, add protections); that is the lessons-learned loop at industrial scale. Related: [[035 Technical Understanding (APIs, SDLC)]].

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Tell me about a project that failed and what you learned" (behavioural) or "How do you ensure lessons are actually applied?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Capture continuously, not only at closing
> - Specific root cause, action, owner
> - Update templates and checklists so learning is institutional
> - For behavioural questions use STAR with your own real example: [Situation], [Task], [Action], [Result], [What I changed afterwards]

## 11. Project Closure
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Acceptance criteria, handover, documentation, celebration

### Definition
Closing is the process group that formally **finalises** all activities. PMBOK's "Close Project or Phase" checks that the work is complete and the **acceptance criteria** are met.

**Closure checklist:**
- **Product/administrative closure:** deliverables formally accepted (validate scope) by the customer; sign-off.
- **Handover** to operations with training, SOPs, warranty and support arrangements.
- **Contracts closed** (procurement closure, final payments, vendor evaluation), claims settled.
- **Documentation archived**; **final report**; financial closure and benefits-realisation handover to the business owner.
- **Release resources**; **lessons learned**; **recognise and celebrate** the team.
- **Premature closure** (cancelled): document status and reasons.

Success is assessed against objectives and the business case, and a **post-project benefits review** (3–12 months later) checks whether benefits materialised.

### Example
Plant commissioning project: performance test shows 98% availability vs the 95% acceptance criterion → customer sign-off; operations team trained, spare parts and warranty terms handed over; retention payment released after 12 months; closing meeting and team recognition.

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai Airport's inauguration on 8 Oct 2025 preceded commercial operations (reported for Dec 2025): inauguration is a milestone, but closure requires operational readiness and handover to the airport operator.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you close a project? What if the client refuses to sign off?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Acceptance criteria agreed up front and formal sign-off
> - Handover, documentation, contract and financial closure
> - Lessons learned and team release/celebration
> - If sign-off is refused: check criteria, resolve gaps, escalate with documentation

## 12. PMBOK 7th Edition
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Tracker hint:_ Principles and performance domains vs process groups

### Definition
The **PMBOK Guide, 7th edition (2021)** shifted from a process-based standard (6th ed.: 5 process groups, 10 knowledge areas, 49 processes) to a **principles-based, outcome-focused** framework for predictive, agile and hybrid delivery.
- **12 principles:** stewardship, team, stakeholders, value, systems thinking, leadership, tailoring, quality, complexity, risk, adaptability and resilience, change.
- **8 performance domains:** Stakeholder, Team, Development Approach and Life Cycle, Planning, Project Work, Delivery, Measurement, Uncertainty.
- Plus **tailoring**, models/methods/artefacts (e.g. WBS, EVM) in an online *PMIstandards+* platform, and the *Standard for Project Management* split from the Guide.

**PMBOK 8 (Nov 2025, per the news box)** consolidates to six principles and seven performance domains and reintroduces the process groups as focus areas (with 40 processes). For interviews, know that **PMP exam content** blends predictive, agile and hybrid (about 50% agile/hybrid in recent exams).

### Example
Under the 6th edition a project manager "does Plan Risk Management". Under the 7th, the manager asks "are we applying the Uncertainty domain and the principle of risk responsiveness for this context?" and tailors the approach: a regulatory plant build uses a predictive approach, a mobile-app MVP an agile one.

### In the news
See news box for PMBOK 8 (Nov 2025). Because the 7th and 8th editions differ, check which edition a PMP course or exam relies on; a revised exam is reported to start from 9 Jul 2026 (secondary source).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What changed in PMBOK 7?" or "Do knowledge areas still matter?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Process-based (6th) to principle-based (7th), with counts
> - 12 principles and 8 performance domains named at a high level
> - Tailoring and hybrid approaches
> - Awareness that PMBOK 8 (Nov 2025) refines again; knowledge-area concepts still underlie practice (see [[038 PMBOK Knowledge Areas]])

## 13. ⭐ Advanced: Predictive vs Agile vs Hybrid Life Cycles
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
| | Predictive (waterfall) | Iterative / incremental | Agile (Scrum, Kanban) | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | Fixed upfront | Refined per iteration | Evolving backlog | Mix |
| Delivery | One at the end | Increments | Frequent small releases | Mix per component |
| Change | Costly, controlled | Moderate | Welcome | Per component |
| Best for | Stable, regulated, physical (construction, plant) | Moderate uncertainty | High uncertainty, software | Large programmes |

Choose using **requirements volatility, cost of change, regulatory constraints, customer availability, team skills**. Agile: Scrum events (sprint planning, daily scrum, review, retrospective), roles (Product Owner, Scrum Master, developers), artefacts (product backlog, sprint backlog, increment). **Hybrid** example: predictive for site works and procurement; agile for the digital layer. Metrics shift from **plan-vs-actual** to velocity, burn-down, cycle time and outcomes.

### Example
A smart-warehouse programme: civil work and equipment installation run predictive (WBS, Gantt, EVM); the WMS software and dashboards run in 2-week Scrum sprints; both synchronised via quarterly integration milestones and a single steering committee.

### In the news
See news box. Navi Mumbai Airport shows predictive logic for megaprojects (land, civil, regulatory approvals) where scope must be locked early, while PMBOK 7/8's emphasis on tailoring recognises that not all work fits one model.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Would you run this project in agile or waterfall?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Decision criteria (volatility, cost of change, regulation)
> - Hybrid as realistic for large programmes
> - How governance and reporting adapt
> - Stakeholder and team readiness for agile

## 14. ⭐ Advanced: Business Case and Project Selection (NPV, Payback)
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
Projects are selected on a **business case**: costs, benefits, risks and alternatives, aligned to strategy.

$$\text{NPV} = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

Accept if NPV > 0. **Payback period** = time to recover the initial investment (ignores time value and later cash flows). **IRR** = discount rate where NPV = 0. **BCR (benefit-cost ratio)** = PV of benefits / PV of costs (>1 acceptable). Also: scoring models (weighted criteria), opportunity cost, strategic fit, risk-adjusted returns. Prefer NPV for comparing projects of different sizes with a capital constraint (use profitability index = NPV / investment).

### Example
Invest ₹100 lakh now; inflows ₹40, ₹50, ₹60 lakh in years 1–3; r = 10%.
PV = 40/1.1 + 50/1.1² + 60/1.1³ = 36.36 + 41.32 + 45.08 = 122.76.
**NPV = 122.76 − 100 = ₹22.76 lakh** (accept). Cumulative inflow: 40, 90, 150 → payback = 2 + (100 − 90)/60 = **2.17 years**. BCR = 122.76/100 = 1.23.

### In the news
See news box. Large infrastructure cases like Navi Mumbai Airport (proposed 1997, approved 2007, opened 2025) show that long delays erode NPV because benefits are pushed out while costs accrue; time value matters.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you decide which of three projects to fund?" or "Compute NPV and payback for these cash flows."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - NPV as primary criterion with formula and the discount rate rationale
> - Payback and IRR as supplements with their limits
> - Strategic fit, risk and resource constraints in a scoring model
> - Sensitivity analysis on delays and cost overruns
