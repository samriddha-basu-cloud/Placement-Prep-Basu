## 1. Tuckman's Stages of Team Development
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning; leader style shifts by stage

### Definition
Bruce Tuckman (1965) described four stages a new group passes through; he and Jensen added a fifth, **adjourning**, in 1977. PMBOK lists it as the standard model under *Develop Team*. Stages are not strictly linear: a new member, a scope change or a leadership change can send the team back to storming.

| Stage | What it looks like | PM's role |
|---|---|---|
| **Forming** | Polite, uncertain, dependent on the PM; people test boundaries | Direct: clarify goals, roles, ways of working; run the kick-off |
| **Storming** | Conflict over roles, methods, leadership; cliques form; productivity dips | Coach: surface and resolve disputes, keep the focus on task, do not suppress |
| **Norming** | Norms accepted, trust grows, people give feedback and share work | Facilitate: reinforce norms, delegate more, document working agreements |
| **Performing** | Autonomous, high-trust, self-correcting | Delegate: remove blockers, stretch goals, recognise |
| **Adjourning** | Project winds down; pride mixed with anxiety about next role | Close: lessons learned, celebrate, release people, handle next assignments |

Why it matters for a PM: team size drives complexity. The number of communication channels is $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$, so adding people multiplies the coordination load, and every joiner can reset the stage.

### Example
An 8-person project team for a WMS roll-out at an Indian retailer has $\frac{8 \times 7}{2} = 28$ channels. When 4 vendor engineers join at the integration stage, the team of 12 has $\frac{12 \times 11}{2} = 66$ channels, 38 more. Predictably, the team that was "performing" in design drops back to storming over who owns interface defects. A good PM expects the dip, holds a short re-forming session (updated charter, RACI, escalation path) and the team recovers in about two sprints instead of drifting for two months.

### In the news
See news box. With manager engagement at 22% globally, a PM who consciously moves a team through storming (instead of ignoring conflict) is solving the exact gap Gallup measured.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Explain Tuckman's model. Which stage is hardest for the PM?" or "Your team is in storming. What do you do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - The five stages, with the behaviour seen in each and a different leadership style per stage
> - Non-linear: new members or change events reset the team
> - Storming is normal and productive if conflict is about work, not people
> - A concrete action: working agreements, clear roles, early wins
> - Link to team size and channels $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$

## 2. Drexler-Sibbet Team Performance Model
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Seven stages; orientation to renewal; diagnose the stuck stage

### Definition
Allan Drexler and David Sibbet's **Team Performance Model** describes seven stages in two phases: **creating** a team (stages 1-4) and **sustaining** it (5-7). Each stage answers a question, and a team stuck at a stage usually shows a recognisable symptom.

| # | Stage | Key question | Symptom if unresolved |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Orientation** | Why am I here? | Confusion about purpose and membership |
| 2 | **Trust building** | Who are you? | Guardedness, hidden agendas |
| 3 | **Goal clarification** | What are we doing? | Mixed goals, arguments over priorities |
| 4 | **Commitment** | How will we do it? | Foot-dragging, "someone else will do it" |
| 5 | **Implementation** | Who does what, when, where? | Missed handoffs, unclear roles |
| 6 | **High performance** | Wow! | (success state) |
| 7 | **Renewal** | Why continue? | Burn-out, loss of purpose in long projects |

Compared with Tuckman, the model is a **diagnostic checklist**: it tells the PM which question the team has not yet answered, and it explicitly includes renewal for long programmes. Both are descriptive models; neither guarantees a team passes through every stage.

### Example
A 14-month ERP programme team keeps missing sprint goals despite strong individual developers. Using the model, the PM finds the team is stuck at stage 4: members agreed goals in the kick-off but never made explicit commitments, so every task was "assumed" to belong to somebody else. Fix: a commitment round in planning where each person states what they will deliver by the next review, tracked on the board. For a team at month 10 who now say "why are we still doing this?", the issue is stage 7: refresh the purpose by showing user impact and rotating tasks.

### In the news
See news box. PMBOK 8's stress on empowered teams assumes stages 3-5 are done well: empowerment without clear goals and commitments produces drift, not autonomy.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you know your team is not gelling, and what do you do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Names a model and uses it diagnostically rather than reciting it
> - Matches symptom to stage (unclear goals vs low trust vs unclear roles)
> - Picks a targeted intervention for that stage
> - Mentions renewal in long projects (rotation, impact stories, celebrating milestones)

## 3. Team Charter and Ground Rules
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Team values, decision rules, communication norms, meeting rules, conflict protocol

### Definition
The **project charter** (sponsor-issued) authorises the project, names the PM and states objectives. The **team charter** is written by the team itself and records how it will work together. PMBOK treats it as a tool for developing the team and lists ground rules as an input to team management. It is short (one or two pages), signed by everyone and revisited when the team changes.

Typical contents:
- **Purpose and shared values** (for example "no surprises", "assume good intent").
- **Roles and decision rights** (who decides what; see the RACI sub-topic below).
- **Communication norms:** channels, response times, core overlap hours, stand-up timing, escalation path.
- **Meeting rules:** agenda in advance, timeboxes, decisions and owners recorded.
- **Decision-making method** (consensus, majority, delegated; see the decision-making sub-topic below).
- **Conflict protocol:** talk to the person first, then the PM, then the sponsor within a time limit.
- **Definition of done, quality norms, working hours, leave coverage.**
- **Consequences:** what happens when a norm is repeatedly broken.

Distinguish it from the **project charter**: one authorises the project, the other governs team behaviour. In Scrum the equivalent is the **working agreement** (see [[041 Agile Project Management]] and [[032 Agile & Scrum Framework]]).

### Example
A hybrid team of 9 across Pune and Bengaluru writes this charter: core overlap 11:00 to 16:00 IST; Slack questions answered in 4 working hours; urgent calls only for P1 incidents; decisions logged in the project wiki within 24 hours; disagreements escalated after two unresolved discussions; every Wednesday a no-meeting block. In week 6 a lead misses two stand-ups; because the charter defines the response (one-to-one first, then PM), the matter is fixed in a conversation instead of a blame email chain.

### In the news
See news box. As Infosys, Wipro and TCS fix office-attendance rules centrally, the team charter is where the project still agrees overlap hours, response times and meeting norms inside those rules.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What do you do in the first week with a new team?" or "How do you set expectations in a team?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Co-creation: the team writes the charter, so ownership is high
> - Content: values, roles, communication, decision rules, conflict protocol
> - Distinction from the project charter
> - Living document, revisited at retrospectives or when members change

## 4. Motivation Theories Applied to Projects
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Maslow, Herzberg two-factor, McGregor X/Y, McClelland, expectancy

### Definition
- **Maslow's hierarchy (1943):** physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualisation. A lower unmet need dominates. On projects: job security for contractors (safety), inclusion (belonging), recognition (esteem), stretch roles (self-actualisation).
- **Herzberg's two-factor theory (1959):** **hygiene factors** (pay, policies, supervision, working conditions, job security) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate; **motivators** (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement) create satisfaction. Removing dissatisfaction is not the same as creating motivation.
- **McGregor's Theory X and Y (1960):** X managers assume people dislike work and need control; Y managers assume people seek responsibility and self-direct. Theory Y fits knowledge work and agile teams; X may be needed in safety-critical or very junior contexts. A third view, **Ouchi's Theory Z**, stresses long-term commitment and consensus.
- **McClelland:** needs for achievement, affiliation and power; assign challenging tasks to high-achievers, coordination roles to high-affiliation people.
- **Vroom's expectancy theory:** motivation = expectancy (effort leads to performance) × instrumentality (performance leads to reward) × valence (value of the reward). If any term is near zero, effort collapses.
- **Pink's autonomy, mastery and purpose** is a popular modern shorthand for intrinsic motivation.

$$\text{Motivation} = \text{Expectancy} \times \text{Instrumentality} \times \text{Valence}$$

### Example
Site engineers on a highway project in Maharashtra are paid on time and have decent accommodation (hygiene fine) yet attrition is high. Applying Herzberg: no recognition and no growth path. The PM introduces a "package owner" role with sign-off authority on a 5 km stretch (responsibility), a monthly quality award (recognition) and a clear route to senior engineer (advancement). Vroom check: scores of 0.8 (expectancy) × 0.7 (instrumentality) × 0.9 (valence) = 0.50; if a delayed bonus lowers instrumentality to 0.3 the product drops to 0.22, which explains why late bonus payment kills effort.

### In the news
See news box. Gallup's finding that engagement is lowest in years suggests many teams have hygiene factors in place but lack the motivators Herzberg describes: recognition, growth and meaningful work.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you motivate a team when you cannot give raises?" or "What is the difference between a motivator and a hygiene factor?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Herzberg correctly: hygiene prevents unhappiness, motivators drive engagement
> - Individual differences: diagnose what each person values (Maslow level, McClelland need)
> - Non-monetary levers: autonomy, recognition, learning, visible impact
> - Theory X vs Y choice depends on context and maturity (ties to situational leadership)
> - Honest limit: a PM often controls recognition and work design, not pay

## 5. Situational Leadership and Leadership Styles
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Hersey-Blanchard S1-S4 by team maturity; servant, transformational, directive styles

### Definition
**Situational leadership** (Hersey and Blanchard) says there is no single best style: the leader adjusts directive behaviour (task) and supportive behaviour (relationship) to the follower's **development level** on a specific task.

| Development level | Follower profile | Style | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | Low competence, high commitment (enthusiastic beginner) | **S1 Directing** | High task, low support: tell, show, supervise |
| D2 | Some competence, low commitment (disillusioned learner) | **S2 Coaching** | High task, high support: explain, invite questions, praise progress |
| D3 | Competent, variable confidence (capable but cautious) | **S3 Supporting** | Low task, high support: listen, encourage, share decisions |
| D4 | Competent and committed (self-reliant achiever) | **S4 Delegating** | Low task, low support: hand over outcome and authority |

Other styles used in project work: **servant leadership** (leader removes obstacles and serves the team; natural in Scrum), **transformational** (inspires through vision and growth), **transactional** (reward for meeting targets), **laissez-faire** (works only with expert teams), **autocratic** (crisis or safety). PMI's power skills include leadership, strategic thinking and collaboration alongside technical skills.

Project fit: matrix organisations give the PM **influence without formal authority**, so leadership leans on expertise, relationships and clarity rather than command.

### Example
A new graduate trainee (D1) joins a supply chain analytics project: the PM gives step-by-step instructions and checks daily (S1). After three weeks she is quick but frustrated by rework (D2): the PM coaches, explaining why the data checks matter. By month 3 she is competent but doubts her recommendations (D3): the PM listens and asks her to present to the client. By month 6 she owns a workstream (D4): the PM only reviews outcomes. Using S4 on day one or S1 in month six would each fail.

### In the news
See news box. PMBOK 8 promotes empowered, self-organising teams, which is S4 for mature teams only; the situational model reminds PMs that the route to empowerment passes through directing and coaching.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What is your leadership style?" or "How do you lead people who do not report to you?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - "It depends on the person and the task": a model, not a slogan
> - Matches style to readiness with an example of changing style over time
> - Influence without authority: expertise, trust, clarity of goal, linking work to sponsor outcomes
> - Names a time the wrong style was used and what was learnt (see [[049 STAR Stories — Leadership & Conflict]])

## 6. Conflict Management and the Thomas-Kilmann Modes
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Five modes on assertiveness vs cooperativeness; PMBOK names; choose by context

### Definition
Conflict is inevitable in projects. PMBOK's commonest sources are **schedule, project priorities, resources, technical opinions, administrative procedures, cost and personalities**; the first three dominate in practice. Moderate task conflict can improve decisions; relationship conflict rarely does.

The **Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (TKI)** plots five modes on two axes: assertiveness (own goals) and cooperativeness (other's goals). PMBOK uses synonyms.

| TKI mode | PMBOK term | Assertive / Cooperative | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Competing** | Force / Direct | High / Low | Emergency, safety, unpopular but essential decision |
| **Collaborating** | Collaborate / Problem-solve | High / High | Both concerns matter, time available, creative solution needed; usually best |
| **Compromising** | Compromise / Reconcile | Medium / Medium | Equal power, time pressure, temporary fix |
| **Avoiding** | Withdraw / Avoid | Low / Low | Trivial issue, cooling-off needed, no power to win |
| **Accommodating** | Smooth / Accommodate | Low / High | Issue matters more to the other side, preserving relationship |

Effective approach: separate people from the problem, agree facts, state interests (not positions), generate options, agree criteria, record the decision, and escalate on a timebox. Techniques: **interest-based** discussion, **mediation**, escalation via the sponsor. Forcing wins the point but leaves the conflict unresolved; collaboration takes the longest but yields durable agreement.

### Example
Two leads disagree: QA wants a 3-week regression window (position) and the build lead wants 1 week to meet a client date (position). The PM asks for interests: QA fears escaped defects at a Tier-1 customer; the build lead fears a penalty of ₹2 lakh per week of delay. Collaborating produces: automate the top 40 regression cases (2 weeks of effort now), run a 1.5-week window, and ship a limited release to one plant first. A compromise (2 weeks flat) would have met neither concern; forcing the 1-week date would have invited defects.

### In the news
See news box. Falling manager engagement is partly a conflict-skills gap: managers who avoid conflict see it return as attrition. In Indian IT projects, new attendance mandates also create fresh conflicts over flexibility that PMs must handle without competing with HR policy.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Tell me about a conflict in your team and how you resolved it." "When would you use force?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Names the five modes and the context for each; collaboration as the default, not the only answer
> - Separates interest from position with a concrete example
> - Early intervention, facts first, private before public
> - Outcome, learning and relationship afterwards (STAR; see [[049 STAR Stories — Leadership & Conflict]])
> - When to escalate to the sponsor and how

## 7. Negotiation and Influence
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ BATNA, ZOPA, interests vs positions; Cialdini's principles; influence without authority

### Definition
PMs negotiate with sponsors (scope, budget), functional managers (people), vendors (price, SLAs) and the team (deadlines). The Harvard school (Fisher and Ury, *Getting to Yes*) gives four rules: separate people from the problem, focus on **interests** not positions, invent options for mutual gain, use objective criteria.

Key concepts:
- **BATNA:** best alternative to a negotiated agreement; the walk-away option that sets your power.
- **Reservation price:** worst deal you accept (set by your BATNA).
- **ZOPA:** zone of possible agreement, the overlap between buyer's maximum and seller's minimum. No overlap, no deal.
- **Anchoring:** the first number shapes the range; make the first offer when you know the market.
- **Distributive (win-lose)** vs **integrative (win-win)** bargaining: integrative adds issues to trade (payment terms, scope, volume, timing).

**Influence** (Cialdini's principles): reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity (and later, unity). In matrix organisations, power sources include expert, referent, reward, legitimate and coercive; PMs rely mostly on expert and referent power. Use the **power-interest grid** from [[040 Risk & Stakeholder Management]] to plan whom to influence.

### Example
Buying 200 handheld scanners for a warehouse project. Buyer's maximum is ₹1.10 crore (alternative: lease at that effective cost); vendor's minimum is ₹0.95 crore. ZOPA = ₹0.95 to ₹1.10 crore, width ₹15 lakh. The PM opens at ₹0.90 crore (anchor), the vendor at ₹1.25 crore. Instead of haggling price only, the PM trades: a 2-year AMC and 30-day payment for a 4% lower price. Settlement at ₹1.02 crore sits inside the ZOPA, and the vendor gains a service contract. A buyer with a weak BATNA (no lease option) would have to settle nearer the top. See [[168 Project Procurement, Contracts & EPC Delivery]] for contract types.

### In the news
See news box. Return-to-office rules are being negotiated project by project: PMs who can trade flexible days for overlap hours and delivery commitments, with data, keep morale up inside the policy limits.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Describe a negotiation you led." "How do you get a functional manager to release a key resource?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Preparation: own BATNA, counterparty interests, ZOPA estimate
> - Interests over positions; multi-issue trades to expand the pie
> - Objective criteria (market rates, data) over pressure
> - Relationship value: repeat game with the vendor or peer
> - Escalation as the last, not first, move

## 8. Emotional Intelligence for Project Managers
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Goleman's five components; self-awareness first; feedback and empathy

### Definition
**Emotional intelligence (EI)** is the ability to recognise and manage one's own emotions and to read and influence others'. Daniel Goleman's mixed model has five components:

| Component | Meaning | PM behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-awareness** | Knowing own moods and triggers | Admits mistakes, seeks feedback |
| **Self-regulation** | Controlling impulses, staying calm | Does not send an angry email at 11 pm |
| **Motivation** | Drive beyond money, optimism | Keeps focus after a setback |
| **Empathy** | Understanding others' feelings | Notices a stressed engineer, adjusts load |
| **Social skills** | Building rapport, managing relationships | Resolves conflict, networks across functions |

PMI's talent triangle names **power skills** (people skills such as collaborative leadership, communication, problem solving) alongside ways of working and business acumen. The 2025 Pulse of the Profession found 66% of project professionals at moderate business acumen, 18% high and 16% low, and positioned it as a differentiator for success ([PMI](https://www.pmi.org/learning/thought-leadership/boosting-business-acumen)).

Practical EI tools: structured **active listening** (paraphrase, ask open questions), **feedback models** (SBI: situation, behaviour, impact), pause before reacting, one-to-ones with a standing agenda.

### Example
Status review: a developer reports a 5-day slip with visible irritation. Low EI: "Why is this always late?" in front of the team. High EI: pause, take it offline, use SBI: "In yesterday's build (situation) the API tests were not run (behaviour), which delayed QA by two days (impact). What got in the way?" It emerges the test environment was down for a week; the PM fixes the environment, resets the plan, and the developer keeps trust and flags the next slip early.

### In the news
See news box. In India, manager engagement dropped from 39% to 30% in a year; empathy and listening are the cheapest levers a PM has to counter that without a budget.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you handle a demotivated team member?" or "Give an example where emotional intelligence helped you."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Components named briefly, with an observable behaviour per component
> - A story with a trigger, your own emotion managed, the other person's perspective and a result
> - Feedback delivered privately, specific and behaviour-focused (SBI)
> - Recognises limits: EI does not replace clear goals and accountability

## 9. Virtual and Hybrid Team Management
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Overlap hours, async norms, trust, tools, inclusion, time zones

### Definition
**Virtual teams** have members in different locations (and often time zones); **hybrid teams** combine office days and remote days. PMBOK lists virtual teams as an acquisition and development approach with benefits (wider talent pool, lower travel and facility cost) and challenges (isolation, communication delay, cultural misunderstanding, trust). For Agile-specific practices see [[041 Agile Project Management]] (distributed teams section).

Operating principles:
- **Overlap hours** fixed for synchronous work; everything else asynchronous with written decisions.
- **Documentation by default:** decisions, actions and risks in a shared tool (Jira, Confluence, Teams), not in chats.
- **Rich channel for rich messages:** video for conflict and kick-offs; chat for quick status.
- **Rotate meeting times** across time zones so one region does not always bear inconvenient hours.
- **Trust through reliability:** commitments visible on a board, small deliveries often.
- **Inclusion:** remote participants speak first; avoid proximity bias in recognition and promotion.
- **Wellbeing:** explicit working hours, no-meeting blocks, leave coverage.
- **Security and compliance:** data access, device policy, client NDAs.

Metrics: meeting hours per head, response-time compliance, sprint predictability, pulse-survey engagement.

### Example
A programme team of 15 across Pune, London and Singapore. Overlap exists only 13:30-16:30 IST. The PM limits live meetings to that window, records decisions in a log within 24 hours, rotates the weekly review (IST, then GMT-friendly slot) and uses a 15-minute async stand-up thread. Result tracked over a quarter: meeting hours per person fall from 11 to 7 per week while sprint completion stays above 90% in the PM's tracker. Without the log, London re-opened settled decisions twice a month.

### In the news
See news box. With Infosys setting a floor of 10 office days a month and TCS asking for five days, "hybrid" in India is policy-set; the PM's job is to organise the work so that office days are used for collaboration and remote days for focused work.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you manage a team across time zones?" "How do you keep remote members engaged?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Overlap hours, async documentation and rotation of inconvenient meeting times
> - Trust and visibility via boards and commitments
> - Inclusion and recognition fairness between remote and in-office staff
> - Specific tools and a metric to check that it works

## 10. RACI and Role Clarity
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ R, A, C, I; one A per task; RASCI, RACI pitfalls

### Definition
A **RACI matrix** maps tasks or deliverables to people with four codes: **R**esponsible (does the work), **A**ccountable (owns the outcome, signs off, exactly **one** per row), **C**onsulted (two-way input before the decision), **I**nformed (one-way update after). Variants: **RASCI** (adds Support), **RACI-VS** (adds Verify and Sign-off), **DACI** (Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed), and **RAM** (responsibility assignment matrix, the generic PMBOK term).

Build steps: list deliverables, list roles (roles, not just names), assign codes, validate (one A, at least one R, few Cs, no empty rows), review with the team, store with the resource plan ([[038 PMBOK Knowledge Areas]]).

Common failures: two As (nobody is accountable), no A, too many Cs (slow decisions), everyone Informed (noise), A held by a person with no authority, and a RACI that is never updated. Also see the use of RACI with stakeholders in [[040 Risk & Stakeholder Management]].

### Example
Go-live of a new dispatch module:

| Task | PM | Dev lead | QA lead | Ops head | Sponsor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Finalise requirements | R | C | C | **A** | I |
| Build and unit test | I | **A/R** | C | I | I |
| UAT sign-off | R | C | R | **A** | I |
| Go-live decision | R | C | C | C | **A** |
| Hypercare plan | **A/R** | R | C | C | I |

Check: each row has exactly one A. UAT sign-off has two Rs (PM and QA) and one A (Ops head), which is acceptable. If the go-live row had Ops head and Sponsor both as A, a defect found at midnight would produce a delay while two executives each assumed the other would decide.

### In the news
See news box. Self-organising teams (PMBOK 8) still need explicit decision rights; empowerment without a clear A is how decisions stall.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What is a RACI? What are its weaknesses?" "Who is accountable when two teams share a deliverable?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Exact definitions; one A per row; R can be many
> - Where used: at kick-off with sponsor, for each phase, for change-control and approvals
> - Pitfalls: over-consultation, stale matrix, accountability without authority
> - Alternatives (DACI, RASCI) and when they fit

## 11. Decision-Making Methods
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Unanimity, majority, plurality, autocratic; multicriteria matrix; Vroom-Yetton; groupthink

### Definition
PMBOK lists group decision techniques: **unanimity** (everyone agrees), **majority** (more than 50%), **plurality** (largest block, even without majority) and **dictatorship** (one person decides). Related tools: **consensus** (everyone can live with it), **voting**, **fist-of-five**, **Delphi**, **multicriteria decision analysis (MCDA)** with a weighted decision matrix, **decision trees** (see [[150 Decision Analysis & Simulation]]), and the **Vroom-Yetton-Jago** model that picks autocratic, consultative or group style by decision quality need, information held and acceptance required.

| Method | Speed | Buy-in | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autocratic | Fast | Low | Crises, trivial or technical calls |
| Consultative | Medium | Medium | PM decides after hearing experts |
| Majority / plurality | Medium | Medium | Routine options, no strong minority |
| Consensus / unanimity | Slow | High | Team norms, hard-to-reverse decisions needing commitment |

Hazards: **groupthink** (cohesion suppresses dissent; counter with devil's advocate and anonymous input), **HiPPO** (highest paid person's opinion), anchoring, sunk-cost bias. Good practice: define the decision owner and method up front, record the decision in a decision log with rationale and date, and set a review trigger.

**Weighted scoring:** score $= \sum_i w_i s_i$ with $\sum w_i = 1$.

### Example
Choosing an integration vendor with weights Cost 0.35, Speed 0.25, Quality 0.25, Risk 0.15 and scores out of 10:

| Vendor | Cost | Speed | Quality | Risk | Weighted score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 7 | 9 | 6 | 8 | 7.40 |
| B | 9 | 6 | 8 | 6 | 7.55 |
| C | 8 | 7 | 9 | 7 | **7.85** |

For C: $0.35 \times 8 + 0.25 \times 7 + 0.25 \times 9 + 0.15 \times 7 = 2.8 + 1.75 + 2.25 + 1.05 = 7.85$. Vendor C wins even though B is cheapest. A sensitivity check (raise cost weight to 0.50 and cut risk to 0.0 with others at 0.25) is advised before committing; the PM records the weights and who agreed them.

### In the news
See news box. PMBOK 8 stresses accountable, empowered decision-making; the decision log is how a hybrid, partly remote team keeps decisions visible across time zones.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you make decisions when the team is split?" or "Tell me about a time you made an unpopular decision."

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Matching method to stakes and urgency (autocratic for crisis, consensus for norms)
> - Criteria and weights agreed before scoring options
> - Awareness of groupthink and HiPPO; devil's advocate, anonymous voting
> - Decision log and communication of the rationale to those who disagreed
> - Disagree-and-commit once the decision is made

## 12. Performance Management, Feedback and Recognition
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Goals/OKRs, feedback loops, appraisal inputs from functional managers, recognition design

### Definition
In a matrix organisation the PM usually has **no line authority**, so performance management is shared: the PM sets project goals, gives continuous feedback and supplies written input to the **functional manager**, who owns the appraisal. PMBOK *Manage Team* mentions **project performance appraisals** (360-degree feedback, SMART goals), observation and conversation.

Components:
- **Goals:** SMART objectives or OKRs per person tied to deliverables.
- **Feedback loop:** frequent, specific, timely; retrospectives for the team; one-to-ones weekly or fortnightly.
- **Coaching** performance gaps early: clarify expectation, find the cause (skill, will, environment), agree actions with dates.
- **Recognition:** timely, specific, public for achievements, private for development; connect it to a behaviour you want repeated. Fairness: avoid proximity bias in hybrid settings.
- **Team rewards** to avoid internal competition; PMBOK notes that only win-win rewards are effective, and a reward is useful only if the person values it.
- **Under-performance:** documented conversation, support plan with 2 to 4 week checkpoints, involve the functional manager and HR if no improvement; release from the project if needed.

Useful metrics (use carefully to avoid gaming): sprint commitment reliability, defect escape rate, on-time milestone delivery, peer feedback scores. Link to schedule and cost measures in [[042 Cost & Budget Management]].

### Example
Software tester Anil's defect escape rate is 12% against a team norm of 5%. PM's coaching: (1) shares data privately, (2) finds that Anil is covering two modules plus production support, (3) removes one module and adds a pairing session twice a week, (4) sets a 4-week checkpoint with target below 7%. At week 4 it is 6%; the PM recognises the improvement in the retrospective and sends the functional manager a note citing numbers for the appraisal. Compare: waiting for the annual review would have lost the project a quarter of quality.

### In the news
See news box. With only about one in five workers engaged worldwide, recognition and clear feedback are the practical, low-cost levers; they matter more in remote setups where visibility is low.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you handle an under-performing team member who does not report to you?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Private, specific, data-based conversation first; root cause (skill, will, environment)
> - Agreed plan with dates; check-ins; documented
> - Involvement of the functional manager and HR at the right point
> - Fair recognition; credit shared with the team
> - Result and learning (STAR; see [[052 Strengths, Weaknesses, Failures]])

## 13. Interview Scenarios: Difficult Team Situations
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Diagnose, act early, preserve relationship, escalate with a proposal

### Definition
Scenario questions test judgement under ambiguity. A reusable approach: **(1) clarify and gather facts, (2) diagnose the cause with a model (Tuckman stage, conflict type, motivation), (3) talk to the person or people privately, (4) agree a time-bound action, (5) escalate with a proposed solution if the action fails, (6) follow up and capture lessons.** Always protect the relationship and the project baseline.

| Scenario | Diagnosis | First move |
|---|---|---|
| Star performer is toxic | Behaviour vs output; others disengaging | Private SBI feedback; link to charter; set a clear standard |
| Two leads clash repeatedly | Conflict over priorities or resources | Interests, not positions; joint working session; sponsor if cross-functional |
| Key member resigns mid-project | Single point of failure | Knowledge transfer plan, pairing, re-baseline, risk register update |
| Sponsor asks for scope increase with no extra time | Negotiation | Show trade-off triangle; offer options (de-scope, add resource, move date); change control |
| Team missing every sprint goal | Commitment or capacity problem | Retrospective, review velocity and interruptions, resize commitment |
| Remote member silent for a week | Wellbeing or blocker | Video one-to-one, ask about blockers, adjust |
| Functional manager withdraws a resource | Priority conflict | Escalate with impact data, propose alternatives |

### Example
Scenario: "Your best developer publicly criticises a colleague's design in stand-up; the colleague has stopped contributing." Model answer: speak to the developer privately with SBI feedback; speak to the colleague to rebuild confidence and clarify that design review happens in a defined forum; update the team charter with a rule that critique is of the work and delivered in design reviews; check in after two weeks. Impact: if two sprints later the colleague is contributing again and the developer has become a design-review lead, the escalation to HR was unnecessary. Mention the alternative if behaviour repeats: documented warning with the functional manager.

### In the news
See news box. Falling engagement shows up first as silence and low contribution; scenario-ready PMs detect it through one-to-ones, not only dashboards.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Your team member is consistently late with deliverables and the client is complaining. What do you do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Facts first, then a diagnosis, then a private conversation
> - A time-bound plan and a protective buffer for the client
> - Escalation with a proposed solution, not just the problem
> - Outcome with numbers and what you changed in the process
> - Practice: prepare three STAR stories (conflict, motivation, failure) and reuse with the frameworks in [[163 PM Interview Types & Answer Frameworks]]; test yourself on [[171 Project Management Interview Questions & Numericals]]

## 14. ⭐ Advanced: Psychological Safety, Team Health and Self-Organising Teams
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
**Psychological safety** (Amy Edmondson): a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking such as asking questions, admitting errors and disagreeing. Google's **Project Aristotle** (launched 2012, 180 teams, about 250 attributes tested) found it the strongest of five dynamics, followed by **dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact** ([LeaderFactor summary](https://www.leaderfactor.com/learn/project-aristotle-psychological-safety/)); who was on the team mattered less than how members treated each other.

Behaviours that build it: leader admits fallibility, asks for input, responds to bad news with curiosity, runs **blameless post-mortems**, and thanks people for early warnings.

**Team health checks:** the Spotify-style health check (traffic-light vote on mission, delivery pace, learning, fun, support), a **pulse survey**, and **retrospective trends**. Warning signs: silence in meetings, bad news reaching the PM late, rising rework, absenteeism, one person always speaking.

**Self-organising teams** (agile principle; PMBOK 8 "empowered culture") decide how to do the work inside clear boundaries: goal, constraints, definition of done. The leader's role shifts to **servant leadership**: remove impediments, protect focus, coach. Preconditions: skill, clarity of outcome, psychological safety, and authority matched to accountability. **Team size** guidance: Scrum recommends a team of about 10 or fewer; Amazon's "two-pizza" idea gives similar bounds; beyond about 9, communication channels $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ grow quickly (9 people = 36, 12 = 66).

### Example
In a post-mortem for a failed cut-over (late night, 4-hour outage at a 3PL client), the PM runs a blameless review: timeline first, decisions in context, five whys, actions with owners. Finding: the on-call engineer had spotted the config mismatch but did not raise it because last quarter a colleague was blamed for a delay. Actions: a "stop-the-line" rule that anyone can pause a release, a thank-you for the engineer's later disclosure, and a monthly health check. Over the next three releases, the number of issues raised before cut-over rises from 2 to 9 per release and production incidents fall, which the PM reports as the evidence of improved safety.

### In the news
See news box. Gallup's 20% global engagement and 22% manager engagement point to a cultural, not technical, bottleneck; psychological safety is the mechanism that converts empowerment (PMBOK 8) into early risk reporting, which feeds [[040 Risk & Stakeholder Management]].

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you create an environment where team members raise problems early?" or "What makes a high-performing team?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Psychological safety with the Google finding (and the caveat that it is one study, not law)
> - Specific leader behaviours: respond to bad news calmly, blameless reviews, model fallibility
> - Measurement: pulse surveys, health checks, count of risks raised early
> - Empowerment within boundaries; servant leadership
> - Link to Agile retrospectives and to risk management
