## 1. Project vs Programme vs Portfolio
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Doing projects right vs doing the right projects; scope, change, success, management

### Definition
- A **project** is a temporary endeavour producing a unique output; success is measured by scope, time, cost, quality (see [[037 PM Fundamentals & Lifecycle]]).
- A **programme** (program in PMI usage) is a group of related projects, subsidiary programmes and activities managed together to obtain **benefits and control not available from managing them separately**. Success is measured by benefits delivered and strategic alignment.
- A **portfolio** is a collection of projects, programmes, subsidiary portfolios and operations managed as a group to achieve **strategic objectives**. Components need not be related. Success is measured by aggregate investment performance and benefit realisation.

| Dimension | Project | Programme | Portfolio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | Deliver output | Deliver benefits via coordinated components | Maximise value of the investment mix |
| Scope | Defined, progressively elaborated | Larger, changes with benefits | Changes with strategy |
| Time | Fixed end | Fixed end, longer | Ongoing, reviewed periodically |
| Change | Controlled via change control | Embraced if benefits require | Monitored against strategy |
| Manager | Project manager | Programme manager | Portfolio manager / investment committee |
| Key question | "Are we building it right?" | "Are the combined results delivering the benefits?" | "Are we funding the right things?" |

The PMI standards (*The Standard for Portfolio Management* and *The Standard for Program Management*, both in 4th editions dated 2017 at the time of writing; check pmi.org for later editions) set out performance domains. For portfolios these cover strategic management, governance, capacity and capability, stakeholder engagement, value management and risk. For programmes: strategy alignment, benefits management, stakeholder engagement, governance and life-cycle management. Outside PMI: AXELOS **MSP** (Managing Successful Programmes) and **MoP** (Management of Portfolios) are widely used in the UK and Indian consulting practice.

### Example
An Indian energy company: **Portfolio** = rooftop solar, grid modernisation, EV charging, smart meters and a back-office ERP upgrade. **Programme** = "EV charging roll-out" with projects for site acquisition, charger procurement, app development and utility approvals; benefit is charging revenue and market share. **Project** = installing 200 chargers in Mumbai (scope, schedule, cost). The portfolio committee decides how many crores each domain receives; the programme manager makes sure the app and chargers deliver revenue together; the project manager delivers the 200 sites.

### In the news
See news box. MoSPI's report is a portfolio view (1,775 projects by ministry and sector), while individual highway projects are managed as programmes of packages and projects.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What is the difference between programme and portfolio management?" or "Is a large project a programme?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Projects deliver outputs, programmes deliver benefits through coordination, portfolios allocate resources to strategy
> - Components of a programme are related; components of a portfolio need not be
> - Size alone does not make a programme; coordinated benefits do
> - A concrete example with the three levels
> - Names PMI's standards (and MSP/MoP) rather than inventing definitions

## 2. Programme Management: Lifecycle, Governance and Roles
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Define, deliver, close; benefits-driven; programme board, sponsor, SRO

### Definition
PMI divides the **programme life cycle** into three phases: **initiation** (programme definition, business case), **delivery** (components planned, executed in tranches, benefits delivered incrementally) and **closure**. MSP organises it as **identifying, defining, delivering tranches, realising benefits and closing**, and describes **transformational flow** and seven governance themes (vision, leadership, benefits realisation, stakeholder engagement, blueprint design, risk and issues, quality assurance).

Key artefacts: **programme business case**, **benefits register** and profiles, **programme roadmap** (components, milestones, dependencies, tranches), **programme risk and issue logs**, **stakeholder engagement plan**, **blueprint** (target operating model), **programme governance plan**.

Roles: **Sponsor / SRO** (accountable for the benefits), **programme board** (steering committee with business and supplier representation), **programme manager** (coordination, integration, reporting), **business change managers** (embed the change in operations), **project managers** for components, and the **programme office** (the support function; PMO types are compared later in this note).

Programme-level processes differ from project ones: **integrated planning** across components, **cross-project risk aggregation** (risks that appear small per project but correlate), **resource and supplier orchestration**, **change control at benefit level** and **tranche reviews** with a go/no-go on the next tranche. Estimation approaches: bottom-up from components with a programme contingency separate from project contingencies (see [[042 Cost & Budget Management]]).

### Example
A retail chain's "Omnichannel" programme (₹120 crore, 24 months) has six projects: OMS, WMS upgrade, store pick-in-store, delivery partner integration, mobile app, loyalty data. Tranche 1 (6 months) delivers the OMS and WMS upgrade; the board reviews at the end of tranche 1 and releases tranche 2 (₹50 crore) only if order-promising accuracy has risen from 91% to at least 95%. The programme manager tracks cross-project dependencies, for instance that the mobile app needs the OMS API by month 5.

### In the news
See news box. The MoSPI data shows 675 projects beyond 80% physical completion; a programme board would normally review such a cohort for benefits and closure readiness rather than only cost.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How is managing a programme different from managing a big project?" or "What does a programme manager actually do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Benefits ownership rather than output delivery; tranche-based funding and reviews
> - Coordination of interdependencies, shared resources and suppliers
> - Programme-level risk aggregation and governance (board, sponsor, business change managers)
> - Stakeholder and change management as a core, not an add-on
> - Link between programme roadmap and benefits register

## 3. Portfolio Management and Prioritisation Models
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Strategic alignment, selection criteria, scoring, financial, AHP, efficient frontier

### Definition
**Portfolio management** is the centralised management of one or more portfolios to achieve strategic objectives: **identify, categorise, evaluate, select, prioritise, balance, authorise, monitor and review** components. Categories help reasoning: *run* (compliance, sustain), *grow* (revenue), *transform* (strategic shifts) or *must-do* (regulatory) versus *discretionary*.

Selection models:
- **Weighted scoring model:** criteria weights $w_i$ (sum to 1), scores $s_{ij}$ per project, total $S_j = \sum_i w_i s_{ij}$. Fast, transparent, subjective. Related to [[034 Prioritization Frameworks]] (RICE, WSJF).
- **Financial models:** NPV, IRR, payback, profitability index $PI = \frac{NPV}{\text{Investment}}$ (see [[109 Valuation Basics (NPV, IRR, DCF)]]).
- **AHP (analytic hierarchy process):** pairwise comparisons build weights; consistency ratio checks judgement quality.
- **Bubble chart:** x = risk, y = benefit, bubble size = cost; shows balance.
- **Efficient frontier:** portfolio combinations giving maximum return at each risk level; select a point matching appetite.
- **Real options and stage-gate funding:** fund in steps, keep the option to stop.
- **Must-do rule:** regulatory or safety projects (for example statutory reporting) bypass scoring but consume budget.

Use a **two-step approach**: hard filters first (mandatory, strategic fit, minimum return) then scoring, then budget and capacity fitting. Rank by score per rupee (or PI) when budget is binding, which approximates the **knapsack** logic. Weights should be agreed by the investment committee before scoring to avoid reverse-engineering the answer.

$$S_j = \sum_{i} w_i \, s_{ij}, \qquad \sum_i w_i = 1$$

### Example
Short form: three projects cost ₹10, ₹20, ₹30 crore with NPVs of ₹15, ₹24 and ₹27 crore. PI = 1.5, 1.2 and 0.9. With only ₹30 crore, funding the first two (cost ₹30 crore, NPV ₹39 crore) beats funding the third alone (NPV ₹27 crore) even though the third has the largest NPV. A full scored example follows in the next sub-topic.

### In the news
See news box. At national scale, the portfolio problem is the same: Rs 37.11 lakh crore of revised cost has to be sequenced against budget capacity, and 17 ministries compete for the same fiscal room.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you decide which projects to fund with a fixed budget?" or "What if the highest-NPV project is not chosen by your model?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Strategic filter, then scoring, then budget and resource fit
> - Weights agreed upfront; sensitivity of ranking to weights
> - Ratio (PI or score per rupee) when budget is constrained
> - Must-do items and interdependencies treated outside the scoring
> - Review and re-prioritise periodically; kill projects that no longer fit

## 4. Worked Portfolio Ranking Example
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Weighted score, budget fit, NPV check, sensitivity

### Definition
A reusable procedure for case interviews or committee papers: (1) fix criteria and weights, (2) score every project, (3) compute weighted score, (4) rank, (5) fund down the ranking until the budget is exhausted, (6) check capacity and dependencies, (7) test sensitivity.

Criteria and weights (agreed first): **Strategic fit 0.30, Financial return 0.30, Risk (higher score = safer) 0.15, Urgency or compliance 0.15, Resource readiness 0.10.** Scores 1 to 10. Budget available: **₹60 crore**.

### Example
A manufacturer's candidate list (NPV in ₹ crore, net of cost):

| Project | Cost | Fit | Fin. | Risk | Urgency | Resources | **Score** | NPV |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A WMS roll-out | 18 | 9 | 8 | 6 | 6 | 7 | **7.60** | 27 |
| B Rooftop solar | 12 | 7 | 9 | 8 | 4 | 8 | **7.40** | 20 |
| D ESG (BRSR) reporting | 6 | 6 | 4 | 8 | 10 | 9 | **6.60** | 4 |
| E Supplier portal | 9 | 7 | 6 | 7 | 5 | 8 | **6.50** | 12 |
| G Cold-chain pilot | 15 | 9 | 6 | 5 | 4 | 6 | **6.45** | 19 |
| C Plant automation | 30 | 8 | 7 | 4 | 5 | 5 | **6.35** | 33 |
| F New ERP module | 24 | 6 | 5 | 5 | 6 | 4 | **5.35** | 14 |
| H Office refit | 8 | 3 | 2 | 9 | 2 | 9 | **4.05** | 1 |

Check for A: $0.30 \times 9 + 0.30 \times 8 + 0.15 \times 6 + 0.15 \times 6 + 0.10 \times 7 = 2.70 + 2.40 + 0.90 + 0.90 + 0.70 = 7.60$.

Funding down the ranking: A (18) → 18; B (12) → 30; D (6) → 36; E (9) → 45; G (15) → 60. Budget exactly used; **C, F and H** are deferred. Portfolio NPV = 27 + 20 + 4 + 12 + 19 = **₹82 crore** on ₹60 crore of spend. An exhaustive search of all 256 combinations confirms that no other affordable set has a higher weighted score or higher total NPV, so greedy ranking is optimal here (this is not guaranteed in general).

Interview-level discussion: C has the single highest NPV (₹33 crore) but needs 30 crore (half of the budget) and has a low risk score; funding it would crowd out five projects. D is funded despite NPV of only ₹4 crore because compliance urgency and low cost lift it, in practice it is a must-do. **Sensitivity:** if the weight on financial return rises to 0.45 and fit falls to 0.15, C moves up the list; the PM tests this and reports the ranking is stable for A and B but not for C, so the committee should explicitly decide on C.

### In the news
See news box. Budget-limited ranking is what ministries do with a fixed capital outlay, and the 10% average overrun shows why risk and resource readiness need explicit weight in the score.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "We have ₹60 crore and eight proposals. Walk me through how you would choose." (Often given as a case with a table.)

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - States criteria and weights before scoring; shows one calculation line
> - Funds down the ranking within budget and reports NPV of the chosen set
> - Flags must-do projects, dependencies and capacity limits
> - Sensitivity analysis and a recommendation about the borderline project
> - Mentions risk-adjusted view and benefit tracking after approval (see the benefits realisation sub-topic below)

## 5. Benefits Realisation Management
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Identify, plan, deliver, sustain; benefit owner; baseline, tracking, post-implementation review

### Definition
**Benefits realisation management (BRM)** ensures that the value promised in the business case is delivered and sustained after the project closes. Stages: **identify** (link to strategic objectives, quantify), **plan** (benefits register, owners, metrics, baseline, target, timing, dependencies), **deliver** (track during delivery, adjust), **transition** (hand to the business owner) and **sustain / review** (post-implementation review 3 to 12 months later).

A **benefits profile** records: description, type (financial cashable, non-cashable, strategic, risk avoidance), KPI, baseline, target, date, owner, assumptions, dis-benefits. A **benefits map** links outputs, outcomes, benefits and strategic goals. Distinguish **output** (a system), **outcome** (behaviour change: orders picked faster) and **benefit** (value: lower cost, higher service level).

Key principle: **the benefit owner is a business line manager**, not the project manager. Projects end; benefits arrive after.

$$\text{Benefit realisation \%} = \frac{\text{Actual change from baseline}}{\text{Target change from baseline}} \times 100$$

### Example
Programme to raise on-time-in-full (OTIF) from 82% to 90%, costing ₹18 crore. Each OTIF point is worth ₹0.45 crore a year (fewer expedites and penalties). Target benefit = $8 \times 0.45 = ₹3.6$ crore a year, plus a one-off ₹6 crore of working capital release in year 1. Evaluating at 12% over 5 years of benefits:

| Scenario | Annual benefit | NPV (₹ crore) | Payback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target (OTIF 90%) | 3.6 | **+0.33** (IRR about 12.9%) | about 3.3 years |
| Actual OTIF 87% (62.5% realised) | 2.25 | **-4.53** | not within 5 years |

Realisation = (87 - 82) / (90 - 82) = 62.5%. The programme looks marginally good on paper but turns NPV-negative if just over a third of the benefit is missed, which is the reason to track benefits monthly and to name an owner. See [[109 Valuation Basics (NPV, IRR, DCF)]] for the NPV mechanics and [[012 Supply Chain Analytics & KPIs]] for OTIF.

### In the news
See news box. The MoSPI report shows spend and physical completion but not benefit realisation; the gap between the two (51.91% of cost spent versus 38% of projects past 80% physical completion) shows why benefit and outcome measures are needed beyond cost tracking.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "A project finished on time and budget but the business says nothing changed. What went wrong?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Output vs outcome vs benefit distinction
> - Benefits owned and tracked by business owners after go-live, with baselines
> - Post-implementation review and benefit tracking until realised
> - Dis-benefits and assumptions managed; link to business case (NPV sensitivity)
> - PMO or programme office maintains the benefits register

## 6. PMO Types and Value
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Supportive, controlling, directive; functions; value proposition; failure modes

### Definition
A **PMO** (project, programme or portfolio management office) is an organisational structure that standardises governance processes and facilitates sharing of resources, methodologies, tools and techniques. PMBOK classifies by **degree of control**:

| Type | Control | What it does | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Supportive** | Low | Consultative: templates, best practices, training, lessons learned, repository | Loosely coupled orgs, project-based work done by units |
| **Controlling** | Moderate | Provides support and requires compliance: framework adoption, templates, audits, governance reviews | Regulated or maturing orgs |
| **Directive** | High | Directly manages projects; PMs report to the PMO | Large programmes, transformation, crisis |

Functions: methodology and standards, **portfolio reporting**, resource management, training and mentoring, **benefits tracking**, tool administration, project audits and health checks, knowledge management, and **strategic alignment**. Other labels: **Enterprise PMO (EPMO)**, **Strategic PMO**, **Agile centre of excellence**, **Value delivery office**.

PMO value metrics: project success rate, schedule and cost variance, % of projects with a benefits owner, resource utilisation, forecast accuracy, time to start projects, stakeholder satisfaction. **Failure modes:** seen as bureaucracy, reports nobody reads, no sponsor, wrong level of control for the culture, measuring compliance rather than value.

### Example
A 3PL has 40 live projects run by regional teams. Year 1: a **supportive** PMO provides templates and a risk checklist; adoption is 55%. Year 2: it becomes **controlling**, requiring stage-gate sign-off for projects above ₹1 crore; the share of projects with a benefits owner rises from 30% to 85%. A failing SAP migration is taken over by a **directive** PMO team for 6 months, then handed back. Moving along the spectrum is a deliberate choice linked to maturity and risk, not a one-way ladder.

### In the news
See news box. PMBOK 8's appendix on PMOs as strategic enablers and the research caution that PMOs can leave senior managers dissatisfied both point to the same lesson: PMO value must be shown in outcomes (delivery, benefits), not forms completed.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What type of PMO would you set up for a company with no project discipline?" or "How would you prove a PMO's value to the CFO?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Three types by degree of control and a rationale for the starting type
> - Evolution as maturity grows; directive for exceptional cases
> - Measurable value: on-time %, benefit realisation, forecast accuracy, cost of delay avoided
> - Avoids bureaucracy: minimum viable process, tailor to risk and size
> - Role in portfolio reporting and resource balancing

## 7. Stage-Gate Governance
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Gates as decision points; go, kill, hold, recycle; criteria; staged funding

### Definition
**Stage-gate** (Robert Cooper) splits a project into stages, each ending in a **gate**, a formal decision point where a governance body decides: **go** (fund the next stage), **kill**, **hold** (pause) or **recycle** (redo with changes). A typical product or capital-project version: idea, scoping, business case, development, testing and validation, launch, post-launch review. PMBOK calls these **phase gates** or **kill points**.

Gate design: **deliverables** expected, **criteria** (strategic fit, market and technical feasibility, financial return, risk, resources), **decision makers** (steering committee, not the PM), **funding released for the next stage only**. Benefits: cheap early learning, discipline to stop failing projects, transparency. Costs: delays between gates, over-formal documentation, gaming of gate criteria. Agile variants: **lightweight gates tied to increments**, funding in tranches or by value stream rather than long upfront approvals.

Expected-cost logic: with stage costs $c_1, c_2, \dots$ and pass probabilities $p_1, p_2, \dots$:

$$E[\text{cost}] = c_1 + p_1 c_2 + p_1 p_2 c_3 + \dots$$

### Example
A new cold-chain service has stages costing ₹0.5, ₹2, ₹6 and ₹20 crore (total ₹28.5 crore) with pass probabilities of 0.7, 0.6 and 0.8 at the first three gates. Expected spend = $0.5 + 0.7 \times 2 + 0.7 \times 0.6 \times 6 + 0.7 \times 0.6 \times 0.8 \times 20 = 0.5 + 1.4 + 2.52 + 6.72 = ₹11.14$ crore. Probability of reaching launch = $0.7 \times 0.6 \times 0.8 = 33.6\%$. Staged funding therefore cuts the expected investment from ₹28.5 crore (all committed up front) to about ₹11.1 crore, because two out of three ideas are stopped when the spend is still small.

### In the news
See news box. With Rs 3.4 lakh crore of overrun already embedded, gate reviews that re-baseline cost before the next tranche is released are the practical control for large public projects.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you govern a Rs 500 crore capex project?" or "What is a phase gate and who decides?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Gates with clear entry/exit criteria and a decision body above the PM
> - Go, kill, hold, recycle options (kill is real, not rhetorical)
> - Funding tied to gates; re-estimate at each gate
> - Risk of bureaucracy; agile and tiered gates by project size
> - Expected value of early stopping

## 8. Maturity Models: CMMI, OPM3 and P3M3
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Five levels; assess, then improve; know which model measures what

### Definition
Maturity models describe how reliably and systematically an organisation delivers.
- **CMMI** (Capability Maturity Model Integration; originated at Carnegie Mellon SEI, now maintained by ISACA): maturity levels **1 Initial, 2 Managed, 3 Defined, 4 Quantitatively Managed, 5 Optimizing**. Strong in software and services, and used in Indian IT tenders (CMMI Level 5 is a common vendor claim).
- **OPM3** (Organizational Project Management Maturity Model, PMI): assesses portfolio, programme and project management across **standardise, measure, control, continuously improve** stages; best practices and capabilities with an improvement plan.
- **P3M3** (Portfolio, Programme and Project Management Maturity Model; AXELOS, now PeopleCert): levels **1 Awareness, 2 Repeatable, 3 Defined, 4 Managed, 5 Optimised**, assessed across three models (portfolio, programme, project) and seven perspectives (such as management control, benefits management, risk, resource, finance).
- Others: **PMMM** (Kerzner), **PMI Pulse** benchmarks.

Use: baseline assessment, gap analysis, roadmap, re-assess in 12 to 24 months. **Pitfalls:** pursuing a level for its own sake (certificate chasing), applying the same level everywhere, and neglecting culture.

| Level (generic) | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Ad hoc, hero-dependent |
| 2 | Basic repeatable practices per project |
| 3 | Organisation-wide standard processes |
| 4 | Measured, controlled with data |
| 5 | Continuous improvement, innovation |

### Example
A mid-size EPC contractor assesses itself on P3M3 and finds: project level 3 (standard templates), programme level 2 (each programme invents its own governance) and portfolio level 1 (project selection by whoever shouts loudest). The PMO's 18-month roadmap: introduce a common scoring model and quarterly portfolio review (portfolio to level 2), standard programme governance pack (level 3), and a benefits register. Reassessment shows the portfolio at level 3 in 18 months; more important, the share of projects approved with a signed business case moves from 40% to 90%.

### In the news
See news box. PMI's finding that only 18% of project professionals have high business acumen indicates a capability gap that maturity models make visible and plan to close.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What is CMMI? How would you assess project management maturity in a company?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - The five-level scale and which model measures what (CMMI process, OPM3 and P3M3 organisational PM)
> - Assessment, gap analysis, roadmap, reassessment loop
> - Maturity as a means; the level should fit the business need
> - Examples of practices at each level; culture alongside process

## 9. Resource Capacity Across Projects
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Demand vs capacity heatmap; utilisation; shared pools; levelling at portfolio level

### Definition
A portfolio is limited by **scarce shared resources** (key engineers, data teams, test environments, vendors, cash). **Capacity management** compares **demand** (FTEs per role by period across all projects) with **supply** (headcount × availability, typically 80 to 85% productive after leave, meetings, support). Output: a **capacity-demand heatmap** and decisions: stagger starts, add capacity (hire, contract), cut scope, defer projects.

$$\text{Utilisation} = \frac{\text{Demand (FTE)}}{\text{Capacity (FTE)}}$$

Over 100% means queueing and delay; sustained above about 85 to 90% on a shared pool typically increases lead time sharply (queueing theory; see [[018 Capacity Management & OEE]] and [[005 Production & Operations Planning]]). Techniques: resource levelling and smoothing ([[039 Scheduling Tools (CPM-PERT-Gantt)]]), **limiting work in progress** (Kanban; see [[041 Agile Project Management]]), **critical-chain** buffers, dedicated vs pooled teams, skill matrices, and **multi-tasking cost** (switching between projects reduces effective output).

### Example
A shared IT pool has 20 people, 85% productive, so capacity = $20 \times 0.85 = 17$ FTE. Demand from four projects:

| Quarter | WMS | Portal | ESG | Cold-chain | Total demand | Utilisation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 8 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 18 | 105.9% |
| Q2 | 10 | 6 | 4 | 6 | 26 | 152.9% |
| Q3 | 9 | 3 | 2 | 8 | 22 | 129.4% |

Q2 is overloaded by 9 FTE. Options: start Cold-chain one quarter later (Q2 demand falls by 6, to 20 FTE or 117.6%), then hire 3 contractors for the WMS peak (Q2 pool demand falls to 17 FTE, exactly 100%, still too tight so keep a small buffer by trimming Portal scope). The PMO shows the committee each choice with its delay or cost, instead of letting all four projects slip a little.

### In the news
See news box. Highways account for 993 of 1,775 projects: the same contractors, equipment and approvals pool serves many packages, so portfolio-level capacity planning is likely a precondition for meeting dates.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Five projects need the same 10 engineers. How do you resolve it?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Quantify demand vs capacity by role and period (heatmap) with realistic productivity
> - Prioritise by value and deadline, not by who asks loudest; re-sequence
> - Options: stagger, add capacity, reduce scope, outsource; show trade-offs
> - Limit multi-tasking and WIP; keep buffers
> - Governance: portfolio board decides conflicts, not individual PMs

## 10. Dependency Management
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Types of dependency; dependency register; critical dependencies; integration milestones

### Definition
A **dependency** is a relationship where one piece of work, deliverable, resource or decision relies on another. Types: **logical/technical** (hard: finish-to-start), **resource** (same team), **external** (vendor, regulator, customer), **discretionary** (preferred sequence), **benefit** (benefit needs another project's output) and **data/technology** (shared platform, API). PMBOK precedence relations: FS, SS, FF, SF with lead and lag ([[039 Scheduling Tools (CPM-PERT-Gantt)]]).

Management steps: identify (workshops, roadmap mapping, dependency matrix or N² chart), record in a **dependency register** (provider, receiver, deliverable, date needed, owner, status, RAG), assign **owners on both sides**, negotiate and agree dates (service-level agreements between teams), monitor on a **programme integrated schedule** with **integration milestones**, and escalate breaches through the programme board. Visual tools: dependency network, **DSM** (dependency structure matrix), PI-planning boards in SAFe ("program board" with strings between teams).

Risks: **cascading delay** (a slip propagates), **circular dependencies**, **hidden dependencies** discovered late, and **critical-path concentration** (a few shared deliverables control many projects). Mitigation: decouple (APIs, feature toggles), buffers at integration points, early integration, shared milestones.

### Example
Programme with four components: WMS upgrade needs the master-data clean-up (finish-to-start), the mobile app needs the OMS API at month 5, the vendor integration needs the regulator's e-way bill sandbox, and the benefit "same-day delivery" needs all three. The dependency register shows 14 dependencies; 3 are tagged critical. When the master-data clean-up slips 4 weeks, the register shows the WMS go-live, the app and the benefit date all move, so the programme manager asks the master-data owner for a recovery plan and moves the app's integration test earlier using stub data, containing the slip to 2 weeks.

### In the news
See news box. Large infrastructure packages depend on land acquisition, utility shifting and approvals owned outside the project; these external dependencies are the classic route from schedule slip to the cost overruns in MoSPI's figures.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How do you manage dependencies between teams in a programme?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Types of dependencies and a register with owners on both sides
> - Integrated schedule, integration milestones, early warnings
> - Decoupling and buffers; escalation path to the programme board
> - Example of a dependency slipping and how impact was contained

## 11. Transformation Office and Enterprise Change
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Large change programmes; value targets; initiative owners; weekly cadence; cash vs run-rate

### Definition
A **transformation office** (TO; also **transformation management office**, TMO) is a temporary, CEO-sponsored PMO-like unit that drives an enterprise-wide improvement or restructuring: cost reduction, turnaround, digital transformation, post-merger integration. It is a favourite structure of consulting engagements (see [[160 Case Interview - Cost Reduction, Turnaround & Pricing]] and [[159 Case Interview - M&A & Due Diligence]]).

Differences from a classic PMO: **value-first** (tracks run-rate and cash savings against targets, not only milestones); **initiative-based** (owners from the business, each with a target); **cadenced** (weekly initiative reviews, monthly steering with the CEO and CFO); **finance-validated** (CFO signs off benefits); **change management** built in (communication, incentives).

Core mechanics: **initiative funnel** (idea, validated, planned, in execution, realised: "stage gates for value"), **value tracker** with baselines, **initiative charters**, **risk-adjusted pipeline**, **governance** (steering committee, sponsor) and **transition to business-as-usual** with benefits embedded in budgets.

Metrics: gross vs net savings, run-rate vs in-year impact, pipeline coverage (pipeline value ÷ target, commonly 1.2 to 1.5 times to allow for attrition), % of initiatives behind plan.

### Example
A manufacturer targets ₹120 crore of run-rate savings in 18 months. The funnel at week 12 holds ₹210 crore of ideas, ₹150 crore validated, ₹95 crore in execution, ₹30 crore realised. Pipeline coverage of validated ideas = 150 ÷ 120 = 1.25 times; the TO flags that 40% of execution-stage value depends on one procurement category and the CFO has validated only ₹22 crore of the ₹30 crore claimed, so the reported number is ₹22 crore. Weekly reviews focus on the five largest at-risk initiatives.

### In the news
See news box. PMBOK 8's PMO-as-strategic-enabler appendix mirrors the move from compliance reporting to value ownership that transformation offices embody.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How would you set up a transformation office for a 3-year cost programme?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Sponsor at CEO level; initiative owners in the business; CFO validation
> - Funnel with stage definitions; run-rate and cash tracking; pipeline coverage
> - Cadence: weekly initiative review, monthly steering
> - Change management and sustaining the savings after the TO closes
> - Avoiding double counting and one-off gains presented as run-rate

## 12. Portfolio KPIs and Dashboards
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Strategic, delivery, financial, benefit, health indicators; RAG; drill-down

### Definition
A **portfolio dashboard** gives the investment committee a one-page view of whether the portfolio is delivering. Layers:
- **Strategic alignment:** % of spend by strategic theme, run/grow/transform mix.
- **Delivery:** schedule performance index $SPI = EV/PV$, cost performance index $CPI = EV/AC$ (see [[042 Cost & Budget Management]]), milestone hit rate, projects RAG.
- **Financial:** budget vs forecast vs actual, EAC, contingency used, capex vs opex.
- **Benefits:** realised vs planned, benefit-at-risk.
- **Risk:** top portfolio risks, aggregate exposure.
- **Resource:** utilisation by role, bench, vendor dependence.
- **Quality/health:** defects, stakeholder satisfaction, change requests, data freshness.

Design rules (see [[047 MIS & Dashboard Design]] and [[044 Power BI & DAX]]): one page, few KPIs, thresholds defined in advance (for example CPI below 0.9 = red), trend not only status, drill-down to the project, consistent definitions, single source of data, "watermelon" check (green outside, red inside) by requiring evidence for RAG. Report cadence: weekly project, monthly programme, quarterly portfolio.

### Example
Portfolio of 5 funded projects (₹60 crore). At month 6 the PMO reports total PV ₹30 crore, EV ₹25 crore, AC ₹30 crore: $SPI = 25/30 = 0.83$, $CPI = 25/30 = 0.83$; both red. EAC at current CPI (BAC ₹60 crore) = $60 / 0.83 = ₹72$ crore, a forecast overrun of ₹12 crore, which the committee must either fund or offset by deferring a project. The dashboard drill-down shows that WMS is the one with CPI 0.70, so the intervention is targeted instead of portfolio-wide.

### In the news
See news box. MoSPI's report is itself a dashboard: it tracks 1,775 projects by ministry, sector, cost overrun and percent completion, showing the standard metric set at national scale.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What would you put on a one-page dashboard for the CEO's portfolio review?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Five to ten KPIs across strategy, delivery, finance, benefits, risk and resources
> - Thresholds and RAG definitions agreed up-front; trends and drill-down
> - Evidence for status, not self-reported colours
> - Tool choice and data source discipline
> - Decisions the dashboard should trigger (stop, fund, re-sequence)

## 13. ⭐ Advanced: Portfolio Balancing, Risk Aggregation and Lean-Agile Portfolios
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
**Balancing** ensures the portfolio is not skewed: short vs long term, high vs low risk, incremental vs transformational, internal vs external benefit. Techniques: **bubble charts**, **risk-return map**, **portfolio-level Monte Carlo** (correlated risks across projects, see [[150 Decision Analysis & Simulation]]), **diversification** across themes, and **stress tests** (budget cut of 20%, key supplier failure).

**Risk aggregation:** portfolio risk is not the sum of project risks; **common-cause** exposures (single vendor, same technology, shared team, regulatory change) create correlation. Maintain a portfolio risk register with cross-cutting risks (see [[040 Risk & Stakeholder Management]]).

**Lean-agile portfolio management (SAFe LPM, Lean portfolio management):** fund **value streams** rather than projects, with **epics** governed by lightweight business cases, **WIP limits** at portfolio level, **participatory budgeting**, quarterly planning and **WSJF** (cost of delay ÷ job size) for sequencing ([[034 Prioritization Frameworks]]). Forecast through **throughput** and **cost of delay**, not only EVM.

**Beyond Rs and time:** include **sustainability and ESG criteria** (for example BRSR in India), **technical debt** and **strategic optionality** in the scoring.

Governance questions an advanced answer covers: who can stop a project, how often is the portfolio re-prioritised (quarterly), how are sunk costs handled (ignore them), how are small "pet" projects controlled.

### Example
A portfolio has five projects with benefits correlated through a single cloud vendor outage risk (probability 10%, impact ₹8 crore per affected project, three projects dependent). Treated independently, a PM might reserve ₹0.8 crore per project (10% × ₹8 crore) = ₹2.4 crore, but the outage hits all three at once with a 10% chance, so the real loss in that scenario is ₹24 crore. The portfolio manager moves one project to a different vendor (single-point reduction), cutting the common-cause scenario to ₹16 crore and spreading exposure. Expected loss falls from ₹2.4 crore to ₹1.6 crore, the worst case from ₹24 crore to ₹16 crore, and the board can set contingency at portfolio level.

### In the news
See news box. The CrowdStrike outage of July 2024, discussed in [[040 Risk & Stakeholder Management]], is the textbook common-cause portfolio risk: one vendor update affected many unrelated programmes at once.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "How is portfolio risk different from project risk?" or "How would you run portfolio management in an agile company?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Correlation and common-cause risks; stress tests; portfolio-level contingency
> - Balance across horizon, risk, type; efficient-frontier idea
> - Lean-agile alternative: fund value streams, WIP limits, WSJF, quarterly rebalancing
> - Ignores sunk costs when deciding to stop
> - Cross-references to benefits tracking and capacity
