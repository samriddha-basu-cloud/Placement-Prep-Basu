## 1. Demand, Supply and Market Equilibrium
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Movement along vs shift of curves; equilibrium; price controls create shortages or surpluses

### Definition
- **Law of demand:** price up → quantity demanded down (movement along the curve). **Shifters of demand:** income, prices of substitutes/complements, tastes, expectations, population, credit availability.
- **Law of supply:** price up → quantity supplied up. **Shifters of supply:** input costs, technology, number of firms, taxes/subsidies, weather (agriculture), expectations.
- **Equilibrium:** where demand equals supply; shortages push prices up, surpluses push them down.
- **Price controls:** ceilings below equilibrium (e.g., price caps on essential drugs under DPCO) cause shortages unless supply is managed; floors above equilibrium (minimum support prices) cause surpluses that the government must buy and store ([[133 Food, Agri & Perishables Supply Chain - India]]).

### Example
Tomato prices spike in July almost every year: monsoon rains damage crops and disrupt transport (supply shifts left) while demand is stable, so prices rise sharply. Because demand for tomatoes is inelastic in the short run, a modest supply cut produces a large price rise. Operations implications: cold-chain and processing capacity (purée) smooth supply across seasons.

### In the news
See news box. Food inflation of 7.74% in September 2026 is a supply-side story feeding into the RBI's decision.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why did onion/tomato/edible-oil prices rise?" or "What happens if the government caps price X?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Identify whether demand or supply shifted, and why
> - Elasticity explains the size of the price move
> - Policy and operational responses (buffer stocks, imports, storage)

## 2. Elasticities: Price, Income and Cross
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ %ΔQ / %ΔX; elastic vs inelastic; revenue test; income elasticity for luxuries and inferior goods; cross elasticity for substitutes/complements

### Definition
- **Price elasticity** $E_p = \%\Delta Q / \%\Delta P$. |E| > 1 elastic: price rise reduces revenue; |E| < 1 inelastic: price rise raises revenue. Determinants: substitutes, necessity vs luxury, share of budget, time horizon.
- **Income elasticity** $E_y = \%\Delta Q / \%\Delta \text{Income}$: > 1 luxury (cars, air travel), 0-1 necessity, < 0 inferior good (coarse grains in some contexts as incomes rise).
- **Cross elasticity** $E_{xy} = \%\Delta Q_x / \%\Delta P_y$: > 0 substitutes (tea and coffee), < 0 complements (cars and fuel).

### Example
1. Cement price rises 4%, volume falls 2%: E = −0.5 (inelastic); revenue ≈ 1.04 × 0.98 − 1 = **+1.9%**.
2. Household incomes rise 8% and SUV demand rises 14%: income elasticity = **1.75** (luxury-like), consistent with utility vehicles growing faster (11% in FY26) than cars (1.9%) ([[237 Recruiter Sector Primers - FMCG, Auto, E-commerce, Manufacturing, IT-GCC & Consulting]]).
3. CNG price rises 10% and demand for CNG cars falls 3%: cross elasticity between CNG price and CNG cars = **−0.3** (complements).

### In the news
See news box. Elasticity also decides AI economics: if token prices halve and usage more than doubles, total spend rises ([[231 AI Industry Landscape, Economics & India AI Ecosystem]], sub-topic 4).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> Pricing cases and "Should we raise prices?" ([[235 Marketing Essentials for Operations & Consulting Interviews]], sub-topic 6).

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - The formula and the revenue test
> - Determinants applied to the product
> - Profit (contribution) impact, not just revenue

## 3. Costs, Marginal Thinking and Economies of Scale
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Fixed vs variable; marginal cost; sunk costs ignored; MR = MC; scale and scope economies; diseconomies

### Definition
- **Fixed costs** (rent, salaried staff, depreciation) vs **variable costs** (materials, power per unit, freight).
- **Marginal cost (MC):** cost of one more unit; **marginal revenue (MR):** revenue from one more unit. Profit is maximised where MR = MC.
- **Sunk costs** are irrecoverable and should not affect forward decisions; **opportunity cost** should.
- **Economies of scale:** average cost falls with volume (spreading fixed cost, purchasing power, specialisation, learning curve [[152 Learning Curves, Work Measurement & Productivity]]). **Economies of scope:** cost savings from producing related products together (shared distribution). **Diseconomies:** coordination costs, congestion, bureaucracy.
- **Break-even volume** = fixed cost / (price − variable cost per unit) ([[110 Cost Accounting for Operations]]).

### Example
A plant's fixed cost is ₹12 crore a year; price ₹500 per unit; variable cost ₹300. Break-even = 12,00,00,000 / 200 = **6 lakh units**. An export order for 50,000 extra units at ₹380 (below full cost but above variable cost) adds 50,000 × ₹80 = **₹40 lakh** of contribution if there is spare capacity and no cannibalisation of domestic prices. The ₹5 crore already spent on a failed product line is sunk and irrelevant to this decision.

### In the news
See news box. Hyperscaler AI capex is a bet on economies of scale in compute ([[231 AI Industry Landscape, Economics & India AI Ecosystem]]).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Should we accept this order below our average cost?" (classic make/accept decision)

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Contribution logic (price vs variable cost)
> - Capacity, cannibalisation and strategic effects
> - Ignoring sunk costs explicitly

## 4. Market Structures and Pricing Power
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, monopoly; barriers to entry; Indian examples; CCI

### Definition
| Structure | Firms | Product | Pricing power | Indian examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | very many | identical | none (price takers) | mandi trading of a single grain variety (approx.) |
| Monopolistic competition | many | differentiated | some (brands) | restaurants, apparel, FMCG brands |
| Oligopoly | few large | similar or differentiated | interdependent | telecom, cement, airlines, steel, quick commerce |
| Monopoly | one | unique | high (regulated) | Indian Railways (passenger rail), state power distribution in many areas |

**Barriers to entry:** scale economies, capital needs, network effects, regulation/licences, brand, control of inputs or distribution. **Competition law:** the Competition Commission of India (CCI) acts against cartels (cement and tyre cartel cases are well-known) and abuse of dominance; deal-value thresholds for merger review were introduced in 2024 ([[159 Case Interview - M&A & Due Diligence]]).

### Example
Cement is a regional oligopoly: high freight cost per tonne limits how far cement travels (typically a few hundred km by road), so a few plants dominate each region; prices move together and capacity additions are watched closely. Consolidation (large players acquiring smaller ones) increases regional concentration, which CCI reviews.

### In the news
See news box. Quick commerce shows oligopoly dynamics: three players with nearly four-fifths of daily orders (Aug 2026) competing on speed and discounts ([[237 Recruiter Sector Primers - FMCG, Auto, E-commerce, Manufacturing, IT-GCC & Consulting]]).

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Why do airlines/cement/telecom have so few players?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Classify the structure and name the barriers
> - Pricing behaviour (interdependence, price wars, tacit coordination)
> - Regulation's role

## 5. Game Theory for Business Decisions
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Prisoner's dilemma; Nash equilibrium; repeated games; first-mover advantage; signalling

### Definition
- **Nash equilibrium:** no player can gain by changing strategy alone, given the other's strategy.
- **Prisoner's dilemma:** individually rational choices (cut price) lead to a worse joint outcome; explains price wars and over-advertising.
- **Repeated games:** cooperation can be sustained by the threat of retaliation (tit-for-tat).
- **Sequential games:** first-mover advantage (capacity pre-emption), commitment and credible threats.
- **Signalling:** capacity announcements, price-matching guarantees.
Supply-chain contracts and game theory are covered in [[137 Supply Chain Contracts & Game Theory]].

### Example
Two quick-commerce firms each choose "high discount" or "normal". Profits (₹ crore/quarter): both normal 50/50; both high 20/20; one high, other normal 70/10. Each firm's best response is "high" whatever the other does (70 > 50; 20 > 10), so both discount and earn 20: a prisoner's dilemma. Escape routes: differentiation (assortment, service), repeated-game discipline, or investor pressure for profitability.

### In the news
See news box.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Our competitor cut prices 15%. Should we match?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Payoff logic and likely competitor reactions
> - Long-run (repeated game) view
> - Non-price responses (service, bundling, segment focus)

## 6. GDP, GVA and Measuring Growth
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ GDP = C + I + G + (X − M); nominal vs real; deflator; GVA by sector; base year; per capita

### Definition
- **GDP (expenditure):** consumption (C, about 55-60% in India) + investment (I, gross fixed capital formation about 30%+) + government consumption (G) + net exports (X − M).
- **GVA (production side):** output minus intermediate consumption, by sector (agriculture, industry, services); GDP = GVA + product taxes − subsidies.
- **Nominal vs real:** real removes inflation; **GDP deflator** ≈ (1 + nominal growth)/(1 + real growth) − 1.
- **Base year:** India moved to a new 2022-23 base series in 2026 ([[180 Current Affairs & Economy Briefing for MBA Interviews (2025-26)]]).
- **Per capita GDP**, PPP comparisons, and limits of GDP (informal sector, distribution, environment).

### Example
Q1 FY27: nominal growth 10.3%, real 7.8%. Implied deflator = 1.103/1.078 − 1 ≈ **2.3%**. Low deflator growth while CPI is near 5% means producer prices (WPI-like) and the GDP basket rose less than consumer prices, which flatters real growth; nominal growth is what drives tax revenue and corporate sales ([[108 Financial Statements & Ratios]]).

### In the news
See news box.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "India is growing at 7.8%. Why aren't FMCG volumes growing?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Real vs nominal and the deflator
> - Composition: investment-led and services-led growth vs rural consumption
> - Distribution (urban vs rural, formal vs informal)

## 7. Inflation: CPI, WPI, Core and Causes
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ CPI targeted at 4% ± 2%; food and fuel volatility; demand-pull vs cost-push; real interest rate; who gains, who loses

### Definition
- **CPI:** consumer prices (food has a large weight); India's inflation target is 4% with a 2-6% band (flexible inflation targeting since 2016).
- **WPI:** wholesale/producer prices; more sensitive to commodities and fuel.
- **Core inflation:** excludes food and fuel; shows underlying demand pressure.
- **Causes:** demand-pull (too much demand), cost-push (oil, wages, rupee depreciation, supply shocks), built-in expectations.
- **Real interest rate** ≈ nominal rate − inflation.
- **Effects:** erodes purchasing power and real wages; hurts fixed-income savers; helps debtors; raises input costs and working capital needs; complicates pricing.

### Example
After the October hike, repo 5.50% − September CPI 5.54% ≈ **−0.04 percentage points**: the real policy rate is roughly zero, which is why markets debated further hikes. For a manufacturer, 7.74% food inflation means wage pressure in labour-intensive plants and weaker rural demand for discretionary products.

### In the news
See news box. CPI rose from about 4.8% in August to 5.54% in September 2026; the RBI projects 6.0% for Q3 FY27.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "Inflation is above 5%. How does that affect our business?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Type of inflation (food/fuel-led cost-push vs demand-pull)
> - Effects on input costs, demand, pricing and working capital
> - Actions: hedging, contract indexation, pack-price changes ([[123 Commodity Price Risk, Hedging & Contract Pricing Mechanisms]])

## 8. Monetary Policy and the RBI Toolkit
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Repo, SDF, MSF corridor; CRR, SLR; OMOs; MPC; transmission to loans, demand, rupee

### Definition
- **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC):** six members (three RBI, three external) set the **repo rate** to meet the inflation target.
- **Liquidity adjustment facility (LAF) corridor:** **SDF** (standing deposit facility, floor) = repo − 25 bps; **MSF** (marginal standing facility, ceiling) = repo + 25 bps; Bank Rate = MSF.
- **Other tools:** CRR (cash reserve ratio, held with RBI), SLR (statutory liquidity ratio, held in government securities), open market operations (OMOs), variable-rate repos/reverse repos, forex intervention.
- **Stances:** accommodative, neutral, tightening ("calibrated tightening" in Oct 2026).
- **Transmission:** policy rate → bank funding costs → lending rates (EBLR-linked loans move fast; MCLR-linked slower) → borrowing, investment and consumption → inflation; also via the rupee (higher rates support the currency).

### Example
EMI impact of the 25 bps hike on a ₹50 lakh, 20-year home loan linked to the repo: at 8.50% the EMI is **₹43,391**; at 8.75% it is **₹44,186**, **+₹794 a month**. For a company with ₹2,000 crore of floating-rate debt, +25 bps = ₹5 crore a year of extra interest.

### In the news
See news box. Corridor after the hike: SDF 5.25%, repo 5.50%, MSF 5.75%.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The RBI just hiked rates. Why, and what does it mean for us?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Reason: inflation above target and rising, rupee pressure
> - Transmission channels with a number
> - Sector effects: autos, real estate, NBFCs, capex plans, working capital

## 9. Fiscal Policy and the Union Budget
> 🟠 Tier 2 · _Key points:_ Fiscal deficit; capex vs revenue spending; multiplier; crowding out; FRBM path; tax structure

### Definition
- **Fiscal deficit** = total expenditure − (revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts); **revenue deficit** and **primary deficit** (fiscal deficit − interest payments).
- **Capital expenditure** (roads, railways, defence assets) has a higher multiplier than most revenue spending; India's public capex push since 2020 supported growth ([[145 India Manufacturing & Supply Chain Policy - PLI, Gati Shakti & NLP]]).
- **Multiplier (simple):** 1/(1 − MPC); smaller in practice because of taxes, imports and crowding out.
- **Crowding out:** government borrowing raises interest rates and reduces private investment.
- **Taxes:** direct (income tax, corporate tax) and indirect (GST, customs; [[227 GST & Indirect Tax for Supply Chains]]).

### Example
Budget figures to carry: fiscal deficit 4.4% of GDP in FY26, FY27 target 4.3%, public capex ₹12.2 lakh crore budgeted for FY27 ([[180 Current Affairs & Economy Briefing for MBA Interviews (2025-26)]], sub-topic 4). With a simple MPC of 0.75 the textbook multiplier is 4; empirical estimates for Indian capital spending are much lower but above 1, which is why capex gets priority over subsidies.

### In the news
See news box. Higher oil prices raise subsidy and fuel-tax trade-offs that pressure the fiscal path.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "What was the most important thing in the Budget for our sector?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - The deficit numbers and the capex figure with dates
> - Sector-relevant measures (infrastructure, PLI, customs duties, GST)
> - Trade-off: growth support vs fiscal discipline

## 10. External Sector: Balance of Payments, the Rupee and Tariffs
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Current account vs capital account; CAD drivers (oil, gold); rupee depreciation winners and losers; tariffs and comparative advantage

### Definition
- **Balance of payments:** current account (trade in goods and services, remittances, income) + capital/financial account (FDI, FPI, loans) + change in reserves = 0.
- India typically runs a **goods trade deficit** (oil, gold, electronics) partly offset by **services exports** (IT, GCCs) and remittances; the **current account deficit (CAD)** widens when oil prices rise.
- **Rupee depreciation:** helps exporters (IT services, pharma, textiles) and import-substituting producers; hurts importers (oil marketing companies, electronics assemblers, airlines with dollar costs) and firms with unhedged dollar debt; raises inflation through imported prices.
- **Tariffs:** taxes on imports protect domestic producers but raise input costs and invite retaliation. **Comparative advantage** explains gains from trade; FTAs reduce tariffs between partners ([[126 International Trade Documentation, Customs & Trade Finance]]).

### Example
A paint company imports \$10 million of crude-linked raw materials a quarter. From ₹88 to ₹96 per dollar, the rupee cost rises from ₹88 crore to ₹96 crore, **+₹8 crore (+9.1%)** per quarter, before any rise in the dollar price of crude. Responses: price increases, hedging, alternate formulations, renegotiated contracts. An IT exporter with \$100 million quarterly revenue gains ₹80 crore of rupee revenue from the same move, partly offset by hedges.

### In the news
See news box. Rupee near ₹96 and Brent near \$107 at end-September 2026; India imports about 88% of its petroleum needs.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> "The rupee hit a record low. Who wins and who loses?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Winners and losers by import/export exposure
> - Numbers for one company
> - Mitigation: hedging, natural hedges, pricing, sourcing shifts

## 11. The "Impact of X on Y" Chain Method
> 🔴 Tier 1 · _Key points:_ Shock → transmission channels → company P&L and balance sheet → actions; first- and second-order effects; time horizon

### Definition
A four-step method for any macro-to-business question:
1. **Shock:** state it with a number and date.
2. **Channels:** demand (customers' income, prices, credit), costs (inputs, energy, wages, freight, currency), finance (interest, working capital, valuation), competition (imports, exports, substitutes), regulation.
3. **P&L/balance-sheet effect:** which lines move and roughly by how much; first-order vs second-order effects; short vs long run.
4. **Actions:** what management should do (pricing, sourcing, hedging, capacity, inventory, communication).

### Example
"Impact of the October 2026 rate hike on a two-wheeler maker":
1. Repo +25 bps to 5.50%.
2. Demand: about two-thirds of two-wheelers are bought on credit (assumption to verify); EMIs rise slightly; rural buyers already squeezed by food inflation. Costs: little direct effect. Finance: dealer inventory funding costs rise.
3. Effect: mild demand slowdown in entry segments; dealer inventory destocking; premium segments less affected.
4. Actions: tie-ups for subsidised financing schemes, tighter dealer inventory norms, product mix towards premium and EV with incentives, keep production flexible ([[153 Aggregate Planning Models & Workforce Strategy]]).

### In the news
See news box.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> Every macro question in placement interviews; also GDs ([[177 Group Discussions & Extempore for MBA Placements]]).

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Channels named explicitly
> - Direction and rough size of effect
> - Actions, not only analysis

## 12. ⭐ Advanced: Macro Scenarios and an Operations Playbook
> ⭐ Advanced · _Added beyond the tracker_

### Definition
Operations leaders translate macro scenarios into playbooks:
| Scenario | Leading indicators | Operations playbook |
|---|---|---|
| Oil shock (Brent > \$100) | crude, freight indices, diesel prices | fuel surcharges in freight contracts, load consolidation, mode shift to rail, packaging and resin hedges ([[125 Transportation Management Deep Dive]]) |
| Rupee depreciation | USD/INR, forex reserves | localise imports, dual sourcing, hedge, revisit landed-cost models |
| Rate hikes / credit tightening | repo, bank lending rates | reduce inventory days, renegotiate payment terms, supply chain finance ([[136 Supply Chain Finance & Working Capital]]) |
| Rural demand slowdown | rural FMCG volumes, wage growth, monsoon | smaller packs, route-to-market cost cuts, shift mix to urban/online channels |
| Tariff shock on exports | trade-policy news, order books | market diversification, FTA utilisation, cost-down programmes |

### Example
For a consumer-durables maker in late 2026: Brent near \$107 (plastics and freight costs up), rupee near ₹96 (imported compressors and electronics up), rates rising (dealer financing costlier), and rural demand weak. A scenario plan: raise prices 3-4% in two steps, accelerate localisation of compressors, cut finished-goods inventory by 7 days to free cash, and shift promotional spend to e-commerce where volume is growing. Track monthly against triggers (Brent > \$115, rupee > ₹100) that would activate the next step.

### In the news
See news box.

### Interview angle
> [!question] How it is asked
> Operations and consulting: "Given today's macro environment, what should our supply chain do?"

> [!tip] Strong answer includes
> - Current facts with dates
> - A scenario table with triggers
> - Concrete operational responses
